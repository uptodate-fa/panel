IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

The urgency of treatment of hyperkalemia varies with the presence or absence of the symptoms and signs associated with hyperkalemia, the severity of the potassium elevation, and the cause of hyperkalemia. The potassium level must be interpreted in conjunction with the underlying medical disorder(s). (See "Clinical manifestations of hyperkalemia in adults" .)

● Hyperkalemic emergency – Identify patients with any of the following clinical features of a hyperkalemic emergency:

• Muscle weakness or paralysis.

• Severe hyperkalemia (ie, >6.5 mEq/L) in the absence of gross hemolysis.

• Rapidly increasing serum potassium >5.5 mEq/L due to increased tissue catabolism (eg, trauma, rhabdomyolysis), oliguric renal failure, or multiple factors (eg, metabolic acidosis, drugs, hemolysis or gastrointestinal bleeding, insulin deficiency, severe illness).

• Cardiac conduction abnormalities or arrhythmias.

Review available tests (eg, basic chemistries, complete blood count [CBC] with differential, platelet count, and reticulocyte count). Obtain an electrocardiogram (ECG), if not already performed, to assess for conduction abnormalities (eg, tall peaked T waves, PR prolongation, loss of P waves, QRS widening, ventricular fibrillation, and asystole).

Patients with hyperkalemic emergencies require immediate treatment with rapidly acting therapies (eg, calcium, insulin, and glucose) ( table 1 ). Treatment should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing or specialty consultation. Repeat electrolytes one to two hours after initiation of therapies. (See "Treatment and prevention of hyperkalemia in adults", section on 'Patients with a hyperkalemic emergency' .)

● Patients needing prompt therapy – Identify patients with any of the following worrisome clinical features requiring prompt reduction of potassium within 6 to 12 hours (see "Treatment and prevention of hyperkalemia in adults", section on 'Patients who need prompt serum potassium reduction' ):

• End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) requiring hemodialysis

• Marginal renal function and/or marginal urine output

• Optimization of metabolic parameters required prior to surgery

Review available tests (eg, basic chemistries, CBC with differential, platelet count, and reticulocyte count). Obtain ECG if not already performed. Obtain nephrology consultation for consideration of dialysis in patients with ESKD or marginal renal function and/or oliguria. Monitor serial electrolytes.

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The history, medication review, and physical examination, as well as routine laboratory tests, may indicate an underlying disease or medication that is causing or contributing to hyperkalemia. If unexplained, patients should undergo a timely evaluation to determine the etiology and trajectory of hyperkalemia. (See "Causes and evaluation of hyperkalemia in adults" .)

If prior potassium levels are unavailable, promptly repeat the potassium measurement to confirm that there is a true increase in level. Because the serum potassium can be falsely elevated due to local release from muscles contracting while ischemic, avoid fist-clenching when repeating the potassium measurement.

● Redraw serum potassium if hemolysis is present or if the specimen is hemolyzed.

● Measure plasma potassium if thrombocytosis is present (elevation of serum potassium approximately 0.15 mEq/L per 100,000/microL elevation in platelet count).

● Obtain laboratory instructions for special processing if severe leukocytosis (eg, white blood cell count >120,000/microL) is present.

Additional evaluation depends on whether the high potassium is confirmed:

● Persistent hyperkalemia – Most patients with hyperkalemia have chronic mild (≤5.5 mEq/L) or moderate (5.5 to 6.5 mEq/L) elevations in serum potassium due to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and/or the use of medications that inhibit the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system ( table 2 ). Such patients do not require urgent lowering of the serum potassium. Additional testing depends on the suspected underlying etiology or contributing factors such as metabolic acidosis. (See "Causes and evaluation of hyperkalemia in adults", section on 'Evaluation' .)

As examples:

• Hypoaldosteronism – Plasma renin activity, serum aldosterone, 8 AM serum cortisol if suspected endocrinopathy

• Hemolytic anemia – Haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin, lactic dehydrogenase (LDH), peripheral blood smear

• Rhabdomyolysis – Serum creatine phosphokinase (CPK) and urine myoglobin

● Transient or pseudohyperkalemia – Pseudohyperkalemia is usually due to potassium movement out of the cells during or after the blood specimen has been drawn. Patients with pseudohyperkalemia have no apparent cause for hyperkalemia, no symptoms or signs of hyperkalemia, and no ECG abnormalities of hyperkalemia. No further evaluation of the potassium level is required. (See "Causes and evaluation of hyperkalemia in adults", section on 'Pseudohyperkalemia' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for potassium is approximately 3.5 to 5.3 mEq/L (mmol/L) but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
