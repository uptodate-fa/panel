ALGORITHM — 

(algorithm 1)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), as well as alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin, are biochemical markers of liver injury. ALT is a more specific marker of hepatic injury than AST.

The approach to elevated aminotransferases depends upon the clinical presentation, which may range from life-threatening (eg, suspicion for acute liver failure) to an incidental finding in an asymptomatic individual. The differential diagnosis is broad and includes viral hepatitis, hepatotoxicity from drugs or toxins, alcoholic liver disease, ischemic hepatitis, and malignant infiltration.

Review:

●Tests that may be available at the time of diagnosis (eg, liver biochemical tests in addition to ALT and AST, including total and direct bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase [GGT], albumin; serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen [BUN], creatinine, glucose, and other chemistries; complete blood count [CBC] with differential and platelet count; international normalized ratio [INR]).

●History, including medications, herbal therapies, alcohol or recreational drug use, toxin exposure. In the United States, acetaminophen is the most common drug implicated in drug-induced liver injury (DILI), followed by antibiotics. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) maintains a searchable database of drugs, herbal medications, and dietary supplements associated with DILI.

Suspected acute liver failure — Acute liver failure is characterized by acute hepatocellular injury with liver tests typically more than 10 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), hepatic encephalopathy, and a prolonged prothrombin time (INR ≥1.5).

An extensive laboratory evaluation is required in patients with acute liver failure to determine the cause, assess the severity, and prepare for possible liver transplantation. Obtain urgent consultation with a clinician who has expertise in acute liver failure. (See "Acute liver failure in adults: Etiology, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis" and "Acute liver failure in adults: Management and prognosis".)

Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

Marked elevation without liver failure — Patients with marked elevation in AST and/or ALT (eg, approximately ≥15 times ULN) without evidence of acute liver failure often have acute hepatitis, although in some cases, there may be underlying chronic liver disease. Massive elevations in ALT, AST (>5,000 international units/L) are usually due to ischemic or drug-induced hepatitis. Other causes of massive elevations in AST include rhabdomyolysis and heat stroke. (See "Approach to the patient with abnormal liver tests", section on 'Marked elevation without liver failure'.)

The initial evaluation should be broad, even in patients with a presumed cause for acute hepatitis, as patients with marked elevation of aminotransferases may decompensate quickly.

Obtain:

●Acetaminophen (paracetamol) level, toxicology screen (eg, screen for cocaine, methamphetamine, heroin)

●Acute viral hepatitis serologies, including:

•Immunoglobulin M anti-hepatitis A

•Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), IgM anti-hepatitis B core (anti-HBc), antibody to HBsAg

•Anti-hepatitis C virus antibodies, hepatitis C RNA

●Serum pregnancy test if not already known to be pregnant

●Markers for autoimmune disease – Antinuclear antibodies, antismooth muscle antibodies, anti-liver/kidney microsomal antibody type 1, immunoglobulin levels

●Right upper quadrant ultrasound with Doppler studies of the portal and hepatic veins, hepatic artery

Findings from the patient's history, physical examination, and initial laboratory tests guide the subsequent evaluation and specialty consultation.

Mild to moderate elevation without liver failure — Patients with mild to moderate elevation in ALT and/or AST (approximately 5 to 15 times ULN) without evidence of acute liver failure require a timely evaluation to determine the cause of aminotransferase elevation (table 1). Common causes of mild to moderate ALT or AST elevation include metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, alcohol-associated liver disease, and chronic hepatitis C. (See "Approach to the patient with abnormal liver tests", section on 'Mild to moderate elevation'.)

The pattern of liver test abnormalities may indicate the underlying cause. A disproportionate increase in ALT and/or AST relative to alkaline phosphatase suggests hepatocellular injury, whereas a disproportionate increase in alkaline phosphatase (≥4 times ULN relative to increase in ALT, AST levels) suggests a cholestatic process. With either pattern, serum bilirubin may be elevated and tests of synthetic function (eg, INR, albumin) may be abnormal.

●Predominant ALT, AST elevation – Screen for significant alcohol consumption.

Obtain:

•Serologic tests for chronic hepatitis B and C infection (see "Hepatitis B virus: Screening and diagnosis in adults", section on 'Chronic HBV infection' and "Screening and diagnosis of chronic hepatitis C virus infection", section on 'Additional evaluation')

•Serum iron, total iron binding capacity, ferritin (see "Approach to the patient with suspected iron overload", section on 'Sequence and interpretation of testing')

•Lipid profile

•Glycated hemoglobin (A1C)

•Right upper quadrant ultrasound

In a patient with a history of significant alcohol consumption, we generally do not obtain additional testing if the above tests are negative. If the initial evaluation fails to identify a likely source of the aminotransferase elevation, sequential testing is based upon the history and physical examination.

●Predominant increase in alkaline phosphatase – Obtain right upper quadrant ultrasound to distinguish extrahepatic biliary tract obstruction from other causes.

Very mild elevation — For asymptomatic patients with very mild elevation in ALT and/or AST (approximately <5 times ULN), we typically recheck the liver tests in three to six months and only pursue an evaluation if they remain elevated.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for ALT ranges from 29 to 33 international units/L for males and 19 to 25 international units/L for females; the normal reference range for AST ranges from 10 to 40 international units/L for males and 9 to 32 international units/L for females. ALT and AST levels may vary with the timing of the blood sample relative to meals, strenuous exercise, and from day to day. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
