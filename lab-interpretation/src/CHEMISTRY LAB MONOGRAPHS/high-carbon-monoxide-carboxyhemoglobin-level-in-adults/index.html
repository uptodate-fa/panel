INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Carboxyhemoglobin is a measurement of the amount of carbon monoxide (CO) bound to hemoglobin. CO poisoning should be suspected in patients with suggestive symptoms (eg, altered mental status, dizziness, chest pain, headache) and a history of exposure ( table 1 ), or when an entire household develops symptoms consistent with CO poisoning in the absence of a clear exposure. Carboxyhemoglobin levels correlate imprecisely with the degree of poisoning. In addition, individuals exposed to the same level of ambient CO may have very different carboxyhemoglobin levels or have dissimilar symptoms (even if carboxyhemoglobin levels are identical). Older adults and individuals with pre-existing heart disease or cerebrovascular disease are more vulnerable to CO toxicity.

Laboratory measurement of carboxyhemoglobin is the only blood test routinely available to diagnose CO poisoning. Arterial or venous blood may be used. In hemodynamically unstable patients, arterial carboxyhemoglobin is more accurate in quantifying acidosis. In hemodynamically stable patients, venous sampling may be used to measure carboxyhemoglobin. Standard pulse oximetry (SpO 2 ) cannot screen for CO exposure, as it does not differentiate carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin. In addition, UpToDate authors do not recommend the use of fingertip CO monitors due to concerns of accuracy. (See "Carbon monoxide poisoning" .)

In patients with a suggestive history, a carboxyhemoglobin level >3 percent in a nonsmoker (>10 percent in a smoker) confirms a diagnosis of CO poisoning. However, the diagnosis of CO poisoning should not be rejected in a smoker with a carboxyhemoglobin level between 3 and 10 percent and a suggestive history simply because of smoking status. (See "Carbon monoxide poisoning", section on 'Diagnosis' .)

Perform a clinical assessment to determine the duration and mechanism of CO poisoning ( table 2 ). For all patients, the source of CO exposure should be identified and addressed so that the patient and others do not return to the environment where CO poisoning likely occurred.

Severe symptoms  —  Severe symptoms of CO poisoning include neurologic (eg, seizures, syncope, coma), cardiovascular (eg, myocardial ischemia, ventricular arrhythmias, pulmonary edema), or metabolic (eg, acid base disturbance) manifestations. Before the carboxyhemoglobin results become available, patients with severe symptoms should have already been removed from the source of CO, administered 100 percent oxygen by a nonrebreather mask, and evaluated for hypoxic injury ( table 2 ). (See "Carbon monoxide poisoning", section on 'ABC's' .)

For patients with severe symptoms, obtain (review):

● Electrocardiogram (ECG)

● Arterial blood gas analysis

● Complete blood count (CBC)

● Serum chemistries

● Troponin (if there are cardiac risk factors, chest pain, ECG changes)

● Pregnancy test, as appropriate

Determine need for hyperbaric oxygen (HBO; obtain consultation with a medical toxicologist if needed). HBO therapy is indicated in the presence of any one of the following:

● Carboxyhemoglobin level >25 percent

● Carboxyhemoglobin level >15 percent in a pregnant patient

● Loss of consciousness

● Severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.25)

● Evidence of end-organ ischemia (eg, ECG changes, elevated cardiac biomarkers, focal neurologic deficit, altered mental status)

Monitor until symptom resolution. Further evaluation is based on clinical assessment, duration, and cause of CO exposure.

Mild or moderate symptoms  —  Patients with mild to moderate symptoms may have any range of elevation in carboxyhemoglobin but usually ≤20 percent. These symptoms include headache (the most common presenting symptom), malaise, chest pain, nausea, and dizziness. Levels can be misleading if a blood specimen is drawn several hours after removal from a CO-contaminated site because the levels decline after removal, and symptoms can remain long after CO levels fall. (See "Carbon monoxide poisoning" and "Carbon monoxide poisoning", section on 'Clinical findings' .)

Obtain:

● ECG

● Troponin (if there are cardiac risk factors, chest pain, ECG changes)

● Pregnancy test in females of childbearing age

Administer 100 percent oxygen by a nonrebreather mask and continue until the patient is asymptomatic, typically in less than six hours. Further evaluation is based on clinical assessment, duration, and cause of CO exposure.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference value for carboxyhemoglobin is approximately <3 percent in nonsmokers and <10 percent in smokers. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
