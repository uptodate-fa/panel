IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

The degree of hypernatremia and the rate of rise of the serum sodium determine acute management.

Rapidly identify patients with any of the following worrisome clinical features (see "Manifestations of hyponatremia and hypernatremia in adults" ):

● Severe hypernatremia (>150 mEq/L [mmol/L])

● Neurologic manifestations of hypernatremia even if mild (eg, lethargy, weakness, and irritability may progress to more severe symptoms)

● Development of hypernatremia within 48 hours or a rapidly rising serum sodium concentration, documented by serial laboratory tests

Stabilize patient, assess, and replace water losses. Additional (simultaneous) evaluation and specialty consultation is directed at identifying and managing contributing factors. (See 'Initial evaluation' below.)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The etiology of high sodium can usually be determined from elements of the history, key features of the physical examination, and the results of serum chemistries and urine specific gravity typically available at the time hypernatremia is identified ( table 1 ). A high serum sodium concentration reflects a hypertonic state due to the presence of a relative water deficit. It is usually due to unreplaced free water loss and, more rarely, to intake of concentrated salt. Hypernatremia occurs more commonly in hospitalized patients than in ambulatory patients. Obtain specialty consultation (if needed) to assist with evaluation and management. (See "Etiology and evaluation of hypernatremia in adults" .)

Review history and assess risk factors for hypernatremia, including:

● Impaired thirst mechanism or decreased water intake for another reason:

• Frailty (diminished sense of thirst or inability to seek water)

• Hypothalamic/pituitary lesion affecting thirst or osmoreceptor function

• Delirium (drugs/toxins, infections, metabolic derangements, critical illness) or mental illness

• Restrictions on oral intake (surgery/trauma, mechanical ventilation)

● Persistent, unreplaced fluid losses:

• Vomiting, gastric drainage, fistulas, osmotic diarrhea, burns

• Osmotic diuresis, postobstructive diuresis, loop diuretics

• Arginine vasopressin deficiency or resistance with impaired thirst mechanism

• High fever or prolonged exposure to environmental heat

● Contributing medications (eg, loop diuretics, lithium, mannitol) or high protein tube feedings.

● Recovering renal failure.

● History of polyuria (urine output >3 liters/day).

● History of salt ingestion or infusion of hypertonic sodium solutions.

● A recent seizure, which may transiently raise the serum sodium concentration due to a shift of water into cells.

Review tests usually available at the time that hypernatremia is diagnosed:

● Previous serum sodium levels, if available

● Serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose

● Urine specific gravity

If the cause of the hypernatremia ( table 1 ) is not apparent from the clinical features, obtain:

● Repeat serum sodium (if previous levels are not available)

● Urine osmolality

Urine osmolality <300 mOsmol/kg  —  Inappropriately dilute urine (eg, urine osmolality <300 mOsmol/kg or urine specific gravity <1.010) in the setting of persistent hypernatremia suggests an arginine vasopressin disorder (diabetes insipidus). Arginine vasopressin resistance (formerly called nephrogenic diabetes insipidus) refers to a decrease in urinary concentrating ability that results from resistance to the action of arginine vasopressin (also called antidiuretic hormone [ADH]). Arginine vasopressin deficiency (formerly called central diabetes insipidus) is characterized by decreased release of arginine vasopressin, resulting in a variable degree of polyuria. (See "Arginine vasopressin resistance (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus): Etiology, clinical manifestations, and postdiagnostic evaluation" and "Arginine vasopressin deficiency (central diabetes insipidus): Etiology, clinical manifestations, and postdiagnostic evaluation" .)

Neither arginine vasopressin resistance nor deficiency causes hypernatremia if thirst is normal and there is access to water. An arginine vasopressin disorder complicated by hypernatremia indicates a coexistent defect in thirst or failure to replace water losses.

Urine osmolality 300 to 600 mOsmol/kg  —  If the urine osmolality is intermediate (between 300 to 600 mOsmol/kg), the hypernatremia may be due to an osmotic diuresis or to an arginine vasopressin disorder. Osmotic diuresis is due to excretion of solutes including glucose (uncontrolled hyperglycemia in diabetes mellitus), urea (resolving acute kidney injury, high protein tube feedings or catabolism), mannitol (after administration of hypertonic mannitol to treat cerebral edema or glaucoma), or sodium (after administration of large volumes of intravenous saline). If an osmotic diuresis is not present, evaluate for an arginine vasopressin disorder. (See "Etiology and evaluation of hypernatremia in adults", section on 'Diagnostic approach' .)

Urine osmolality >600 mOsmol/kg  —  If the urine osmolality is >600 mOsmol/kg, hypernatremia is most likely due to extrarenal water losses (eg, gastrointestinal). Repeat measurements of urine and plasma osmolality should be obtained as the patient is treated with hypotonic fluids and the serum sodium returns closer to the normal range.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The reference range for serum sodium is 135 to 145 mEq/L (mmol/L) but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
