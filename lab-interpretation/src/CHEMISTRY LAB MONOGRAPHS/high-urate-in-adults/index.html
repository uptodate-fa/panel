ALGORITHM  — 

( algorithm 1 )

IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

Rapidly identify patients with any of the following:

● Severe hyperuricemia (>15 mg/dL or 893 micromol/L)

● Acute oliguric or anuric renal failure due to uric acid precipitation in the renal tubules

● Rapid overproduction and excretion of uric acid (eg, acute tumor lysis, rhabdomyolysis, brisk hemolysis)

Implement preventive measures, provide supportive care of clinically unstable patients, and obtain specialty consultation, as appropriate. (See "Uric acid kidney diseases", section on 'Acute uric acid nephropathy' .)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

For patients not requiring immediate action, determine the reason and timing of the test. In patients with established hyperuricemia, compare with prior serum urate levels and distinguish asymptomatic hyperuricemia from gout. Review:

● Complete blood count (CBC) and platelet count

● Chemistries, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, electrolytes, calcium, and liver biochemical tests

● Urinalysis

Symptomatic hyperuricemia  —  The clinical manifestations of monosodium urate crystal deposition disease may include recurrent flares of inflammatory arthritis (gout), a chronic arthropathy, tophaceous gout, uric acid nephrolithiasis, and a chronic urate nephropathy. Consider rheumatology consultation if unclear diagnosis, frequent or severe recurrences, polyarticular disease, tophi, or chronic arthritis. (See "Gout: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis" .)

Gout  —  Identification of monosodium urate crystals in clinically affected tissues or body fluids and/or the validated clinical diagnostic criteria ( algorithm 2 ) can distinguish gout from other forms of arthritis in patients presenting with monoarthritis. Serum urate may be low, normal, or high in people with a gout flare (see "Gout: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Range of signs and symptoms' ):

● Obtain a repeat serum urate two or more weeks after a gout flare completely subsides to establish a baseline.

● In patients with recurrent gout flares, management includes reduction of serum urate levels to a target of <6 mg/dL [<360 micromol/L]). (See "Gout: Pharmacologic urate-lowering therapy and treatment of tophi" .)

Uric acid nephrolithiasis  —  Uric acid stone disease can occur at any time in the course of gout and in other conditions associated with chronically increased uric acid production ( table 1 ):

● Obtain biochemical analysis of a passed stone (see "Kidney stones in adults: Uric acid nephrolithiasis" and "Uric acid kidney diseases" )

● Consider nephrology consultation to help manage underlying chronic kidney disease (CKD) in patients with gout or nephrolithiasis and to help evaluate and prevent recurrent stone disease

● Obtain urology consultation to address stones >10 mm in diameter, proximal ureteral stones, and stones failing a trial of conservative management (see "Kidney stones in adults: Diagnosis and acute management of suspected nephrolithiasis", section on 'Urologic consultation' )

Chronic urate nephropathy  —  Chronic urate nephropathy is an uncommon form of CKD induced by the deposition of sodium urate crystals in the medullary interstitium. The clinical features are indistinguishable from the multiple other more common causes of renal impairment in patients with gout (eg, hypertension, diabetes mellitus). Obtain consultation with nephrology as appropriate for other causes of CKD. (See "Uric acid kidney diseases", section on 'Chronic urate nephropathy' and "Overview of the management of chronic kidney disease in adults", section on 'Referral to nephrologists' .)

Asymptomatic hyperuricemia  —  The initial evaluation should include a comprehensive history and physical examination. Further laboratory determinations are directed to the suspected specific causative factor(s). (See "Asymptomatic hyperuricemia" .)

In patients with unexplained asymptomatic hyperuricemia, repeat serum urate after at least one week.

Level <7 mg/dL (<420 micromol/L)  —  No further evaluation of this isolated laboratory value is necessary in the absence of worrisome clinical features.

Level between 7 and 8 mg/dL (420 to 480 micromol/L)  —  Repeat testing in 6 to 12 months to determine if the serum urate level is >8 mg/dL (480 micromol/L) before initiating a more detailed evaluation.

Level >8 mg/dL (>480 micromol/L)  —  Initiate the evaluation of hyperuricemia in asymptomatic individuals when the serum urate level exceeds 8 mg/dL (480 micromol/L):

● Review medications for hyperuricemia-inducing drugs that can be discontinued or substituted. These drugs cause urate overproduction (eg, nicotinic acid or cytotoxic drugs) or decreased renal excretion (eg, thiazides and loop diuretics, low-dose salicylates, ethanol).

● Address modifiable risk factors for the development of gout (eg, increased alcohol consumption, high intake of fructose, high levels of meat and seafood ingestion).

● Identify associated disorders, including:

• Lymphoproliferative and myeloproliferative disorders

• Psoriasis

• Lead toxicity from drinking moonshine and other environmental exposure (eg, lead paint, lead dust with construction)

• Features of metabolic syndrome (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity, type 2 diabetes)

• CKD

● Inquire about family history of gout and identify any known familial disorder (eg, familial juvenile hyperuricemic nephropathy characterized by hyperuricemia and gout early in the course of the disease and by progressive renal impairment). (See "Asymptomatic hyperuricemia", section on 'Sustained marked hyperuricemia' .)

In approximately 80 to 90 percent of patients, the initial evaluation will identify one or more of the many causes of hyperuricemia, occasionally due to increased urate production ( table 1 ) and usually due to uric acid underexcretion ( table 2 ). Chronic, relatively modest hyperuricemia in CKD usually reflects decreased renal excretion. (See "Secondary factors and progression of chronic kidney disease", section on 'Hyperuricemia' .)

For patients without an identifiable cause of hyperuricemia on initial evaluation, further evaluation and need for specialty consultation depends on the clinical presentation, age, renal function, and the extent to which the urate level exceeds normal. As an example, further evaluation, usually by a rheumatologist or metabolic physician, is indicated for men <25 years and premenopausal women with stable and normal renal function.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The reference range for serum urate is generally reported as 3 to 7 mg/dL (180 to 420 micromol/L). The normal range may vary based on the population and the laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based on the reference range reported for that result.
