ALGORITHM — 

(algorithm 1)

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

Identify patients with severe (<1.0 mg/dL [0.32 mmol/L]) hypophosphatemia and/or symptomatic hypophosphatemia, including any of the following worrisome clinical findings (see "Hypophosphatemia: Clinical manifestations of phosphate depletion"):

●Muscle weakness (eg, respiratory failure or difficulty weaning from the ventilator due to weakness of the diaphragm, dysphagia, or ileus)

●Hematologic dysfunction (eg, hemolysis, mucosal bleeding)

●Rhabdomyolysis

●Encephalopathy (eg, delirium, coma, seizures)

●Impaired myocardial contractility, arrhythmia

Most symptomatic patients have a plasma phosphate concentration <1.0 mg/dL (0.32 mmol/L).

Provide hemodynamic support, if indicated. Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

Review tests usually available at the time of presentation:

●Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

●Electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose, calcium, magnesium

Obtain:

●Repeat phosphorus, calcium

●Electrocardiogram

Identify and correct underlying cause (table 1) often evident from the history and basic labs. Patients with chronic phosphate depletion (eg, alcohol use disorder, uncontrolled hyperglycemia) are prone to severe symptomatic hypophosphatemia after admission to the hospital, due to movement of extracellular phosphate into the cells during intravenous therapy with dextrose-containing solutions. (See "Hypophosphatemia: Evaluation and treatment", section on 'Patients with alcohol use disorder' and "Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state in adults: Clinical features, evaluation, and diagnosis", section on 'Serum phosphate'.)

Additional testing is based upon clinical presentation. (See "Hypophosphatemia: Evaluation and treatment", section on 'Evaluation'.)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to identify the underlying cause, which is usually apparent from the history (table 1). (See "Hypophosphatemia: Causes of hypophosphatemia".)

Spurious hypophosphatemia (pseudohypophosphatemia) may occur in certain clinical settings and should be excluded to avoid unnecessary testing and treatment of hypophosphatemia. It is usually caused by medications (eg, liposomal amphotericin, large doses of intravenous [IV] mannitol) or by paraproteins (eg, in patients with multiple myeloma or other monoclonal gammopathies) that interfere with the phosphate assay. (See "Hypophosphatemia: Evaluation and treatment", section on 'Exclude pseudohypophosphatemia'.)  

In patients with unexplained hypophosphatemia:

●Repeat phosphorus

●Review electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose, calcium, magnesium

●Review medications (table 2)

●Review dietary history (eg, malnutrition)

Transient hypophosphatemia — Transient hypophosphatemia (eg, redistribution) usually resolves within 6 to 12 hours as long as the underlying cause is corrected. No further evaluation of hypophosphatemia is required if hypophosphatemia has resolved, and there are no worrisome clinical findings.

Persistent hypophosphatemia — In the outpatient setting, most hypophosphatemic patients will not require therapy other than that aimed at the underlying cause. Reviewing the serum calcium level can identify the two most common causes:

●If calcium is high, measure parathyroid hormone (PTH) to assess for hyperparathyroidism. Even in severe hyperparathyroidism, serum phosphorus is almost never below 1.5 to 2 mg/dL.

●If calcium is low, measure 25-hydroxyvitamin D to assess for vitamin D deficiency.

If the cause is not clinically apparent, measure the 24-hour urine phosphorus excretion along with creatinine to distinguish between gastrointestinal and renal phosphate losses. Alternatively, calculate the fractional excretion of filtered phosphate (FePO4) from a random urine specimen. (See "Hypophosphatemia: Evaluation and treatment", section on 'Evaluation'.)

●Urinary excretion <100 mg/24 hours (<3.23 mmol/24 hours) or FePO4 <5 percent – Indicates an appropriate renal response to hypophosphatemia. The differential diagnosis includes:

•Decreased intestinal absorption (eg, malabsorption and chronic diarrhea, vitamin D deficiency, chronic antacid therapy, niacin therapy, chronic alcohol use)

•Redistribution from extracellular to intracellular space (eg, hyperventilation, refeeding or treatment of uncontrolled diabetes mellitus)

●Urinary excretion ≥100 mg/24 hours (≥3.23 mmol/24 hours) or FePO4 ≥5 percent – Indicates an inappropriate renal response to hypophosphatemia. The differential diagnosis includes:

•Primary hyperparathyroidism (see "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and evaluation", section on 'Diagnostic evaluation')

•Secondary hyperparathyroidism (eg, due to vitamin D deficiency) (see "Vitamin D deficiency in adults: Definition, clinical manifestations, and treatment", section on 'Evaluation of groups at high risk')

•Renal phosphate wasting (eg, Fanconi syndrome, tumor-induced osteomalacia) (see "Hypophosphatemia: Causes of hypophosphatemia", section on 'Primary renal phosphate wasting')

•Medications (eg, intravenous iron) (see "Hypophosphatemia: Causes of hypophosphatemia", section on 'Miscellaneous')

•History of chronic kidney disease with successful renal transplantation (see "Kidney transplantation in adults: Persistent hyperparathyroidism after kidney transplantation", section on 'Hypophosphatemia')

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for phosphorus is approximately 3.0 to 4.5 mg/dL (0.97 to 1.45 mmol/L) but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
