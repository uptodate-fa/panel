ALGORITHM — 

Low serum bicarbonate (HCO3) concentration can reflect a primary metabolic acidosis (table 1) or, alternatively, the compensatory response to a primary respiratory alkalosis (table 2 and algorithm 1).

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

Rapidly identify patients with low HCO3 and worrisome clinical findings, including those with:

●Hemodynamic instability or decreased perfusion due to shock, severe anemia, and/or severe hypoxemia

●Ketoacidosis due to uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, alcoholism, or starvation

●Poisonings (eg, acetaminophen, salicylate, methanol, ethylene glycol, carbon monoxide)

●Acute kidney injury (AKI) due to sepsis, trauma, and/or multiorgan failure

In patients with acute metabolic acidosis, the critical value for serum HCO3 depends on the patient's ability to compensate with increased respiration. A life-threatening HCO3 value may be <6 mEq/L in a patient with normal pulmonary and neurologic function, whereas it may be <12 mEq/L in a patient with significant underlying comorbidities. (See "Simple and mixed acid-base disorders", section on 'Response to metabolic acidosis'.)

Review chemistries available at the time of diagnosis (serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen [BUN]/creatinine, glucose, calcium, albumin, phosphorus).

Calculate the anion gap (AG). AG (mEq/L) = Na - (Cl + HCO3). Calculate an adjusted AG if hypoalbuminemia is present. Albumin-adjusted AG = AG + (2.5 x [4 - albumin in g/dL]).

Obtain:

●Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis in patients with hemodynamic instability

●Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

●Liver biochemical tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and bilirubin

●Lactate level

●Beta-hydroxybutyrate in patients with suspected diabetic ketoacidosis

●Toxicology screen and serum osmolality in patients with suspected poisonings

An ABG is useful to determine the presence of a primary respiratory disorder or to accurately determine the degree of respiratory compensation for metabolic disorders. (See "Approach to the adult with metabolic acidosis", section on 'Evaluation'.)

Measurement of peripheral venous pH and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) is an alternative, less invasive, and more convenient diagnostic procedure than arterial measurement but may give misleading results in patients with hemodynamic instability. Periodic correlation with arterial measurements should be performed if venous measurements are used for serial monitoring. (See "Simple and mixed acid-base disorders".)

Further testing and specialty consultation is based on the initial findings. Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Patients not requiring prompt therapy should undergo a timely evaluation to determine the cause(s). When a series of laboratory results are available, the analysis should consider the changes that occurred over time.

Identify the primary disorder — Low serum HCO3 concentration can reflect a primary metabolic acidosis (table 1) or, alternatively, the compensatory response to a primary respiratory alkalosis (table 2).

Review chemistries (serum electrolytes, BUN/creatinine, glucose) available at the time of diagnosis. Obtain:

●Serum albumin

●CBC with differential and platelet count

●Liver biochemical tests, including ALT, AST, and bilirubin

The diagnosis of metabolic acidosis can be made on the basis of a reduced HCO3 concentration alone (provided that chronic respiratory alkalosis can be ruled out on clinical grounds) (table 2). Consider ABG analysis or measurement of peripheral venous pH and PCO2 if the history, physical examination, and chemistries do not suggest a simple acid-base disorder or if there is clinical deterioration.

Assess the serum anion gap — Major causes of metabolic acidosis may be subdivided according to the mechanism and the AG (table 1):

●Calculate the AG (AG [mEq/L] = Na - [Cl + HCO3])

●Adjust the AG if hypoalbuminemia present (albumin-adjusted AG = AG + [2.5 x (4 - albumin in g/dL)]) or observed AG + (0.25 x [40 - observed albumin in g/L])

Normal anion gap (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis — Normal AG metabolic acidosis (AG approximately 3 to 12 mEq/L) may be due to the following:

●Diarrhea

●Diversion of urine into the gastrointestinal (GI) tract or a malfunctioning urinary bladder reconstruction/diversion

●Post-treatment of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA; loss of ketoacid anions into the urine after fluid resuscitation)

●Renal tubular acidosis (RTA; type 1, 2, or 4)

Assess for GI fluid losses (eg, diarrhea) and for a history of kidney disease. If evaluation is negative, assess for less common disorders (table 1).

(See "Acid-base and electrolyte abnormalities with diarrhea", section on 'Metabolic acidosis' and "Etiology, diagnosis, and treatment of hypoaldosteronism (type 4 RTA)" and "Etiology and diagnosis of distal (type 1) and proximal (type 2) renal tubular acidosis" and "Diabetic ketoacidosis in adults: Treatment", section on 'Resolution criteria'.)

High anion gap metabolic acidosis — An elevated AG usually indicates the presence of a metabolic acidosis due to lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, toxins/drugs, or acute or chronic kidney disease (glomerular filtration rate [GFR] <20 mL/min/1.73 m2). In patients with high AG metabolic acidosis, obtain:

●Lactate level (see "High lactate in adults")

●Beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration, if ketoacidosis is suspected

●Toxicology screen and serum osmolality, if suspected poisoning (eg, salicylate poisoning, toxic alcohol ingestion, and chronic acetaminophen use)

If a mixed acid-base disorder is suspected in patients with a high AG metabolic acidosis, calculate the delta AG/delta HCO3- ratio. (See "Simple and mixed acid-base disorders" and "The delta anion gap/delta HCO3 ratio in patients with a high anion gap metabolic acidosis".)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for serum HCO3 is approximately 23 to 28 mEq/L, although low serum HCO3 is often defined as <22 mEq/L. The normal reference ranges can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
