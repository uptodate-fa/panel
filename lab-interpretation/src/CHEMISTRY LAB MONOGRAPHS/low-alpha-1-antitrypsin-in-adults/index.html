INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT), also called protease inhibitor (PI), deficiency is a clinically under-recognized inherited disorder affecting the lungs (eg, emphysema, bronchiectasis), liver (eg, cirrhosis), and, rarely, skin (panniculitis). All adults with persistent airflow obstruction on spirometry should be tested for AAT deficiency, especially those from geographic areas with a high prevalence of AAT deficiency (eg, North America and Europe). (See "Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and natural history of alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency" and "Extrapulmonary manifestations of alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency" .)

Serum AAT assays quantify the amount of AAT protein in serum. Different tests have slightly different normal ranges, and the optimal cutoff point for detecting AAT deficiency varies somewhat with the test. AAT is an acute phase reactant. However, it is unlikely that the very low serum levels of AAT in individuals with severe AAT deficiency will increase enough to enter the normal range during clinical illness.

If an individual has a low serum AAT level, the diagnosis of AAT deficiency is confirmed by additional testing with targeted genotyping or isoelectric focusing (IEF; analysis of protein migration patterns). With the availability of targeted genotyping and gene sequencing, IEF is performed less often but is still useful for rare variants. Testing strategies depend on local preferences; simultaneous testing of AAT serum level and the confirmatory test is often performed, but sequential testing (ie, serum AAT level tested first and then genotype later if the AAT level is low) is an acceptable alternative in most circumstances.

Obtain confirmatory test, if not ordered simultaneously.

Confirmatory test  —  Targeted genotyping is preferred when available. Targeted genotyping uses polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to identify common AAT variants, such as F, I, S, and Z, although panels vary among laboratories. It identifies 95 percent of pathogenic variants ( table 1 ).

IEF analyzes protein migration patterns (also known as the phenotype) but does not assess protein function. IEF or SERPINA1 gene sequencing may be used to identify rare variants when genotyping for the common S, F, I, and Z alleles is negative and AAT deficiency is still suspected clinically.

Obtain specialty consultation if AAT deficiency is confirmed or if there is a low serum AAT level without a genetic variant.

Abnormal allele or variant protein identified  —  If the confirmatory test is positive for an abnormal allele or variant protein, AAT deficiency is confirmed. There is no need to perform another confirmatory test to establish AAT deficiency.

Abnormal allele or variant protein not identified  —  If the confirmatory test is negative, perform gene sequencing of SERPINA1 gene to identify a null or rare variant:

● Null or rare variant identified – AAT deficiency is confirmed.

● Null or rare variant not identified – Consider factors other than AAT deficiency (eg, low AAT levels due to variation in the assay or factitious due to improper storage of specimen).

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

There is laboratory-to-laboratory variation in the normal reference range. A reasonable threshold for differentiating normal genotype from other genotypes with one or more deficient alleles is approximately 20 micromol/L (100 mg/dL), depending on the assay and specific laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
