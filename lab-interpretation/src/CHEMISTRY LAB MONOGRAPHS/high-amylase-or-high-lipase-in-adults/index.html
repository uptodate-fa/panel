CLINICAL PRESENTATION CONSISTENT WITH ACUTE PANCREATITIS  — 

While serum amylase and lipase are usually obtained together in patients with abdominal pain to diagnose acute pancreatitis, lipase is more sensitive and persists longer. Predominant elevation in serum lipase and normal or minimal elevations in amylase may be due to delayed presentation of acute pancreatitis (shorter half-life of amylase), acute alcoholic pancreatitis, acute-on-chronic pancreatitis, and hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis. Amylase and/or lipase may be elevated in patients with moderate to severe reduction in renal function.

Review tests usually available at the time of presentation:

● Complete blood count with differential and platelet count

● Liver biochemical tests (including alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase)

● Serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, glucose, calcium, total protein, and albumin

● Triglycerides

● Pregnancy test in all women of childbearing potential

Amylase and/or lipase ≥3 times upper limit of normal  —  For patients with a clinical presentation consistent with acute pancreatitis (eg, acute onset of persistent, severe epigastric abdominal pain, sometimes radiating to the back, associated with nausea and vomiting) and an amylase and/or lipase level ≥3 times the upper limit of normal, the diagnosis of acute pancreatitis is likely. While most extrapancreatic causes of pancreatic enzyme elevations are not associated with a greater than threefold elevation, lipase greater than three times normal has been observed in patients with renal insufficiency, malignant tumors, cholecystitis, and esophagitis. (See "Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and natural history of acute pancreatitis", section on 'Diagnosis' and "Approach to the patient with elevated serum amylase or lipase", section on 'Elevations in amylase and lipase' .)

● Determine the etiology of acute pancreatitis ( table 1 )

● Obtain abdominal ultrasound to evaluate for cholelithiasis or choledocholithiasis

● Assess disease severity (see "Predicting the severity of acute pancreatitis" ):

• Evaluate for early fluid losses

• Identify organ failure, particularly cardiovascular, respiratory, or renal systems

● Urgent gastrointestinal consultation may be necessary in selected patients, including any of the following:

• Moderately severe or severe pancreatitis.

• Gallstone pancreatitis and cholangitis, common bile duct obstruction (visible stone on imaging, dilated common bile duct, or increasing liver tests without cholangitis).

• Signs of clinical deterioration after initial presentation.

● In acute pancreatitis, daily measurements of enzymes after initial high diagnostic values are not clinically useful.

Amylase and lipase <3 times upper limit of normal  —  For patients with acute abdominal pain and serum amylase and lipase levels <3 times the upper limit of normal, abdominal imaging is required to establish the diagnosis of acute pancreatitis and to exclude other causes of abdominal pain with elevated enzymes ( table 2 and table 3 ) (see "Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and natural history of acute pancreatitis", section on 'Diagnosis' ):

● In nonpregnant patients, perform contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan to distinguish acute pancreatitis from other causes of abdominal pain.

● In patients with renal insufficiency or severe contrast allergy, perform abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without gadolinium.

● In pregnant women, obtain pelvic ultrasound to exclude gynecologic causes (eg, ectopic pregnancy). (See "Ectopic pregnancy: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis" .)

Imaging consistent with acute pancreatitis  —  Determine the etiology of acute pancreatitis ( table 1 ). Assess disease severity as described above. (See 'Amylase and/or lipase ≥3 times upper limit of normal' above.)

Imaging not consistent with acute pancreatitis

● In patients with persistent abdominal pain with negative initial imaging, monitor abdominal pain and evaluate for imaging negative causes ( table 2 and table 3 ). Specialty consultation may be helpful to guide further evaluation (eg, for chronic pancreatitis, suspected occult pancreatic malignancy). Repeating amylase and/or lipase in such patients is not clinically useful.

● In patients in whom abdominal pain has resolved, no further evaluation for elevated amylase or lipase is necessary. Possible causes include medications ( table 4 and table 5 ), chronic alcohol use, and renal insufficiency.

(See "Approach to the patient with elevated serum amylase or lipase" .)

CLINICAL PRESENTATION ATYPICAL FOR ACUTE PANCREATITIS  — 

For patients with abdominal pain that is not characteristic for acute pancreatitis, we perform the same evaluation as described for patients with amylase or lipase <3 times the upper limit of normal. Abdominal imaging is required to establish the diagnosis of acute pancreatitis and to exclude other causes of abnormal amylase and/or lipase ( table 2 and table 3 ). The imaging modality chosen will depend on suspected etiology and renal function. (See 'Amylase and lipase <3 times upper limit of normal' above.)

Pancreatic enzymes can be elevated in the absence of an identifiable disease. Subsequent management in patients with a negative initial evaluation depends on the presence of continued abdominal pain. Patients with persistent abdominal pain with negative initial imaging should be evaluated for other causes of abdominal pain. Repeating amylase and/or lipase in such patients is not clinically useful. In patients in whom abdominal pain has resolved, we do not pursue additional evaluation. (See "Approach to the patient with elevated serum amylase or lipase" .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The reference range for amylase is approximately 25 to 125 units/L. Lipase ranges vary considerably depending on the laboratory. Normal ranges may also vary based on the population. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
