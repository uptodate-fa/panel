INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Hypomagnesemia can be induced by increased gastrointestinal or urinary losses (eg, chronic diarrhea, diuretic use, alcohol use disorder) ( table 1 ). The etiology of low magnesium can usually be determined from the history and physical examination. (See "Hypomagnesemia: Causes of hypomagnesemia" and "Hypomagnesemia: Clinical manifestations of magnesium depletion", section on 'Clinical manifestations' .)

Review tests usually available at the time of initial diagnosis:

● Electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose

● Calcium

● Phosphorus

Severe hypomagnesemia (<1 mg/dL) or symptomatic  —  Patients with severe hypomagnesemia (eg, magnesium <1 mg/dL [<0.4 mmol/L or <0.8 mEq/L]) or severe symptoms of magnesium depletion (eg, tetany, seizures, arrhythmias) require urgent treatment with intravenous (IV) magnesium in the acute setting. Symptomatic magnesium depletion is often associated with multiple biochemical abnormalities (eg, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, and metabolic alkalosis), which may contribute to the clinical findings. (See "Low calcium in adults" and "Low potassium in adults" and "Hypomagnesemia: Evaluation and treatment" .)

● Obtain (or review, if available) 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) and provide continuous cardiac monitoring during treatment

● Treat associated metabolic abnormalities (eg, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis)

In general, great caution should be exercised in treating patients who have acute or chronic kidney injury with magnesium-containing medications. (See "Hypomagnesemia: Evaluation and treatment" .)

Mild to moderate hypomagnesemia (1 mg/dL to lower limit of reference range) and asymptomatic  —  Asymptomatic patients with mild to moderate depletion (eg, 1 to 1.6 mg/dL [0.4 to 0.7 mmol/L or 0.8 to 1.4 mEq/L] in an assay with a normal reference range of 1.6 to 2.6 mg/dL) require a timely evaluation to determine and correct the cause(s) of low magnesium. If no etiology is apparent based upon history or initial laboratory testing, repeat the serum magnesium level.

If the magnesium level is persistently low and unexplained, measure a 24-hour urinary magnesium excretion (or determine the fractional excretion of magnesium on a random urine specimen ( calculator 1 and calculator 2 )) at a time when the patient is not receiving magnesium supplementation to distinguish between gastrointestinal and renal magnesium losses. (See "Hypomagnesemia: Evaluation and treatment", section on 'Evaluation' .)

The underlying disease ( table 1 ) should be corrected, if possible. Treat with magnesium, as needed. Many hospitalized patients with hypomagnesemia are given IV rather than oral magnesium supplementation, even if symptoms are minimal or absent, due to inability to take oral magnesium or to gastrointestinal side effects. In an asymptomatic patient with mild to moderate hypomagnesemia and risk factors for cardiac arrhythmias (eg, history of myocardial ischemia, postcardiac surgery), IV repletion is typically administered to avoid arrhythmias. (See "Hypomagnesemia: Evaluation and treatment" .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for serum magnesium is approximately 1.6 to 2.6 mg/dL (0.7 to 1.1 mmol/L or 1.4 to 2.2 mEq/L) but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
