IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

The severity of clinical manifestations correlates with the degree of hypokalemia, its chronicity, the presence of other electrolyte disorders, and risk factors for arrhythmia. Symptoms of hypokalemia are more common if the potassium is <3 mEq/L or if the patient has a predisposition to arrhythmia, such as use of digoxin or underlying heart disease. (See "Clinical manifestations and treatment of hypokalemia in adults", section on 'Severe or symptomatic hypokalemia'.)

Rapidly identify patients with any of the following worrisome clinical features:

●Severe hypokalemia (potassium <3 mEq/L [<3 mmol/L]).

●Worrisome symptoms of potassium depletion (eg, arrhythmias, severe muscle weakness).

●Predisposition to arrhythmia in the setting of hypokalemia (eg, structural heart disease, long QT syndrome, cardiomyopathy, recent myocardial infarction (MI), digoxin, concurrent metabolic abnormalities).

Review prior electrolytes blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose, calcium, and magnesium (if available). Obtain 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG).

Telemetry or continuous ECG monitoring is indicated for hypokalemic patients with a prolonged QT, other ECG changes associated with hypokalemia, and/or underlying cardiac issues that predispose to arrhythmia in the setting of hypokalemia. (See "Clinical manifestations and treatment of hypokalemia in adults", section on 'Cardiac arrhythmias and ECG abnormalities'.)

Symptomatic potassium depletion is often associated with multiple biochemical abnormalities (eg, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia, and metabolic alkalosis) that may contribute to the clinical findings. Treat associated metabolic abnormalities (eg, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis) if present. Patients with concurrent hypomagnesemia are refractory to potassium replacement alone. These patients may require intravenous magnesium (depending on the severity) and serial magnesium and electrolyte measurement following each dose. (See "Hypomagnesemia: Evaluation and treatment".)

In general, careful monitoring of the serum potassium during replacement is essential. Serum potassium should initially be measured every two to four hours to ascertain the response to therapy and need for dose adjustment. Great caution should be exercised in treating patients who have acute or chronic kidney injury with supplemental potassium. (See "Clinical manifestations and treatment of hypokalemia in adults", section on 'Careful monitoring'.)

Additional (simultaneous) evaluation is directed at identifying contributing factors (table 1). (See 'Initial evaluation' below and "Evaluation of the adult patient with hypokalemia", section on 'Evaluation'.)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

The etiology of low potassium can usually be determined from the history and physical examination (eg, vomiting, diarrhea, diuretic use) (table 1). (See "Causes of hypokalemia in adults" and "Evaluation of the adult patient with hypokalemia", section on 'Evaluation'.)

●Review medications/intravenous fluids.

●Review available chemistries, including serum electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose, calcium, magnesium.

●Identify and correct underlying cause (eg, renal loss, gastrointestinal loss, low magnesium).

●Repeat the serum potassium.

If mild to moderate hypokalemia persists, and there is no apparent etiology based upon history or initial laboratory testing:

●Obtain (review) serum calcium, magnesium.

●Obtain a spot urine for potassium and creatinine concentrations to estimate urine potassium excretion and to help identify reasons for hypokalemia. The urine potassium-to-creatinine ratio is usually less than 13 mEq/g creatinine (1.5 mEq/mmol creatinine) when hypokalemia is caused by transcellular potassium shifts, gastrointestinal losses, or previous use of diuretics. Higher values are seen with renal potassium wasting.

●Assess acid-base status (eg, serum sodium, bicarbonate, and chloride and, in some cases, urine sodium and chloride) to distinguish renal potassium losses from other causes (table 1). As examples:

•Metabolic acidosis with a low rate of urinary potassium excretion could signify laxative abuse or a villous adenoma (many villous adenomas can generate metabolic acidosis, but about 20 percent generate metabolic alkalosis).

•Metabolic acidosis with urinary potassium wasting may be due to diabetic ketoacidosis or to type 1 (distal) or type 2 (proximal) renal tubular acidosis.

•Metabolic alkalosis with urinary potassium wasting may be due to diuretic use, vomiting, Gitelman or Bartter syndrome, or some villous adenomas).

●Assess the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis in patients with hypertension and low potassium. (See "Diagnosis of primary aldosteronism", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

The underlying disease (table 1) should be corrected, if possible. Replete potassium, as needed. In general, great caution should be exercised in treating patients who have acute or chronic kidney injury with supplemental potassium. (See "Clinical manifestations and treatment of hypokalemia in adults", section on 'Estimation of the potassium deficit'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal range for serum potassium is approximately 3.5 to 5.0 mEq/L (mmol/L) but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
