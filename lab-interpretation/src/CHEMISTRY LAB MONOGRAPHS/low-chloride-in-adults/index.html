INITIAL EVALUATION — 

A low serum chloride should be interpreted in the context of the clinical presentation as well as with other components of the metabolic profile, including the serum sodium concentration and the serum bicarbonate concentration.

A low serum chloride is usually accompanied by a low serum sodium and/or a high serum bicarbonate. Patients with these associated electrolyte abnormalities may have worrisome clinical findings requiring immediate action. (See "Low sodium in adults", section on 'Immediate action' and "High bicarbonate in adults", section on 'Immediate action'.)

The cause of a low serum chloride is often apparent from the history, physical examination, review of medications for diuretic use, and assessment of volume status.

Review:

●Electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose

●Prior electrolytes, if available

Coexisting electrolyte abnormalities — If other electrolyte abnormalities are present (usually a low serum sodium and/or a high bicarbonate), the evaluation of these abnormalities takes precedence.

Occasionally, both the serum chloride and serum bicarbonate may be low in some patients with a high anion gap metabolic acidosis; the causes include uremia, lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, salicylates, and toxic alcohols (table 1). In addition, the anion gap is sometimes elevated in hypochloremic patients who have a normal or elevated serum bicarbonate; this finding indicates that a mixed acid-base disturbance is present. (See "Simple and mixed acid-base disorders".)

Low sodium — A low serum sodium concentration usually indicates hypotonicity, although there are exceptions (eg, hyperglycemia). If hyperglycemia is present, calculate the corrected sodium (calculator 1).

A low serum chloride with a low corrected serum sodium may be due to any of the disorders that cause hyponatremia (eg, water retention in excess of sodium (table 2), significant volume depletion). A disproportionate loss of chloride compared with sodium may be due to gastric losses (gastric chloride content is significantly greater than gastric sodium content) due, for example, to gastric outlet obstruction, protracted vomiting in alcoholics, or self-induced vomiting.

If the corrected sodium is low, the diagnostic approach to low serum chloride is the same as the approach to hyponatremia. (See "Diagnostic evaluation of adults with hyponatremia" and "Low sodium in adults", section on 'Initial evaluation'.)

High bicarbonate — A low serum chloride with a high serum bicarbonate may reflect either a primary metabolic alkalosis or a chronic respiratory acidosis (reduced chloride and elevated bicarbonate are due to metabolic compensation to hypercapnia). (See "High bicarbonate in adults", section on 'Initial evaluation'.)

Chronic respiratory acidosis can usually be excluded on clinical grounds so that confirmation of metabolic alkalosis does not always require blood gases. Risk factors for hypercapnia include severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (emphysema); obstructive sleep apnea; sedative use; postanesthesia; and central nervous system, neuromuscular, and/or thoracic cage disorders. Patients with a high bicarbonate due to respiratory acidosis are hypoxic on room air.

Major causes of metabolic alkalosis include vomiting (or nasogastric suction), loop or thiazide diuretics, and mineralocorticoid excess (table 3). If a low serum chloride and high serum bicarbonate resolve promptly with intravenous fluids, it can generally be attributed to metabolic alkalosis caused by either loss of gastric fluid or diuretics.

When the cause is not apparent from the history of physical examination, obtain:

●Repeat electrolytes

●A spot urine chloride

The urine chloride is low (<10 to 20 mEq/L) when metabolic alkalosis is associated with volume contraction and chloride depletion (eg, gastric loss of chloride, diuretics [but only once their diuretic effect has worn off or waned]). The urine chloride is not low (>20 mEq/L) in alkalotic patients who are still taking diuretics and in patients with Bartter's syndrome, Gitelman's syndrome, and hypertensive disorders associated with mineralocorticoid excess. (See "Causes of metabolic alkalosis".)

Isolated low chloride — An isolated low serum chloride is likely due to laboratory error.

Obtain:

●Repeat electrolytes, BUN, creatinine

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for serum chloride is approximately 98 to 106 mEq/L (mmol/L). The normal reference range can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
