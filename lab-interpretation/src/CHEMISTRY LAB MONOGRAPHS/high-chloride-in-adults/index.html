INITIAL EVALUATION — 

A high serum chloride should be interpreted in the context of the clinical presentation as well as with other components of the metabolic profile, including the serum sodium concentration, the serum bicarbonate concentration, and the serum anion gap (the difference between the serum sodium concentration and the sum of the serum chloride and bicarbonate [Na – {CL + HCO3}]). (See "Low bicarbonate in adults", section on 'Immediate action' and "High sodium in adults", section on 'Immediate action'.)

The cause of high chloride is often apparent from the history, physical examination, and review of medications, fluids, and other laboratory tests. In hospitalized patients, high chloride may result from administration of relatively high chloride intravenous fluids (eg, 0.9% sodium chloride). In addition, hypoalbuminemia is usually associated with a reciprocally elevated serum chloride. Rarely, chloride measurements are spuriously elevated by other anions (eg, due to medications containing bromide, iodides) or high salicylate levels.

Review:

●Electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose

●Prior electrolytes, if available

●Fluid administration

Coexisting electrolyte abnormalities — If other electrolyte abnormalities are present, the evaluation of these abnormalities may take precedence.

Low bicarbonate — Low serum bicarbonate with high chloride may reflect a primary normal anion gap metabolic acidosis or the compensatory response to a primary chronic respiratory alkalosis. If chronic respiratory alkalosis can be ruled out (table 1), then a diagnosis of metabolic acidosis can be made. (See "Low bicarbonate in adults" and "Approach to the adult with metabolic acidosis", section on 'Diagnosis and evaluation'.)

High sodium — A high serum chloride with a high serum sodium may be due to any of the disorders that cause hypernatremia (most often due to unreplaced water loss (table 2)). Further evaluation depends on the clinical features. Patients with acute hypernatremia usually require hospitalization to manage the cause of hypernatremia and to prevent additional fluid losses. (See "High sodium in adults", section on 'Initial evaluation' and "Etiology and evaluation of hypernatremia in adults" and "Treatment of hypernatremia in adults", section on 'Approach to therapy'.)

Isolated high chloride — An isolated high serum chloride in the absence of hypoalbuminemia is most often due to laboratory error or to pseudohyperchloremia (eg, bromide or iodide ingestion or toxic salicylate levels). (See "Serum anion gap in conditions other than metabolic acidosis", section on 'Low serum anion gap' and "Serum anion gap in conditions other than metabolic acidosis", section on 'Negative serum anion gap'.)

Obtain:

●Repeat electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, albumin

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for serum chloride is approximately 98 to 106 mEq/L. The normal reference range can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
