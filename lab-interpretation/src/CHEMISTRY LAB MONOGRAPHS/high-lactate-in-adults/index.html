IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

Lactate levels are most commonly measured to evaluate acutely ill patients with metabolic acidosis and/or tissue hypoperfusion caused by hypovolemia, cardiac failure, or sepsis. The presence of an elevated serum lactate level is associated with adverse outcomes, including the development of shock. The early stages of shock are more amenable to therapy and are more likely to be reversible compared with end-stage shock, which is associated with irreversible end-organ damage and death. (See "Causes of lactic acidosis" and "Evaluation of and initial approach to the adult patient with undifferentiated hypotension and shock" .)

Clinical manifestations of shock present  —  Assess for signs of shock. Shock is a physiologic continuum, and the clinical findings vary with the stage of presentation. Mild to moderate hyperlactatemia may be the first or only clinical sign of early shock.

Rapidly identify patients with worrisome clinical features associated with shock or early shock (cryptic shock), including any of the following:

● Hypotension or change in baseline blood pressure.

● Tachypnea.

● Tachycardia.

● Altered mental status.

● Altered skin temperature.

• Warm, hyperemic skin may be present prior to the onset of compensatory vasoconstriction in early distributive shock.

• Cold, clammy, cyanotic skin in overt shock.

● Elevated lactate.

• Rising lactate level (2 to 4 mmol/L) in early shock.

• Lactic acidosis (lactate level >4 mmol/L [>36 mg/dL]) in overt shock.

● End-organ dysfunction (eg, oliguria).

Rapidly restore tissue perfusion (eg, aggressively administer intravenous fluids) in patients with clinical manifestations of shock. Provide other early empiric therapies. Life-saving therapies should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

Obtain (review):

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential, platelet count.

● Chemistries (electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen [BUN], creatinine, glucose).

● Liver biochemical tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), bilirubin, albumin.

● Coagulation studies (prothrombin time [PT], activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT], D-dimer).

● Blood gas analysis.

● Blood cultures.

● Troponin, brain natriuretic peptide (BNP).

● Electrocardiogram (ECG).

● Chest radiography.

● Point-of-care (POC) ultrasonography in selected patients with undifferentiated shock and hypotension.

After stabilization, additional laboratory tests and imaging are directed at specific etiologies ( table 1 ) or the sequelae of shock (eg, respiratory acidosis, acute kidney injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation [DIC]).

Clinical manifestations of shock absent

● Infection – A high lactate in the absence of clinical manifestations of shock may be due to localized infection and/or early sepsis. In patients with sepsis, there is a continuum of severity ranging from bacteremia to septic shock, such that some patients with early blood stream infection may not have obvious clinical features of shock. All patients with a focus of infection or bacteremia are at risk of developing sepsis. In such patients, obtain additional microbiologic cultures (eg, sputum, urine, intravascular catheter, wound or surgical site, body fluids) as indicated. Imaging is directed at defining the potential source of infection. (See "Evaluation and management of suspected sepsis and septic shock in adults", section on 'Initial therapy' and "Evaluation and management of suspected sepsis and septic shock in adults", section on 'Choosing a regimen' .)

Provide empiric therapies and confirm that the patient has received appropriate treatment of the infection. (See "Evaluation and management of suspected sepsis and septic shock in adults", section on 'Initial therapy' and "Evaluation and management of suspected sepsis and septic shock in adults", section on 'Choosing a regimen' .)

Although lactate-guided resuscitation has not been convincingly associated with improved outcomes, we follow serum lactate levels every six hours until there is a definitive clinical response and the levels are clearly falling. A rising lactate level should prompt reevaluation. (See "Evaluation and management of suspected sepsis and septic shock in adults", section on 'Laboratory' .)

● Regional tissue hypoperfusion – Lactate levels may be elevated in patients with regional tissue hypoperfusion (eg, intestinal ischemia, burns, traumatic limb injury). Target the evaluation to identify the likely cause.

● Other – Other causes of a high lactate level include drugs and toxin ingestions/exposure. In patients with poisoning, elevated lactate levels may indicate cellular hypoxia. Multiple factors may contribute to an elevated level (eg, sepsis in a patient with diabetes taking metformin).

• Drug-induced lactic acidosis.

- Acetaminophen overdose. (See "Acetaminophen (paracetamol) poisoning in adults: Pathophysiology, presentation, and evaluation" .)

- Salicylate poisoning. (See "Salicylate (aspirin) poisoning: Clinical manifestations and evaluation" .)

- Metformin toxicity. (See "Metformin poisoning and toxicity" .)

- Antiretroviral medication-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. (See "Mitochondrial toxicity of HIV nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors", section on 'Hyperlactatemia and lactic acidosis' .)

• Toxin ingestions/exposure.

- Chronic, severe alcoholism. (See "Fasting ketosis and alcoholic ketoacidosis", section on 'Alcoholic ketoacidosis' .)

- Cyanide poisoning. (See "Cyanide poisoning", section on 'Lactate' .)

- Carbon monoxide poisoning. (See "Carbon monoxide poisoning" .)

- Ethylene glycol poisoning. (See "Methanol and ethylene glycol poisoning: Pharmacology, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis", section on 'Lactate' .)

Ethylene glycol may falsely elevate lactate levels due to the inability of many laboratory instruments to differentiate between lactate and the metabolites of ethylene glycol that are structurally similar to lactate.

• Intense exercise, shivering, or seizures – Lactic acidosis rapidly resolves when the muscle activity slows or ceases.

• Falsely elevated lactate levels – The lactate concentration may be artifactually high in patients with ethylene glycol intoxication due to the inability of many laboratory instruments to differentiate between lactate and either glycolate or glycoxylate.

Provide early empiric therapies while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing. Stop any offending drugs, and treat complications (eg, acute kidney injury). (See "Causes of lactic acidosis", section on 'Type B lactic acidosis' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for serum or plasma lactate is 0.7 to 2.1 mmol/L (6.3 to 18.9 mg/dL) but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the laboratory-specific normal value reported for that result.

Lactate levels >2 mmol/L (>18 mg/dL) represent hyperlactatemia. Lactic acidosis is defined as a serum lactate concentration >4 mmol/L (>36 mg/dL).
