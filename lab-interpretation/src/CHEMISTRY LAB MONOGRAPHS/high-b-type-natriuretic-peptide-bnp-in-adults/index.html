ALGORITHM  — 

This monograph and algorithm address the use of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) in the evaluation of suspected decompensated heart failure (HF) or dyspnea of uncertain etiology ( algorithm 1 ). The role of BNP in the evaluation of other cardiac or noncardiac conditions, or in the management of HF, is less well defined. Please refer to UpToDate topics for other uses. (See "Natriuretic peptide measurement in non-heart failure settings" .)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The diagnosis of acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) is based primarily on signs and symptoms and appropriate investigations, such as electrocardiogram (ECG), chest radiograph, biomarkers (BNP, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP], and troponin), and Doppler echocardiography. In patients with symptoms that may be due to HF and/or a noncardiac condition, an elevated BNP may be useful to support a diagnosis of HF (see "Approach to diagnosis and evaluation of acute decompensated heart failure in adults", section on 'Diagnostic evaluation' and "Heart failure: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis in adults" ):

● Results of BNP testing must be interpreted with caution because many conditions cause an elevated BNP (or NT-proBNP) ( table 1 ), including other cardiac conditions (eg, coronary heart disease, valvular heart disease, constrictive pericarditis), pulmonary disease (eg, pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary emboli), neurologic disorders (eg, stroke), sepsis, acute and chronic kidney insufficiency, and drugs (eg, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors). Some patients with end-stage HF, acute pulmonary edema, and right-sided HF may have unexpectedly low BNP levels. BNP levels may be normal in patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).

● BNP is cleared by the kidneys, and optimal cutoff values for diagnosis in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) have not been clearly established. The impact of kidney insufficiency should be considered in patients with elevated BNP and NT-proBNP; small increases may not be clinically important. (The main value would be finding a low plasma BNP, which would exclude HF as the cause of symptoms.)

● In patients receiving angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors, NT-proBNP should be obtained instead of BNP. BNP (unlike NT-proBNP) is degraded by neprilysin, so neprilysin inhibition causes elevation of BNP. (See "High N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide in adults" .)

● Normal plasma BNP values increase with age and are higher in females than males. Thus, somewhat higher cutoff values may be needed in older patients, although the optimal discriminatory values have not been determined.

Suspected acute decompensated heart failure or dyspnea of uncertain etiology  —  Patients with suspected ADHF require prompt diagnosis, identification of the underlying cause(s) (eg, ischemia) and triggers (eg, infection, uncontrolled hypertension, rhythm disturbances, nonadherence to medications and diet), and treatment.

BNP measurements should be used as an adjunct to, and not a substitute for, clinical assessment. They may be useful as a part of the assessment of patients presenting with dyspnea of uncertain etiology, including patients with a history of cardiomyopathy and HF who may have a noncardiac cause of dyspnea. Any concurrent conditions that may cause dyspnea or exacerbate HF (eg, lung disease, anemia) should be identified and managed.

BNP is most useful in patients with intermediate pretest probability of HF. The pretest probability of HF is based on traditional assessment (ie, a comprehensive history, physical examination, and review of tests usually obtained at the time of BNP measurement).

High pretest probability of acute heart failure  —  In patients with a high probability of ADHF (eg, new-onset orthopnea, elevated jugular venous pressure [JVP], typical chest radiograph findings of pulmonary edema, and no fever), the diagnostic evaluation should proceed independent of BNP level. The patient's symptoms are likely due to ADHF. The value of serial BNP measurements in guiding management of acute HF has not been supported by definitive studies.

Further evaluation should include echocardiography for patients with new HF and for patients with a prior history of HF with suspected change in cardiac function.

Intermediate pretest probability of heart failure  —  In patients with an intermediate probability of HF (eg, symptoms of dyspnea in a patient with predisposing conditions for HF, no evidence of fluid retention), BNP thresholds may be used to complement clinical assessment of HF risk.

BNP >400 pg/mL  —  A BNP value >400 pg/mL is consistent with HF, although other conditions can lead to increased levels. Patients may present with more than one cause of dyspnea (such as pneumonia in the setting of chronic HF) ( table 1 ).

BNP 100 to 400 pg/mL  —  In the range between 100 and 400 pg/mL, plasma BNP concentrations are not very sensitive or specific for detecting or excluding HF. Other diagnoses, such as pulmonary embolism, left ventricular dysfunction without exacerbation, and cor pulmonale, should also be considered in patients with plasma BNP concentrations in this range.

Obtain echocardiography as part of the initial evaluation.

Low pretest probability of heart failure  —  In patients with a low probability of HF (ie, symptoms of dyspnea without evidence of cardiac disease [including a normal ECG] and with another explanation for dyspnea, such as chronic lung disease or pneumonia), management should proceed independent of the BNP level. (See "Approach to the patient with dyspnea", section on 'Follow-up testing in chronic dyspnea' and "Approach to the adult with dyspnea in the emergency department", section on 'Acute coronary syndrome/heart failure' .)

Depending on clinical suspicion, further evaluation may include any of the following:

● Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) or ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan (pulmonary embolism)

● Peak flow and spirometry (asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD])

● Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (to support the possibility of deconditioning)

Suspected chronic decompensated heart failure  —  In patients with chronic HF, BNP plays little role in management. The diagnosis of chronic decompensated HF is generally straightforward, especially when a patient presents with fluid retention and exertional dyspnea. It is essential to rule out alternate causes of the patient's symptoms and signs. Most often, BNP is not low enough to exclude worsening HF, and clinical decisions are based on clinical presentation (see "Natriuretic peptide measurement in heart failure", section on 'Chronic HF' ):

● Review initial laboratory and imaging studies

● Obtain echocardiography in patients with an abrupt or unexplained change in cardiac function

● Review medications and optimize therapy for HF

Most experts would not regularly monitor BNP in a patient with known chronic HF and elevated BNP. Although BNP predicts clinical outcomes in all stages of HF, BNP does not provide any prognostic value in an individual patient.

BNP levels compared with NT-proBNP levels  —  Limited data are available comparing the diagnostic and prognostic values of plasma BNP and NT-proBNP, and there is no universal conversion factor to compare BNP and NT-proBNP levels. In most healthy adults, both BNP and NT-proBNP are low. In patients with left ventricular dysfunction, plasma NT-proBNP rises more than BNP, with NT-proBNP concentrations approximately fourfold higher than BNP concentrations. In individuals with CKD, reduced renal clearance causes increased plasma BNP and even greater elevation in NT-proBNP concentrations.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

Plasma levels may vary depending upon the assay used, age (age-related shift towards higher values), sex (higher values in females), body mass index (BMI; lower levels with higher BMI >30 kg/m 2 ), kidney disease (increased), and genetic factors. There are no well-defined reference limits based on age and sex for BNP. In general, BNP levels <100 pg/mL are optimal for excluding a diagnosis of HF. When interpreting serial BNP results, intraindividual biologic and analytic assay variation must be taken into account. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported for that result.
