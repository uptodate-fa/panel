ALGORITHM — 

(algorithm 1)

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

Rapidly identify patients who require immediate intervention to avoid worsening of kidney injury and/or potentially fatal complications, including those with any of the following:

●Oliguria/anuria (eg, acute postrenal obstruction from tumors or kidney stones, rhabdomyolysis).

●Rapidly rising serum creatinine (SCr) concentration (eg, ≥0.3 mg/dL or >50 percent increase in 48 hours).

●Suspicion for severe systemic disorders (eg, sepsis, vasculitis).

●Suspicion for rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis.

●Complications of progressive kidney injury (eg, severe hyperkalemia, fluid overload, uremia).

Stabilize the patient in the acute care setting and arrange for specialty consultation as appropriate. Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing. (See "Evaluation of acute kidney injury among hospitalized adult patients" and "Overview of the management of acute kidney injury (AKI) in adults".)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely assessment to determine the clinical significance of an abnormal SCr. In the outpatient setting, this may require telephone evaluation or referral to office, depending on the reason for the test and degree of abnormality. (See "Assessment of kidney function".)

Determine whether SCr is stable (ie, rising or falling):

●Review prior SCr (if available) to determine the change in SCr from the patient's baseline

●Review electrolytes (typically drawn with creatinine as part of a panel)

●Review urinalysis (if available)

●Review medications

Seemingly well patient without risk factors for kidney disease — In a seemingly well individual without risk factors for acute or subacute kidney disease and without a known history of chronic kidney disease (CKD), the creatinine elevation may be due to an easily remediable cause (eg, volume depletion) or it may be transient:

●Correct hypovolemia.

●Address medications that may increase SCr (eg, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs], diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme [ACE] inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers).

●Repeat creatinine in 24 to 48 hours to exclude transient elevation.

A transient elevation of SCr does not require further evaluation if the patient has no worrisome clinical features. A persistent elevation or rising SCr requires further monitoring and evaluation.

Known chronic kidney disease — CKD is defined by the presence of kidney damage (urinary albumin excretion rate ≥30 mg/day) or decreased kidney function (decreased estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <60 mL/min) that persists for at least three months (table 1). (See "Definition and staging of chronic kidney disease in adults" and "Chronic kidney disease (newly identified): Clinical presentation and diagnostic approach in adults".)

Stable serum creatinine elevation — In patients with known CKD who have a stable SCr:

●Evaluate the cause of CKD if unknown (eg, urinalysis with microscopy, kidney ultrasound, random "spot" urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, other tests as appropriate).

●Determine stage of CKD based on eGFR and the degree of albuminuria (table 2). Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) estimation equations are more accurate than SCr for following gradual changes in GFR over time in CKD.

●Address risk factors and comorbid conditions (eg, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, complications of CKD).

●Treat reversible causes and complications. Adjust medication doses based on eGFR.

Indications for nephrology consultation, even if "stable" SCr, include:

●High-grade proteinuria (≥1 g/24 hours) and/or hematuria to evaluate the possibility of glomerular disease.

●eGFR <30 mL/min, stage 4 CKD to anticipate need for kidney replacement therapy.

●Evidence of progressive kidney disease or increased risk of developing future kidney or cardiovascular complications (eg, uncontrolled diabetes or hypertension).

In addition, referral may be helpful to ensure implementation of the appropriate management plan by the primary care team. (See "Overview of the management of chronic kidney disease in adults".)

Unstable serum creatinine elevation — In patients with known CKD, a rise in creatinine (reduction in GFR) implies either progression of the underlying disease or the development of a superimposed and often reversible problem. Address any obvious, remediable reason for SCr elevation (eg, hypovolemia). Repeat SCr to monitor response to treatment. Determine the pace of the creatinine rise. Of note, GFR estimating equations are not reliable in the setting of unstable SCr:

●Steady progression (slowly rising SCr over months to years) – In CKD, the SCr rises slowly over time and may fluctuate. If steady progression in SCr elevation, address risk factors and underlying disease (eg, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease). Consult nephrology, if not already following.

●Abrupt rise in SCr (days to weeks) – If the rise in SCr is abrupt, evaluate for acute or subacute kidney injury (in the setting of CKD). (See 'Acute kidney disease' below.)

Acute kidney disease — Acute kidney disease refers to acute kidney injury (AKI) and subacute injury:

●AKI is an abrupt and frequently reversible decline in the GFR, depending on the clinical setting of the patient and comorbidities. Although there is no standard definition of AKI, accepted criteria include an increase in SCr ≥0.3 mg/dL (27 micromol/L) or >50 percent increase in SCr within 48 hours or decrease in urine output to <0.5 mL/kg/hour for ≥6 hours (table 3).

●Subacute kidney injury develops more slowly than AKI, but the SCr generally worsens over <3 months duration. The initial evaluation of an increase in SCr of uncertain duration is similar to the approach to subacute kidney injury.

If there is an obvious, remedial reason for the rise in creatinine (eg, hypovolemia), address the reason and repeat the SCr, typically drawn with electrolytes as part of a panel. Ensure that hyperkalemia is not present.

If there is not an apparent remedial reason, further evaluation includes:

●Complete blood count with differential and platelet count

●Serum electrolytes, creatinine, glucose, calcium, phosphorus, uric acid

●Electrocardiogram, if hyperkalemic

●Urinalysis (with microscopy if routine part of urinalysis)

Obtain in selected patients:

●Urine culture to exclude infection

●Random albumin-to-creatinine ratio to quantify proteinuria in those with positive urine dipstick for proteinuria or to stage CKD

●Ultrasound imaging to exclude obstruction or to evaluate unexplained kidney injury

Results of the urinalysis and kidney ultrasound generally direct the diagnostic evaluation. Knowing the disease duration helps to narrow the differential diagnosis and to provide prognostic information to guide management. (See "Evaluation of acute kidney injury among hospitalized adult patients" and "Diagnostic approach to adult patients with subacute kidney injury in an outpatient setting" and "Overview of the management of acute kidney injury (AKI) in adults".)

Obtain nephrology consult if the cause is unexplained, if there is ongoing kidney injury despite treatment, or if there is concern that kidney replacement therapy may be needed.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal SCr varies according to sex, race, and body size in part related to muscle mass. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
