ALGORITHM  — 

( algorithm 1 )

IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

Rapidly identify patients who have any of the following worrisome clinical findings:

● Shock, early sepsis, hemodynamic instability (see "Evaluation of and initial approach to the adult patient with undifferentiated hypotension and shock" )

● Fever, chills, right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain, jaundice, and/or a history of prior biliary surgery (see "Complications of laparoscopic cholecystectomy" )

● Severe, acute liver injury (see "Acute liver failure in adults: Etiology, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis" )

Review initial laboratory tests, usually available at the time of diagnosis:

● Liver biochemical tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)

● Liver function tests – albumin, prothrombin time (PT)/international normalized ratio (INR)

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

Obtain:

● Blood, fluid, and urine cultures, as appropriate

● RUQ imaging

Subsequent evaluation is based on clinical findings. Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing. The differential diagnosis includes acute bacterial cholangitis, complications of cholecystitis, and acute liver injury. (See "Acute cholangitis: Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and management", section on 'Diagnostic approach' and "Acute liver failure in adults: Management and prognosis" .)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to determine the cause of high alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin.

Repeat liver biochemical tests  —  Repeat ALT, AST, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase, and obtain (or review) albumin and PT/INR.

● Transient – Transient abnormalities in liver biochemical and function tests do not require further evaluation in asymptomatic patients without risk factors for liver disease.

● Persistent – If tests are persistently abnormal over one to two weeks, review medications, including over-the-counter drugs that may cause drug-induced liver injury. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) maintains a searchable database of drugs, herbal medications, and dietary supplements associated with drug-induced liver injury.

Review tests usually available at the time of presentation:

● CBC with differential and platelet count

● Total and conjugated (direct) bilirubin

Elevated alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin levels may reflect cholestatic injury (intrahepatic or extrahepatic), hepatocellular injury, or mixed injury. In patients with cholestasis, conjugated bilirubin is the predominant fraction. However, liver biochemical tests may not always distinguish hepatocellular injury from cholestatic syndromes, because of overlapping and variable presentation of these diseases. Obtain imaging (typically RUQ ultrasound) to assess bile ducts and the hepatic parenchyma. (See "Approach to the patient with abnormal liver tests", section on 'Elevated alkaline phosphatase' and "Enzymatic measures of hepatic cholestasis (alkaline phosphatase, 5'-nucleotidase, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase)", section on 'Clinical interpretation' .)

Review RUQ imaging

Dilated bile ducts  —  Dilated bile ducts suggest extrahepatic cholestasis (eg, gallstones, strictures, malignancy) ( table 1 ). Obtain a gastroenterology (GI) consult as needed for guidance regarding additional imaging (eg, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography [MRCP], endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography [ERCP]).

Nondilated bile ducts  —  A nondilated biliary system suggests intrahepatic cholestasis (eg, viral hepatitis, hepatotoxicity from drugs or toxins, alcohol-associated liver disease) ( table 2 ). However, extrahepatic cholestasis may be present despite nondilated bile ducts on imaging.

If intrahepatic cholestasis is suspected, perform sequential testing (eg, serologic tests for viral hepatitis, ferritin for hemochromatosis) guided by clinical features to determine the cause. (See "Diagnostic approach to the adult with jaundice or asymptomatic hyperbilirubinemia", section on 'Subsequent testing based on pattern of liver injury' and "Approach to the patient with abnormal liver tests", section on 'Elevated serum aminotransferases' .)

Obtain GI consultation for patients with intrahepatic cholestasis and any of the following:

● Persistent unexplained liver test abnormalities (≥2 times the upper limit of normal for aminotransferases or 1.5 times the upper limit of normal for alkaline phosphatase [of hepatic origin])

● Liver parenchymal abnormalities identified by imaging to assess for infiltrative diseases or chronic liver disease

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for serum alkaline phosphatase is 30 to 120 units/L. The normal range for total bilirubin is approximately 0.2 to 1.2 mg/dL (3.4 to 20.5 micromol/L) and for conjugated (direct) bilirubin approximately 0.1 to 0.4 mg/dL (1.7 to 6.8 micromol/L). Reference ranges may vary based on age, sex, race, and clinical circumstances. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
