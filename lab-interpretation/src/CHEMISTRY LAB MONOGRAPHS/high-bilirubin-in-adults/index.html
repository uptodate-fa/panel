ALGORITHM  — 

Bilirubin is measured along with other liver biochemical tests in patients with suspected liver disease or suspected hemolytic anemia. An elevated bilirubin may also be an incidental finding ( algorithm 1 ).

IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

The approach to the patient with high bilirubin depends on the clinical setting. In patients with hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice may or may not be present. Jaundice is usually not detected until the serum bilirubin is >2 mg/dL (34 micromol/L).

Suspected liver disease  —  Rapidly identify patients with worrisome clinical findings, including those with any of the following:

● Shock, early sepsis, hemodynamic instability (see "Evaluation of and initial approach to the adult patient with undifferentiated hypotension and shock" )

● Fever, chills, right upper quadrant pain, jaundice, and/or a history of prior biliary surgery (see "Acute cholangitis: Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and management" )

● Severe, acute liver injury (see "Acute liver failure in adults: Etiology, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis" )

Total bilirubin should be interpreted in conjunction with the following tests, usually available at the time of diagnosis:

● Liver biochemical tests, including alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)

● Prothrombin time (PT)/international normalized ratio (INR)

● Albumin

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

Obtain:

● Blood cultures as appropriate

● Right upper quadrant imaging

Findings from the patient's history, physical examination, and initial laboratory tests guide the subsequent evaluation and consultation. Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

● (See "Evaluation of and initial approach to the adult patient with undifferentiated hypotension and shock", section on 'Hemodynamic support' .)

● (See "Acute cholangitis: Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and management", section on 'Management' .)

● (See "Acute liver failure in adults: Management and prognosis" .)

Suspected hemolytic anemia  —  In patients with suspected hemolytic anemia, bilirubin should be interpreted in conjunction with the following tests, usually available at the time of diagnosis:

● CBC with differential, platelet count, corrected or absolute reticulocyte count

● Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin

● Peripheral blood smear

Additional information is reviewed in a separate monograph. (See "Low hemoglobin, hematocrit in adults" .)

Obtain hematologic consultation to guide evaluation and management of new-onset hemolysis. (See "Diagnosis of hemolytic anemia in adults", section on 'Diagnostic approach' .)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to determine the cause of hyperbilirubinemia (see "Classification and causes of jaundice or asymptomatic hyperbilirubinemia" and "Diagnostic approach to the adult with jaundice or asymptomatic hyperbilirubinemia" ):

● Review liver biochemical tests

The pattern of liver biochemical test abnormalities may suggest the underlying cause of liver disease: primarily the result of hepatocyte injury (elevated aminotransferases) or cholestasis (elevated alkaline phosphatase). In some cases, two or more factors may contribute to the development of the abnormal test. Consider the underlying illness, recent therapy (eg, drugs, surgery), and possible associated complications.

Normal alkaline phosphatase, AST, and ALT  —  The initial step in evaluating a patient with isolated hyperbilirubinemia is to fractionate the bilirubin to determine whether the hyperbilirubinemia is predominantly conjugated (direct hyperbilirubinemia) or unconjugated (indirect hyperbilirubinemia) ( table 1 ) (see "Approach to the patient with abnormal liver tests", section on 'Isolated hyperbilirubinemia' ):

● Elevated indirect bilirubin – An increase in indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin results from bilirubin overproduction (eg, hemolysis) or impairment of uptake or conjugation of bilirubin (eg, Gilbert syndrome). (See "Diagnosis of hemolytic anemia in adults", section on 'Diagnostic approach' and "Gilbert syndrome" .)

● Elevated direct bilirubin – A predominant elevation in direct (conjugated) bilirubin is rare and may be due to genetic disorders of biliary excretion or reuptake of bilirubin glucuronides. (See "Inherited disorders associated with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia" .)

Abnormal alkaline phosphatase, AST, and/or ALT  —  The pattern of liver test abnormalities may indicate the underlying cause. A disproportionate increase in ALT and/or AST relative to alkaline phosphatase suggests hepatocellular injury, whereas a disproportionate increase in alkaline phosphatase suggests a cholestatic process. With either pattern, both fractions of bilirubin are usually elevated, and tests of synthetic function (eg, INR, albumin) may be abnormal:

● Predominant alkaline phosphatase elevation – In most instances, right upper quadrant ultrasound (and, less often, spiral computed tomography [CT] scan) is the first imaging test obtained in patients with suspected biliary obstruction of unknown etiology. (See "Approach to the patient with abnormal liver tests", section on 'Elevated alkaline phosphatase' and "High alkaline phosphatase in adults" .)

● Predominant aminotransferase elevation – The broad differential diagnosis includes viral hepatitis, hepatotoxicity from drugs or toxins, alcoholic liver disease, ischemic hepatitis, and malignant infiltration. (See "Approach to the patient with abnormal liver tests", section on 'Elevated serum aminotransferases' and "High alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in adults" .)

Screen for chronic alcohol use.

Obtain:

• Serologic tests for chronic hepatitis B and C infection (see "Hepatitis B virus: Screening and diagnosis in adults", section on 'Chronic HBV infection' and "Screening and diagnosis of chronic hepatitis C virus infection", section on 'Additional evaluation' )

• Serum iron, total iron binding capacity, ferritin (see "Approach to the patient with suspected iron overload", section on 'Sequence and interpretation of testing' )

• Lipid profile

• Hemoglobin A1C

• Right upper quadrant ultrasound

In a patient with a history of significant alcohol consumption, we generally do not obtain additional testing if the above tests are negative. If the initial evaluation fails to identify a likely source of the aminotransferase elevation, sequential testing is based upon the history and physical examination. (See "Approach to the patient with abnormal liver tests", section on 'Evaluation of mildly or moderately elevated aminotransferases' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The reference range for total bilirubin is approximately 0.2 to 1.2 mg/dL (3.4 to 20.5 micromol/L), for direct bilirubin approximately 0.1 to 0.4 mg/dL (1.7 to 6.8 micromol/L), and for indirect bilirubin approximately 0.1 to 0.8 mg/dL (1.7 to 13.6 micromol/L), but the reference ranges may vary based on age, sex, and race. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
