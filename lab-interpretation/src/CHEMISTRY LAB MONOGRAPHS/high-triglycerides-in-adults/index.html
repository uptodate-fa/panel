ALGORITHM  — 

( algorithm 1 )

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Hypertriglyceridemia most often occurs in the setting of other diseases or treatments (eg, obesity, alcoholism, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, nephrotic syndrome, hypothyroidism, oral estrogen therapy). Less commonly, it is due to an inherited disorder (eg, familial chylomicronemia, familial hypertriglyceridemia, familial combined hyperlipidemia). Genetic and acquired disorders often coexist (see "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Approach to evaluation" ):

● Obtain fasting triglyceride level – The risk of pancreatitis increases progressively with TG levels over 500 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L), with marked increase in risk in patients with prior, recent acute pancreatitis. Reduce serum triglycerides in patients with persistent fasting triglyceride levels ≥880 mg/dL (10 mmol/L) or >500 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) in a patient with a history of pancreatitis (see "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Management", section on 'General measures' and "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Management", section on 'Severe hypertriglyceridemia' ):

• Restrict dietary fats (patients with values above 1000 mg/dL [11.3 mmol/L] may need extreme dietary restriction)

• Avoid alcohol intake

• Initiate drug therapy to lower triglycerides to <500 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L)

● Identify the cause - For all patients with persistently elevated unexplained fasting triglycerides, attempt to identify a secondary cause. (See "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Approach to evaluation" .)

Obtain:

• A1C or fasting glucose

• Serum creatinine

• Thyroid-stimulating hormone

• Urinalysis (albumin/protein)

If no secondary cause is evident, the patient likely has a hereditary cause. In most cases, genetic testing is not performed because it does not influence management. Genetic testing is generally reserved for patients with suspected familial chylomicronemia syndrome, a rare form of severe hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides >880 mg/dL [10 mmol/L] or >1000 mg/dL [11.3 mmol/L]) that may require more aggressive intervention and screening of other family members. (See "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Approach to evaluation", section on 'Genetic factors' and "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Approach to evaluation" .)

First-degree relatives of patients with triglyceride levels above 500 mg/dL (5.7 mmol/L) and no clear cause for an acquired disorder (eg, absence of obesity, diabetes, hypothyroidism) should be screened with a fasting triglyceride level, as familial causes are common. (See "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Approach to evaluation" .)

● Assess risk for ASCVD - Elevated triglyceride levels are independently associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease events, although a causal role has not been established. Patients with hypertriglyceridemia should undergo an assessment of their atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk. (See "Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk assessment for primary prevention in adults" .)

When triglycerides are >150 mg/dL (>1.7 mmol/L), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) underrepresents the cardiovascular risk because triglycerides are exchanged for cholesterol esters in the low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particle. This results in small, dense cholesterol-depleted LDL particles that may not be captured by LDL-C measurement. Non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C), apolipoprotein B concentrations, and LDL particle numbers are better measures than LDL-C of the excess concentrations of atherogenic lipoproteins in such patients.

Measure:

• Apolipoprotein B

• Non-HDL-C

All patients with hypertriglyceridemia should participate in lifestyle interventions, such as weight loss in patients with obesity, aerobic exercise, and avoidance of concentrated sugars and medications that raise serum triglyceride levels. Further management is based on LDL-C and cardiovascular disease risk. (See "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Management", section on 'General measures' and "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Management", section on 'Moderate hypertriglyceridemia' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

A normal triglyceride level is less than 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L). The normal reference range for triglycerides can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
