INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Procalcitonin is a serum biomarker that helps distinguish bacterial infection from other causes of inflammation, particularly viral infections. Procalcitonin may be detected in the serum within four hours of an inciting bacterial infection. Procalcitonin peaks at 12 to 48 hours and rapidly declines with resolution of the inflammatory process (see "Procalcitonin use in lower respiratory tract infections", section on 'Clinical use' ):

● Because procalcitonin can help distinguish bacterial from viral infections, procalcitonin use has been extensively studied to help guide antibiotic decision-making in patients with lower respiratory tract infections.

● Evidence supporting its use is strongest for helping guide antibiotic discontinuation in clinically stable patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). It is similarly used in patients with sepsis. (See "Procalcitonin use in lower respiratory tract infections", section on 'Community-acquired pneumonia in hospitalized patients' and "Evaluation and management of suspected sepsis and septic shock in adults", section on 'Initial investigations' .)

● In general, procalcitonin is not used to help determine initiation of antibiotics for CAP or guide initial antibiotic decision-making for most other disorders because it has not been clearly shown to add value over clinical judgment alone.

● Procalcitonin should not override clinical judgment because assay sensitivity and specificity are imperfect. Results vary based on the specific assay, type of bacterial infection, and underlying patient comorbidities (eg, renal disease, immunosuppression, surgery/trauma).

Procalcitonin is typically measured at the time of diagnosis and repeated every one to two days in hospitalized patients, depending on severity of illness. A single value is less helpful than serial values. Procalcitonin must always be interpreted in conjunction with other clinical findings, as many conditions can elevate procalcitonin levels in the absence of bacterial infection (eg, pancreatitis, surgery, severe trauma) ( table 1 ).

Clinically stable community-acquired pneumonia  —  In clinically stable patients with known or suspected bacterial CAP, declining procalcitonin levels, along with clinical judgment, may help guide the timing of antibiotic discontinuation. Decisions to stop antibiotics based on procalcitonin thresholds presume that the patient is stable and that a bacterial infection that requires a longer course of therapy (eg, metastatic infection, empyema, Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia) was not identified. Optimal procalcitonin thresholds for guiding antibiotic therapy have not been precisely determined. (See "Procalcitonin use in lower respiratory tract infections", section on 'Guiding antibiotic therapy' .)

Procalcitonin declining  —  Procalcitonin levels that are declining and <0.25 ng/mL may be used as a threshold for discontinuing antibiotics in clinically stable patients. Some experts use a lower threshold, typically 0.1 ng/mL. Others take into account the rate of decline and discontinue antibiotics when the procalcitonin level has decreased by ≥80 percent from its peak. The latter approach is most often used when initial values are >5 ng/mL and the time period it would take to decline to <0.25 ng/mL would exceed a reasonable duration of antibiotics.

If the decline in procalcitonin reaches one of these criteria, consider stopping antibiotics in clinically stable patients in the following settings:

● Uncertain initial diagnosis of CAP and an alternative diagnosis has been made (eg, heart failure)

● Probable or proven viral CAP (ongoing bacterial infection is unlikely in the absence of definitive microbiology results)

● Known or suspected bacterial CAP in patients who have improved clinically and have received several days of appropriate antibiotic therapy

The decision should be reassessed if clinical deterioration occurs after stopping antibiotics.

If the decline in procalcitonin does not meet any of these criteria, we generally continue antibiotic therapy for patients with known or suspected bacterial CAP for at least the standard recommendation treatment duration. Declining procalcitonin levels suggest response to antibiotic therapy.

Procalcitonin stable or rising  —  For patients with persistently elevated or rising procalcitonin levels, assess for other causes of elevated procalcitonin, consider whether to continue or change antibiotic regimens based on the individual patient characteristics, and consider obtaining specialty consultation for guidance. In patients who are not clinically improving, persistently high or rising procalcitonin levels suggest poorer prognosis and/or uncontrolled infection.

Critically ill community-acquired pneumonia  —  In critically ill patients with presumed bacterial CAP, the thresholds used to guide antibiotic discontinuation differ compared with stable patients (eg, stopping antibiotics when procalcitonin levels fall below 0.50 ng/mL [or decrease by ≥80 percent from peak if initial value >5 ng/mL]). The threshold is higher in critically illness because baseline procalcitonin levels tend to be higher in critical illness. Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting test results. Obtain specialty consultation (eg, expertise in infectious disease and/or critical care). (See "Procalcitonin use in lower respiratory tract infections", section on 'Critically ill patients' .)

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)  —  The utility of procalcitonin in patients with COVID-19 is uncertain. An elevated value may be due to generic systemic inflammation and is not specific for secondary bacterial infection. On the other hand, when clinical suspicion for bacterial infection is low, a low procalcitonin value may support the decision to withhold or discontinue antibiotics. (See "Procalcitonin use in lower respiratory tract infections", section on 'Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

There are no well-defined reference thresholds based on age and gender for procalcitonin. Levels vary depending upon the assay used, kidney disease, and the presence and intensity of an inflammatory process. In the absence of definitive microbiology results, a persistently negative procalcitonin level makes infection with a bacterial pathogen unlikely.

When interpreting serial procalcitonin results, take into account intraindividual biologic and analytic assay variation. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
