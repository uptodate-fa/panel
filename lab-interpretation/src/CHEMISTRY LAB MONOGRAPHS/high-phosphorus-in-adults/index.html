ALGORITHM — 

(algorithm 1)

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

Identify patients with acute hyperphosphatemia and/or worrisome clinical findings, including any of the following:

●Hemodynamic instability, oliguria, acute kidney injury

●Symptomatic hypocalcemia (eg, carpopedal spasm, tetany, seizures, decreased cardiac function, or prolonged QT interval) due to precipitation of calcium-phosphate in tissues (see "Clinical manifestations of hypocalcemia", section on 'Acute manifestations')

●Marked tissue breakdown (eg, acute leukemia, tumor lysis syndrome, rhabdomyolysis) (see "Acute myeloid leukemia: Overview of complications", section on 'Hyperphosphatemia' and "Tumor lysis syndrome: Pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, definition, etiology and risk factors", section on 'Hyperphosphatemia' and "Rhabdomyolysis: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis")

●Absorption of large amounts of phosphate (eg, use of phosphate-containing laxatives and enemas), administration of excess phosphate during total parenteral nutrition (TPN) (see "Acute phosphate nephropathy", section on 'Clinical presentation and prognosis' and "Nutrition support in critically ill adult patients: Parenteral nutrition")

Provide hemodynamic support, if indicated. Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

Review:

●Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

●Electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose, calcium, magnesium

Obtain:

●Repeat phosphorus, calcium

●Electrocardiogram

Additional testing in selected patients to determine cause (table 1) may include:

●Creatine kinase (rhabdomyolysis, hyperthermia)

●Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), uric acid (tumor lysis syndrome)

●Serum lactate level, beta-hydroxybutyrate (decreased perfusion and/or ketoacidosis)

Obtain nephrology consultation to manage patients with acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, or patients in whom hemodialysis may be needed (especially in patients with hyperphosphatemia and symptomatic hypocalcemia or worsening acute renal injury). (See "Overview of the causes and treatment of hyperphosphatemia".)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Patients not requiring immediate action still require a timely evaluation to determine the cause of hyperphosphatemia. The etiology can usually be obtained from the history (eg, acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, hypoparathyroidism, medication-related) (table 1). A diet high in phosphate content may cause or exacerbate hyperphosphatemia in patients with mild impairment of renal phosphorus excretion, such as mild chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] >30 mL/min/1.73 m2). (See "Management of hyperphosphatemia in adults with chronic kidney disease", section on 'Dietary phosphorus restriction in all patients'.)

In patients with unexplained hyperphosphatemia,

●Repeat phosphorus level

●Review electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose, calcium

●Review medications

●Review dietary history

Transient hyperphosphatemia — Transient hyperphosphatemia usually resolves within 6 to 12 hours if renal function is intact. No further evaluation of hyperphosphatemia is required if renal function is normal, hyperphosphatemia has resolved, and there are no worrisome clinical findings.

Persistent hyperphosphatemia

GFR relatively preserved — If hyperphosphatemia is persistent, the serum calcium level can identify the two most common causes (see "Diagnostic approach to hypercalcemia" and "Hypoparathyroidism", section on 'Diagnosis and evaluation'):

●If calcium is high, measure 25-hydroxyvitamin D to assess for vitamin D toxicity

●If calcium is low, measure parathyroid hormone (PTH) to assess for hypoparathyroidism

In the absence of vitamin D toxicity or hypoparathyroidism, persistent hyperphosphatemia with relatively preserved glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is rare (table 1).

GFR reduced — The tendency toward phosphate retention develops early in chronic kidney disease due to the reduction in the filtered phosphate load. Overt hyperphosphatemia may develop when the eGFR falls below 25 to 40 mL/minute/1.73 m2, particularly with excessive dietary intake. (See "Management of hyperphosphatemia in adults with chronic kidney disease" and "Management of secondary hyperparathyroidism in adult patients with nondialysis chronic kidney disease".)

●Evaluate dietary sources of exogenous phosphate

●Obtain nephrology consultation to address reversible causes of kidney injury, assist in the management of chronic hyperphosphatemia, and to anticipate need for dialysis

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for phosphorus is approximately 3.0 to 4.5 mg/dL (0.97 to 1.45 mmol/L) but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
