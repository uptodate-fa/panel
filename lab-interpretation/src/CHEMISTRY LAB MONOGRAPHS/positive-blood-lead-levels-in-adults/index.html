INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Blood lead levels (BLLs) may reflect recent exposure to exogenous lead sources or prior exposure due to release of lead stored in bone and soft tissue. The majority of elevated BLLs in adults are a result of workplace respiratory tract exposures. Review medical/environmental history to identify potential sources of lead exposure, including past history of lead exposure in previous jobs, ingestion of folk remedies, or childhood lead poisoning (table 1). (See "Lead exposure, toxicity, and poisoning in adults: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Lead sources and absorption'.)

All individuals who work in occupations and/or environments associated with possible lead exposure should have regular screening with BLLs. Venous blood sampling is preferable to earlobe capillary blood sampling. Surface skin contamination can result in false elevations in capillary blood-sampled lead levels, even after the area is cleaned with an alcohol wipe. BLL thresholds are used to determine whether removal from lead exposure is warranted, how frequently to repeat BLL, when to return to work, and for other treatment decisions.

Lead poisoning is a clinical diagnosis. In the United States, lead poisoning in nonpregnant adults is defined as a BLL ≥10 mcg/dL accompanied by symptoms or signs of lead exposure, whereas lead toxicity is defined as BLL >10 mcg/dL. In pregnant women, lead exposure with even slight BLL elevations (<10 mcg/dL) may have toxic effects on the developing fetus, and therefore, thresholds for intervention should be modified for pregnant women or those contemplating pregnancy.

When lead poisoning is diagnosed or strongly suspected, obtain consultation with a medical lead expert/toxicologist for treatment recommendations. In addition, clinicians may refer a patient with BLL ≥20 mcg/dL to an occupational/ environmental medicine clinician with experience in lead toxicity to assist in the diagnosis of lead poisoning and ongoing management.

The Association of Occupational and Environmental Clinics (AOEC) website includes information about finding board-certified occupational medicine clinicians who may assist with:

●Diagnosis of lead poisoning

●Decisions regarding chelation therapy

●Arrangement of environmental site evaluations

●Preventive measures/education to reduce lead exposure and poisoning

●Issues related to worker compensation

Blood lead level ≥40 mcg/dL — Patients with BLL ≥40 mcg/dL require prompt medical evaluation and consultation with a clinician who has expertise in the management of lead poisoning. All patients with elevations to this degree should be removed from the source of lead exposure.

Assess for symptoms that may be seen with acute lead poisoning (eg, abdominal pain "lead colic," headache, muscle aches, fatigue, and irritability) and for signs that may be seen with chronic excess lead burden (eg, high blood pressure, neurocognitive deficits, encephalopathy, and anemia). There is a general correlation between health effects and BLLs. However, manifestations of lead poisoning vary from individual to individual and are often nonspecific. Lead encephalopathy can occur with BLL >100 mcg/dL (more typically >150 mcg/dL), and manifestations include seizures, obtundation, confusion, and papilledema.

In patients with suspected lead poisoning, obtain or review:

●Previous BLL, if available

●Complete blood count (CBC) with differential

●Peripheral blood smear in patients with anemia and/or BLLs >80 mcg/dL (≥3.86 micromol/L)

●Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine

●Urinalysis

●Zinc protoporphyrin (ZPP) or free erythrocyte protoporphyrin (FEP)

●Additional symptom-driven testing as appropriate, such as computed tomography of the head in patients with encephalopathy or prominent cognitive issues

Interventions for specific BLLs are approximations, and the approach should be individualized. Patients with BLL ≥80 mcg/dL (or BLL 50 to <80 mcg/dL with symptoms of acute lead poisoning) may benefit from urgent chelation therapy. If chelation therapy is recommended, BLLs should be monitored during treatment and for at least six months after completion. Before returning to work, conditions that caused lead poisoning must be remediated to avoid re-exposure. Exposure controls should be re-examined and improved.

Blood lead level 30 to 39 mcg/dL — Individuals with BLL 30 to 39 mcg/dL should be removed from working with or around lead (eg, transfer to another job in a lead-free area). Repeat BLL testing in one month.

Blood lead level 20 to 29 mcg/dL — Individuals with BLL 20 to 29 mcg/dL should be closely monitored. Repeat BLL testing in one month. If BLL is persistently ≥20 mcg/dL despite efforts to reduce lead exposure, the patient should be removed from working in and around lead, followed by repeat BLL testing in one month.

Blood lead level 10 to 19 mcg/dL — Individuals with BLL 10 to 19 mcg/dL should have repeat BLL testing, the timing of which depends upon the clinical setting:

●Repeat a second BLL in three months if the first BLL is 10 to 19 mcg/dL.

●Continue monthly BLL monitoring in individuals who had an initial BLL ≥20 mcg/dL. Individuals may return to work after two BLLs are ≤15 mcg/dL at least one month apart.

●When BLL is reduced to <10 mcg/dL, repeat BLL testing annually as long as lead exposure continues.

Blood lead level 5 to 9 mcg/dL — Individuals with BLL 5 to 9 mcg/dL should have continued monitoring to ensure that this level is sustained. Repeat BLL annually if exposure continues.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

In the United States, the reference range is <5 mcg/dL (0.24 micromol/L) in adults. However, there is no apparent lower threshold for safe exposure. Ideally, for individuals who work in and around lead, maintain BLL <10 mcg/dL (0.48 micromol/L) and <5 mcg/dL for women who might become pregnant. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon levels appropriate to adult toxicity, rather than childhood toxicity (which are sometimes the ranges of concern reported by testing laboratories).
