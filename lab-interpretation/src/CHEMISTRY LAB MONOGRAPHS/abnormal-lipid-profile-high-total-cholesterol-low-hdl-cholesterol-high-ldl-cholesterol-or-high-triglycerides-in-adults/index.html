INITIAL EVALUATION — 

A full lipid profile (total cholesterol, triglycerides, high density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], and calculated [or directly measured] low density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]) is preferred in a patient with known or suspected hypertriglyceridemia (eg, overweight; diabetes; taking medications that are known to raise triglycerides, such as steroids).

A full lipid profile is often obtained after a fast, since serum triglyceride levels vary with food intake and determination of the calculated LDL-C includes triglyceride values. However, many laboratories can measure LDL-C directly, which allows patients to undergo a nonfasting lipid profile and still have an accurate assessment of LDL-C. (See "Screening for lipid disorders in adults", section on 'Choice of tests'.)

Elevated LDL-C — Recommended lifestyle behaviors for all patients at increased cardiovascular risk include regular exercise, smoking cessation, attainment of a target body weight, and a healthy diet. (See "Overview of primary prevention of cardiovascular disease in adults", section on 'Identifying and addressing key risk factors'.)

For the purpose of primary prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD), only LDL-C lowering, among all lipid components, has been shown to be of clinical benefit. (See "Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol-lowering therapy in the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease", section on 'Indications for statin therapy'.)

LDL-C >190 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L) — An LDL-C >190 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L) raises suspicion for familial hypercholesterolemia (table 1). In all males and most females, initiate pharmacologic therapy for an LDL-C >190 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L), independent of calculated cardiovascular risk. For young women of childbearing age who are considering having children in the near term, the treatment decision might be postponed depending on the severity of LDL-C elevation, family history, and evidence for any pre-existing atherosclerotic vascular disease. Statins are contraindicated in pregnant women (see "Familial hypercholesterolemia in adults: Overview" and "Management of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) in the secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease", section on 'Monitoring therapy' and "Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol-lowering therapy in the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease"):

●Screen first-degree relatives for familial hypercholesterolemia

●Repeat a lipid profile after six weeks of initiation or change in therapy and then every 12 months

LDL-C ≤190 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L) — Management is based on the patient's CVD risk. For individuals 30 to 79 years of age, calculate the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease using a validated risk calculator. Multiple risk calculators are available and vary depending on locale and patient-specific race and ethnic groups. A sample calculator for populations in the United States is presented (calculator 1). For individuals 20 to 29 years or >79 years, we perform an informal risk assessment. We do not routinely use a risk calculator to perform quantitative risk estimates in these patients. (See "Cardiovascular disease risk assessment for primary prevention: Risk calculators" and "Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk assessment for primary prevention in adults", section on 'ASCVD risk assessment'.)

A threshold for intervention is often set at a 10-year risk >7.5 to 10 percent, or lifetime risk >39 percent. (See "Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk assessment for primary prevention in adults", section on 'Additional risk stratification and management'.)

For patients not requiring immediate intervention:

●Assess CVD risk and repeat measurements every five years

●In patients found to be at increased CVD risk based on their lipid measurements, or who are near a threshold for treatment, repeat measurement every three years

●For patients who have had more than one prior normal screening, stop screening at age 65

For patients requiring intervention:

●Repeat a lipid profile after six weeks of therapy or change in therapy and then every 12 months

Isolated low HDL-C — Low serum HDL-C often occurs in association with hypertriglyceridemia and can also be found in patients with elevated total serum cholesterol and low density lipoprotein (LDL) particles, but it can occur as the sole lipid abnormality. Most causes of isolated low HDL-C are inherited. (See "HDL cholesterol: Clinical aspects of abnormal values", section on 'Inherited causes'.)

Other causes include:

●Drugs (beta blockers, benzodiazepines, androgens)

●Acute infection

●Inflammation

●Gammopathies

Drugs that may be the cause of a low HDL-C should not be stopped if they are important for treatment of medical conditions.

Although evidence clearly indicates an inverse relationship between HDL-C and cardiovascular event risk, low levels of HDL-C have not been established as causative for atherosclerosis, and interventions to raise HDL-C, as the only therapeutic target, have not demonstrated consistent cardiovascular benefit. (See "HDL cholesterol: Clinical aspects of abnormal values", section on 'Our approach to patients with low HDL cholesterol'.)

Isolated elevated triglycerides — The evaluation of hypertriglyceridemia is reviewed in a separate Lab Interpretation monograph. (See "High triglycerides in adults" and "Hypertriglyceridemia in adults: Approach to evaluation".)

When triglycerides are >150 mg/dL (>1.7 mmol/L), LDL-C underrepresents the cardiovascular risk because triglycerides are exchanged for cholesterol esters in the LDL particle. This results in discordance between LDL particles and LDL-C. Multiple studies have shown that apolipoprotein B or LDL particle number is more strongly predictive of cardiovascular risk. Therefore, apolipoprotein B is measured as a secondary target of therapy.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

Lipid levels can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory (table 2):

●Total cholesterol

•<200 mg/dL (5.2 mmol/L) – Desirable

•200 to 239 mg/dL (5.2 to 6.1 mmol/L) – Borderline high

•>239 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L) – High

●LDL-C

•<100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) – Optimal

•100 to 129 mg/dL (2.6 to 3.3 mmol/L) – Near optimal

•130 to 159 mg/dL (3.4 to 4.1 mmol/L) – Borderline high

•160 to 189 mg/dL (4.1 to 4.9 mmol/L) – High

•>189 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L) – Very high

●HDL-C

A low level is commonly defined:

•Females – <50 mg/dL (1.30 mmol/L)

•Males – <40 mg/dL (1.03 mmol/L)

●Triglycerides – A normal triglyceride level is <150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L).

Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
