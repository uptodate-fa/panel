EVALUATION — 

Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) and cardiac troponin T (cTnT) are structural proteins in the heart. Following myocardial injury from any cause, these cardiac biomarkers are released into the circulation. Institutions vary on whether they report cTnI or cTnT. For most clinical purposes, it appears that cTnT and cTnI have equal utility. (See "Troponin testing: Clinical use" and "Cardiac troponins in patients with kidney disease", section on 'Evaluation of suspected myocardial infarction'.)

Measurement of cTnI and cTnT can be performed using high-sensitivity or sensitive (older) assays. High-sensitivity troponin (hs-cTn) assays (if available) are preferred to sensitive troponin assays because they can quantify biochemical evidence of myocardial injury at lower concentrations. In addition, hs-cTn assays can detect small changes (rise and/or fall) substantially sooner than sensitive cardiac troponin (cTn) assays, where detection may take hours after presentation of myocardial infarction (MI).

Although troponin assays are not interchangeable, the interpretation of elevated findings and the subsequent approach are the same. Although higher concentrations are more likely to be due to acute MI, there is no single threshold troponin value that reliably discriminates acute MI from any of the other causes of elevated troponin. As is true for most other diagnostic tests, the positive and negative predictive values of a given troponin test result for the diagnosis of acute MI are influenced by the prevalence of the disease in the population in which the test is being used.

Initial elevated troponin — Prompt diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is essential for early initiation of treatment. By the time the troponin results become available, the patient should have already been evaluated for ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), hemodynamic instability, and other potentially life-threatening causes of myocardial injury (eg, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, sepsis). A very high troponin concentration (eg, hs-cTn >1000 ng/L) is indicative of severe myocardial injury (see "Overview of the acute management of ST-elevation myocardial infarction" and "Overview of the nonacute management of ST-elevation myocardial infarction"):

●Review testing obtained at the time of initial cTn measurement, including:

•Electrocardiogram (ECG)

•Complete blood count (CBC) with platelet count

•Serum electrolytes, magnesium, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose

•Chest imaging

●Obtain (review):

•Serial ECGs

•Second cTn measurement using the same assay, the timing of which depends on the assay (approximately one to three hours if using a high-sensitivity assay and three to six hours if using a sensitive assay)

Pattern of change — The pattern of change in the troponin values helps differentiate ACS from other causes of elevated troponin (table 1). cTn concentrations usually begin to rise two to three hours after the onset of acute MI, but this may vary depending on the underlying mechanism of the MI and the analytical sensitivity of the assay. Other causes of acute myocardial injury (eg, acute myocarditis and stress [takotsubo] cardiomyopathy) can also have highly dynamic (ie, rising and falling) patterns. There is no single threshold troponin value that reliably discriminates any of these causes. The use of serial values to assess for a dynamic rise or fall in concentration, the absolute concentration, and the clinical syndrome should all be integrated by the clinician to make an assessment regarding the cause of myocardial injury.

Acute rise and/or fall pattern — An acute rise, a rise and fall, or a fall from an initial elevated troponin level, along with supportive evidence (eg, typical symptoms, suggestive ECG changes), establishes the presence of acute myocardial injury and increases the probability of the diagnosis of coronary ischemia. However, acute myocarditis and stress (takotsubo) cardiomyopathy can mimic the clinical presentation and troponin results seen in acute MI. Obtain cardiology consultation to guide further evaluation and management. (See "Troponin testing: Clinical use", section on 'Diagnosis of acute MI'.)

Ambiguous pattern — An ambiguous pattern is defined as troponin values that exceed the 99th percentile upper reference limits, but do not meet criteria for acute MI (ie, insignificant change from prior values). For patients who are subsequently diagnosed with acute MI, an ambiguous pattern may occur if they present >12 hours after the onset of symptoms. (See "Evaluation for suspected non-ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndromes", section on 'Late presentation after symptom onset'.)

For patients with an ambiguous pattern, obtain a third cTn measurement. If the hs-cTn assay is used, repeat the troponin (using the same assay) in one to three hours. If a sensitive cTn assay is used, repeat the troponin (using the same assay) in six hours:

●An acute rise, a rise and fall, or a falling troponin establishes the presence of acute myocardial injury, which may be due to ischemic heart disease. (See 'Acute rise and/or fall pattern' above.)

●An ambiguous pattern without clinical suspicion for ischemia supports chronic elevation due to nonischemic myocardial injury. Evaluate the patient for other potential underlying causes (table 1), including:

•Comorbidities – renal failure, sepsis, acute respiratory failure, stroke (see "Cardiac troponins in patients with kidney disease")

•Cardiomyopathy, heart failure, pre-existing structural heart disease, left ventricular hypertrophy

Very rarely, cTn may be chronically elevated due to heterophilic or troponin autoantibodies affecting cTnI or skeletal muscle disease affecting cTnT. If suspected, obtain an alternative cTn assay, if available. (See "Elevated cardiac troponin concentration in the absence of an acute coronary syndrome", section on 'Non-MI causes of an elevated troponin' and "Troponin testing: Analytical considerations", section on 'Assay false positives and false negatives'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference cutoff value for hs-cTn is ≤99th percentile of a cardiac disease-free reference population. For most sensitive cTn assays, the normal reference range is undetectable troponin. hs-cTn values are reported in ng/L, which is 1000 times larger than sensitive troponin tests (which are reported in ng/mL). Clinical decision pathways for the emergency evaluation of chest pain incorporate values different than the reference cutoff in order to identify or exclude MI with high probability.

When interpreting serial cTn results, intraindividual biologic (age, sex, kidney function) and analytic assay variation must also be taken into account, as these can influence baseline troponin levels, and there is no simple algorithm that takes each into account. Sex-specific 99th percentile values are available for most hs-cTn assays. High circulating levels of biotin can interfere with cTn assays (reports suggest that cTn values are decreased by biotin). Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory and the clinical context in which the test was applied.
