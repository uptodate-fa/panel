INITIAL EVALUATION — 

For cardiovascular risk assessment in most patients, only the total cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) are needed, and they can be obtained in the fasting or nonfasting state. However, we typically screen with a full fasting lipid profile because costs are generally similar, and triglycerides can add useful information. (See "Screening for lipid disorders in adults", section on 'Choice of tests'.)

In addition, obtain a fasting lipid profile (total cholesterol, HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], triglycerides) if the patient has any of the following:

●Total cholesterol >250 mg/dL (>6.5 mmol/L) – A total cholesterol greater than 250 mg/dL may be due to high triglycerides.

●HDL-C <40 mg/dL (<1 mmol/L) – Low HDL-C is often associated with hypertriglyceridemia.

●History of hypertriglyceridemia or medications known to elevate triglycerides (eg, estrogens, glucocorticoids).

●Diabetes mellitus.

●Family history of genetic hyperlipidemia.

Calculate cardiovascular risk

●For individuals 30 to 79 years of age, use a cardiovascular risk calculator to determine the potential 10-year cardiovascular risk. For individuals 30 to 59 years of age, lifetime (rather than 10-year) risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) should be emphasized.

Multiple risk calculators are available and vary depending on locale and patient-specific race and ethnic groups. A sample calculator for populations in the United States is presented (calculator 1). (See "Cardiovascular disease risk assessment for primary prevention: Risk calculators" and "Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk assessment for primary prevention in adults", section on 'ASCVD risk assessment'.)

●For individuals 20 to 29 years or >79 years, we perform an informal risk assessment. We do not routinely use a risk calculator to perform quantitative risk estimates in these patients.

Management is based on the patient's CVD risk. For the purpose of primary prevention of CVD, only LDL-C lowering, among all lipid components, has been shown to be of clinical benefit. While HDL-C is associated with CVD risk, no causal role has been established for HDL and therefore it is not a target to which therapies are directed.

Recommended behaviors for all patients at increased cardiovascular risk include regular exercise, smoking cessation, attainment of a target body weight, and a healthy diet. (See "Overview of primary prevention of cardiovascular disease in adults", section on 'Identifying and addressing key risk factors'.)

For patients not requiring immediate intervention:

●Assess CVD risk and repeat measurements every five years.

●In patients found to be at increased CVD risk based on their lipid measurements, or who are near a threshold for treatment, repeat measurement every three years.

●For patients who have had more than one prior normal screening, stop screening at age 65.

For patients requiring intervention:

●Repeat a lipid profile after six weeks of therapy or change in therapy and then every 12 months.

(See "Screening for lipid disorders in adults", section on 'Repeat screening' and "Screening for lipid disorders in adults", section on 'Stopping screening' and "Management of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) in the secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease", section on 'Monitoring therapy'.)

Isolated low HDL-C — Low serum HDL-C most often occurs in association with hypertriglyceridemia or elevated total serum cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particles but can occur as the sole lipid abnormality. Most causes of isolated low HDL-C are inherited. (See "HDL cholesterol: Clinical aspects of abnormal values", section on 'Inherited causes'.)

Other causes include:

●Drugs (beta blockers, benzodiazepines, androgens, ritonavir)

●Acute infection

●Inflammation

●Gammopathies

Drugs that may be the cause of a low HDL-C should not be stopped if they are important for treatment of medical conditions.

Although evidence clearly indicates an inverse relationship between HDL-C and cardiovascular event risk, low levels of HDL-C have not been established as causative for atherosclerosis, and interventions to raise HDL-C, as the only therapeutic target, have not demonstrated consistent cardiovascular benefit. (See "HDL cholesterol: Clinical aspects of abnormal values", section on 'Our approach to patients with low HDL cholesterol'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

Lipid levels can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory:

●Total cholesterol

•<200 mg/dL (5.2 mmol/L): desirable

•200 to 239 mg/dL (5.2 to 6.1 mmol/L): borderline high

•>239 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L): high

●HDL-C

A low level is commonly defined:

•Females: <50 mg/dL (1.30 mmol/L)

•Males: <40 mg/dL (1.03 mmol/L)

Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
