INITIAL EVALUATION — 

The urea cycle converts nitrogen from peripheral muscle and dietary protein into urea, primarily in the liver. The serum urea concentration, expressed as the blood urea nitrogen (BUN), varies inversely with the glomerular filtration rate (GFR), increasing as GFR falls. However, the BUN has limited value as an index of GFR because rates of urea production and reabsorption are variable and independent of GFR. The rate of urea production increases with a high-protein diet and with enhanced tissue breakdown due to hemorrhage, trauma, or glucocorticoid therapy; it decreases with a low-protein diet or liver disease. (See "Assessment of kidney function".)

The serum creatinine (SCr) is a more reliable estimate of GFR (eGFR) than the BUN because creatinine is produced at a relatively constant rate by skeletal muscle and is not reabsorbed by the renal tubules. With stable kidney function, as seen in patients with normal kidney function or chronic kidney disease, a rise in SCr almost always represents a reduction in GFR (see "High serum creatinine in adults"):

●Review SCr and other components of the metabolic profile in the context of clinical presentation

●Review prior BUN and SCr values

●Review (obtain) urinalysis with microscopy in patients with established or suspected kidney disease

BUN/SCr ratio — The pattern of BUN elevation relative to SCr may be helpful in some patients with renal insufficiency. In stable ambulatory patients with adequate dietary protein intake and normal GFR, the ratio of BUN to creatinine (measured in mg/dL) is approximately 10:1 to 20:1. A high BUN/SCr ratio (≥20) may be used as one parameter (in addition to clinical features and examination of the urine) to distinguish prerenal disease from other causes of renal insufficiency (eg, acute tubular necrosis [ATN]).

However, BUN/SCr ratios are only approximate and may be misleading in certain settings (eg, prerenal disease may not result in a high BUN/SCr ratio if dietary protein intake is very low, whereas intrinsic renal disease may result in a high BUN/SCr ratio in an older patient with decreased creatinine production due to low muscle mass). The BUN/SCr ratio must be interpreted in view of these limitations.

The thresholds outlined in the approach below are only approximate. In addition, these ratio thresholds do not apply in countries where values for serum urea (rather than BUN) are reported in mmol/L and serum creatinine is reported either in mcmol/L or mmol/L. If serum urea and creatinine are both reported in mmol/L, a BUN to creatinine ratio of 10:1 (in mg/dL) would be a urea to creatinine ratio of approximately 40:1 (and if creatinine is reported in mcmol/L, the comparable ratio would be 0.04:1). For the remainder of this discussion, we will refer to the ratio of BUN to creatinine reported in mg/dL.

Ratio ≥20:1

●Normal or near normal SCr (normal GFR) – A high BUN/SCr ratio (≥20:1) may be caused by conditions that increase urea production or that increase urea reabsorption by the renal tubules with minimal change in SCr; these conditions are usually apparent at the time of clinical assessment.

Conditions that increase urea production include:

•High dietary protein intake, total parenteral nutrition

•Absorption of blood due to slow gastrointestinal (GI) hemorrhage, especially from the upper GI tract

•Enhanced tissue breakdown (eg, infection, trauma, chemotherapy, and glucocorticoid therapy)

Conditions that increase urea reabsorption by the renal tubules include:

•Mild intravascular volume depletion

•Moderately severe heart failure

A high BUN and normal SCr can also be seen in often critically ill patients when there is decreased production of creatinine due to low muscle mass. In this setting, a "normal" SCr may lead to the false conclusion that the GFR is normal. A high BUN/creatinine ratio in the absence of conditions that increase urea production or increase urea reabsorption can be a clue that the true GFR is low. If the cause is unclear from clinical assessment and initial testing, obtain specialty consultation.

●Increased SCr (reduced GFR) – An increase in both BUN and SCr, with a disproportionate increase in BUN relative to SCr, raising the BUN/SCr ratio to ≥20:1, may be caused by conditions associated with markedly reduced renal perfusion (eg, markedly reduced cardiac output, septic shock, hypovolemic shock) and/or urinary tract obstruction (which also increases urea reabsorption by the renal tubules). Severe GI hemorrhage is associated with decreased renal perfusion plus increased urea production.

If the cause is unclear from clinical assessment, initial testing, and examination of the urine, obtain specialty consultation. The interpretation of a high SCr is reviewed in more detail separately. (See "High serum creatinine in adults".)

Ratio <20:1

●Ratio between 10:1 and 20:1 – In most circumstances, the BUN and SCr concentration vary inversely with the GFR, increasing as the GFR falls.

In a patient without hypovolemia, a proportional increase in both BUN and SCr, such that the BUN/SCr ratio is normal (approximately between 10:1 and 20:1), can be caused by intrinsic acute or chronic kidney disease (eg, ATN, interstitial nephritis, glomerulonephritis).

In a patient with hypovolemia, the BUN/SCr ratio is commonly elevated; however, there are exceptions. A proportionately high BUN and SCr (with a normal ratio) may be seen with hypovolemia if dietary protein intake is very low or if creatinine production is increased (eg, rhabdomyolysis).

●Ratio ≤10:1 – An increase in both BUN and SCr, with a disproportionate increase in SCr relative to BUN (reducing the BUN/SCr ratio to approximately ≤10:1), may be caused by decreased urea production due to a low-protein diet or liver disease. Less commonly, it may be caused by increased creatinine production due to a high intake of cooked meat, creatine supplements, extremely large muscle mass, or rhabdomyolysis.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The reference range for serum or plasma BUN ranges from 8 to 20 mg/dL. The BUN is affected by GFR, renal perfusion, protein intake, and other factors. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported for that result and clinical evaluation of the patient.
