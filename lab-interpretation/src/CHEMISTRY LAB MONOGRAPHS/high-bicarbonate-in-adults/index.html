ALGORITHM  — 

High serum bicarbonate (or total carbon dioxide [CO 2 ]) can be caused by a primary metabolic alkalosis ( table 1 ), represent the compensatory response to a primary respiratory acidosis ( table 2 ), or develop with mixed acid-base disorders ( algorithm 1 ).

IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

In patients with risk factors for hypercapnia (eg, sedative use; history of obstructive sleep apnea; postanesthesia hypoxemia state; severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]; central nervous system, neuromuscular, and/or thoracic cage disorders), a high bicarbonate (HCO 3 ) may occur in response to respiratory acidosis.

Rapidly identify patients with risk factors for acute or chronic hypercapnia who present with any of the following findings of acute respiratory failure:

● New or worsening shortness of breath

● Change in mental status

● Hypersomnolence

● New or worsening hypoxemia

● Recent increase in supplemental oxygen

Review:

● Pulse oximetry, supplemental oxygen therapy (eg, delivery device, inspired oxygen concentration [FIO 2 ])

● Electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

Obtain:

● Arterial blood gas (ABG)

● Chest imaging

Provide initial bedside therapies and monitoring. Life-saving interventions (eg, ensuring a patent airway, oxygenation, and adequate ventilation) should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

Further testing and specialty consultation is based on the clinical setting and ABG analysis. In acute respiratory acidosis, the compensatory response to hypercapnia increases the serum HCO 3 concentration by approximately 1 mEq/L for every 10 mmHg (1.3 kPa) elevation in the partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO 2 ). (See "The evaluation and management of the nonventilated adult with acute hypercapnic respiratory failure" and "Simple and mixed acid-base disorders" .)

Once the patient is stabilized, the etiology can usually be determined or narrowed to a few possibilities. (See "The evaluation and management of the nonventilated adult with acute hypercapnic respiratory failure", section on 'Suspecting and diagnosing hypercapnia' and "The evaluation and management of the nonventilated adult with acute hypercapnic respiratory failure" .)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Patients without acute respiratory failure require less urgent, but timely, evaluation to determine the underlying disease that is causing or contributing to a persistently elevated HCO 3 . The underlying disease may be apparent from the history, physical examination, and routine laboratory tests, including serum electrolytes, BUN, and creatinine. The patient may have evidence of a primary metabolic alkalosis ( table 1 ), a stable chronic respiratory acidosis ( table 2 ), or a mixed acid-base disorder. In this setting, additional laboratory testing may not be required. If the etiology is not apparent, additional laboratory evaluation is based upon the suspected underlying acid-base disorder.

Metabolic alkalosis  —  The diagnosis of metabolic alkalosis can be made on the basis of a persistently elevated HCO 3 concentration if chronic respiratory acidosis can be ruled out on clinical grounds. (See 'Chronic respiratory acidosis' below.)

The cause of metabolic alkalosis may be apparent from the history and from an assessment of volume status ( table 1 ). Review medications for thiazide or loop diuretic use. Hypokalemia is very common and, when present, replete potassium and obtain serum magnesium level. Treat the underlying cause if possible.

If a high serum HCO 3 resolves promptly with intravenous fluids, it can generally be attributed to metabolic alkalosis caused by either loss of gastric fluid, diuretics, and/or laxative use. Self-induced vomiting and surreptitious diuretic or laxative abuse associated with the anorexia nervosa-bulimia nervosa spectrum are relatively common and must always be considered in patients with unexplained metabolic alkalosis, especially in women.

When the etiology is not apparent from the history and physical examination, obtain a spot urine chloride (see "Clinical manifestations and evaluation of metabolic alkalosis", section on 'When the cause is not apparent from the history' ):

● Urine chloride low (≤20 mEq/L) – The spot urine chloride is persistently low when metabolic alkalosis is associated with volume contraction and chloride depletion. Examples include vomiting, nasogastric suction, laxative abuse, and cystic fibrosis. The urine chloride is also low when the alkalosis is generated by diuretics but only once their diuretic effect has worn off or waned. (See "Clinical manifestations and evaluation of metabolic alkalosis", section on 'Disorders associated with a low urine chloride concentration (less than 20 mEq/L)' .)

● Urine chloride not low (>20 mEq/L) – The spot urine chloride is not low when metabolic alkalosis is due to any of the following disorders:

• Disorders that cause high blood pressure (eg, primary hyperaldosteronism, Cushing's disease, ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone [ACTH] production, exogenous mineralocorticoids, mineralocorticoid-like substances, Liddle's syndrome, syndrome of apparent mineralocorticoid excess, licorice ingestion or use of certain herbal teas). (See "Apparent mineralocorticoid excess syndromes (including chronic licorice ingestion)" and "Diagnosis of primary aldosteronism" .)

• Very severe potassium depletion.

• Genetic mutations that mimic the effect of loop and/or thiazide diuretics (eg, Bartter and Gitelman syndromes). (See "Genetic disorders of the collecting tubule sodium channel: Liddle syndrome and pseudohypoaldosteronism type 1" and "Inherited hypokalemic salt-losing tubulopathies: Pathophysiology and overview of clinical manifestations", section on 'Clinical manifestations' .)

• Use of thiazide or loop diuretics if the tubule inhibitory effect of the diuretic is still active.

● Diuretic-induced alkalosis – Diuretics that block sodium chloride reabsorption (thiazide and loop diuretics) produce a high urine chloride concentration when they are active, but a low urine chloride concentration develops when the diuretic effect has dissipated. (See "Clinical manifestations and evaluation of metabolic alkalosis", section on 'Diuretic-induced alkalosis' .)

Chronic respiratory acidosis  —  A persistently high serum HCO 3 may suggest a diagnosis of chronic hypercapnia. The compensatory response to chronic respiratory acidosis increases the serum HCO 3 by approximately 3 to 4 mEq/L for every 10 mmHg (1.3 kPa) elevation in the arterial PCO 2 .

Severe or end-stage lung disease (eg, COPD [emphysema], interstitial fibrosis) can be apparent from the clinical history and physical examination.

Review:

● Pulse oximetry, supplemental oxygen therapy (eg, delivery device, FIO 2 )

● Serum electrolytes, BUN, creatinine

● Spirometry (for evidence of airflow obstruction or to suggest restrictive lung disease)

● Chest imaging (for evidence of hyperinflation and pulmonary parenchymal pathology that may be associated with hypercapnia)

Obtain:

● ABG if not previously measured or if unexplained elevated HCO 3 :

• If the serum HCO 3 is not as high as expected for the PCO 2 , then metabolic acidosis may coexist, and the arterial pH may be substantially reduced. Alternatively, a component of the increase in PCO 2 may have developed acutely (acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis).

• If the serum HCO 3 is higher than expected for the PCO 2 , then metabolic alkalosis may coexist, and the arterial pH may be inappropriately normal or even elevated. Alternatively, a chronically high PCO 2 may have been acutely reduced (for example, the patient was placed on a ventilator) and the HCO 3 has not yet fallen to the appropriate level (posthypercapnic metabolic alkalosis).

Further testing is based upon clinical setting. (See "Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: Diagnosis and staging" .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for total HCO 3 is approximately 23 to 30 mEq/L (or mmol/L [SI]), although high HCO 3 is often defined as >27 mEq/L. The normal reference ranges can vary depending on the patient population, geographic location, and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
