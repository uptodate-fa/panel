INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) is a nonspecific, indirect measure of the inflammatory response. The magnitude of elevation does not identify the etiology. An elevated ESR must be interpreted in the context of the reason the test was ordered, a comprehensive history and physical examination, laboratory tests, and whether the patient is clinically ill. If there are no worrisome clinical features, a borderline normal ESR <20 mm/hour may be the individual's baseline level and may not require further evaluation. (See "Acute phase reactants", section on 'Laboratory evaluation' .)

Review tests that may be available at the time of presentation:

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

● C-reactive protein (CRP)

● Basic chemistries, including glucose, creatinine, liver biochemical tests, and calcium

A normocytic anemia may be present as part of a general acute inflammatory response. Depending upon the patient's symptoms, physical findings, and other diagnoses under consideration, additional testing may be required.

Mildly elevated ESR 20 to 40 mm/hour  —  A mildly elevated ESR (eg, 20 to 40 mm/hour) may be due to an acute and/or chronic inflammatory process, or due to factors unrelated to inflammation (eg, hematologic disorders or end-stage kidney disease). ESR increases proportionally with age and female sex. Mild elevations are also seen in those with overweight, obesity and metabolic syndrome. If the mildly elevated ESR is not explained by the clinical presentation, reassess clinically and obtain repeat ESR with CRP. (See "High C-reactive protein in adults" .)

● ESR and CRP normal – If both the CRP and repeat ESR are normal in an individual without signs and symptoms of systemic disease, a significant inflammatory process is unlikely.

● ESR and CRP discordant – If the CRP and repeat ESR are discordant, the cause of the discrepancy may be due to any of the following:

• Differences in kinetics, with CRP rising and decreasing more rapidly than the ESR

• Characteristics of the inflammatory response or immune disease:

- ESR may be a better marker of some low-grade bone and joint infections.

- A disproportionate elevation of the ESR compared with the CRP may be seen in some patients with active systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in the absence of active infection.

• Factors unrelated to inflammation:

- Hematologic factors (eg, anemia, increased normal and abnormal immunoglobulins frequently and significantly elevate the ESR)

- Renal disease (eg, unlike CRP, ESR increases in end-stage kidney disease or the nephrotic syndrome)

- Technical factors (eg, tilting of the ESR tube, room temperature, clotting of the blood sample or delay in testing)

The cause of the discrepancy is often unknown. Further investigation may be warranted if an inflammatory condition has not yet been identified.

● ESR and CRP elevated – If both the CRP and repeat ESR are elevated, an underlying inflammatory process is likely, and further evaluation and management must be correlated with clinical presentation. Markedly elevated levels of CRP are strongly associated with infection. (See "High C-reactive protein in adults" .)

Moderately to markedly elevated ESR >40 mm/hour  —  A moderately to markedly elevated ESR (eg, >40 mm/hour) may be due to a chronic or an acute inflammatory state, including infection, trauma, infarction, inflammatory arthritides and other systemic autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, and various neoplasms. The diagnosis is often apparent at the time of clinical assessment.

Initial considerations of markedly elevated levels include acute illness due to any of the following:

● Bacterial infection – Bacterial arthritis, infective endocarditis, and osteomyelitis.

● Systemic inflammatory rheumatic disease – Giant cell arteritis, adult-onset Still's disease, and polymyalgia rheumatica (however, the ESR can be mildly elevated or even normal). (See "Diagnosis of giant cell arteritis" and "Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of polymyalgia rheumatica" .)

● Malignancy – Most patients with malignancy and a markedly elevated ESR have widely metastatic disease, which can occur with many types of cancer, rather than occult neoplasms. Extreme elevations may be associated with multiple myeloma.

Other causes of markedly elevated levels include drug hypersensitivity reactions, thrombophlebitis, kidney disease (particularly the nephrotic syndrome), and vasculitis.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The ESR is defined as the rate (expressed in mm/hour) at which erythrocytes suspended in plasma settle when placed in a vertical tube. The magnitude of the ESR depends largely upon plasma constituents (eg, fibrinogen, immunoglobulins, and the size, shape, and number of red cells). A commonly accepted reference range is 0 to 15 mm/hour in men and 0 to 20 mm/hour in women. The reference range increases with age, female sex, and obesity. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based on the reference range reported with that result and the clinical context that prompted testing.

The ESR can be corrected for age by using the following formulas: the upper limit of the reference range equals (age in years)/2 for men and (age in years + 10)/2 for women.
