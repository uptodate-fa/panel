INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The serum free light chain (FLC) assay is a sensitive antibody-based system that measures free kappa or lambda immunoglobulin light chains in the serum. The test report consists of three parts: the serum free kappa level, the serum free lambda level, and serum kappa/lambda FLC ratio.

An elevation in one of the FLCs coupled with an abnormal FLC ratio is suggestive of the presence of a clonal plasma cell proliferative disorder, such as multiple myeloma (MM), smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM), Waldenström macroglobulinemia, immunoglobulin light chain (AL) amyloidosis, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), and solitary plasmacytoma. It indicates excess production of one type of FLC (kappa or lambda) that occurs with monoclonal proliferation of plasma cells ( table 1 ). (See "Laboratory methods for analyzing monoclonal proteins", section on 'Indications and uses' .)

Abnormal serum FLC results must be interpreted with caution in patients with renal insufficiency. As the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) declines, there is a reduction in excretion of FLCs, a rise in serum FLCs, and an increase in the kappa to lambda ratio (urinary free kappa excretion is more severely reduced than lambda excretion).

Serum FLC results should be obtained in patients in whom there is clinical suspicion for a plasma cell proliferative disorder, and the results should be interpreted in conjunction with the serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and immunofixation obtained as part of the initial laboratory evaluation. Plasma cell disorders are suspected when patients present with osteolytic bone lesions, unexplained hypercalcemia, normochromic normocytic anemia, or acute renal failure. They can also be suspected in patients with recurrent unexplained infections, infiltrative cardiomyopathy, hyperviscosity symptoms, lymphadenopathy, or organomegaly.

Review (obtain) the following tests, as appropriate for the clinical situation:

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

● Serum calcium and creatinine (SCr)

● SPEP and immunofixation

● Quantitative immunoglobulins

● Urinalysis with examination of sediment

Obtain specialty consultation:

● Hematology consultation if there is evidence of a monoclonal protein (M-protein) by any method ( table 2 ) or if the clinical assessment is concerning for a plasma cell disorder ( table 3 ). (See "Multiple myeloma: Clinical features, laboratory manifestations, and diagnosis" and "Clinical presentation, laboratory manifestations, and diagnosis of immunoglobulin light chain (AL) amyloidosis" and "Epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis of Waldenström macroglobulinemia" .)

● Nephrology consultation if there is evidence of renal impairment. The presence and severity of impaired kidney function correlates with survival. A renal biopsy may be required for diagnosis. (See "Kidney disease in multiple myeloma and other monoclonal gammopathies: Etiology and evaluation" .)

Free light chain ratio abnormal  —  The FLC assay is interpreted by looking at both the absolute level of serum FLC as well as the FLC ratio. Although the FLC ratio is reported as kappa/lambda, for purposes of clinical practice, we use involved/uninvolved FLC ratio thresholds to make management decisions for ease of use. Thus, when the FLC ratio is abnormal, we recommend calculating the involved/uninvolved FLC ratio by dividing the higher FLC level with the lower level.

Serum free light chain levels increased  —  The presence of an abnormal FLC ratio in conjunction with elevation of the involved light chain above the reference range is by definition indicative of the presence of a clonal plasma cell disorder ( table 1 ). In most patients, the abnormality is mild and indicates MGUS (if the serum immunofixation is positive for an M-protein) or light chain MGUS (if no heavy chain abnormality is detected on serum immunofixation). Guidance on interpretation and management based on the severity of the abnormality is provided below:

● FLC ratio abnormal but <8 – An abnormal FLC ratio that is <8 is most often related to MGUS, light chain MGUS, or low-risk SMM. Less frequently, borderline abnormalities of the FLC ratio may be seen in patients with renal insufficiency. Obtain hematology consultation if the patient has clinical features of a plasma cell dyscrasia or if the patient has a serum M-protein on immunofixation. In patients with MGUS and SMM, the presence of an abnormal FLC ratio is associated with an increased risk of progression.

● FLC ratio 8 to 100 – Even if renal insufficiency is present, an FLC ratio ≥8 suggests a clonal plasma cell proliferative disorder, such as SMM or MM. Obtain hematology consultation for further evaluation regardless of symptoms. In patients with MGUS and SMM, the presence of an abnormal FLC ratio is associated with an increased risk of progression.

● FLC ratio ≥100 – An FLC ratio ≥100 is usually indicative of a serious plasma cell disorder and is associated with near inevitable progression to end-organ damage within the next two years. Obtain urgent hematology consultation, if not already involved. All patients should undergo a 24-hour urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation. Occasionally, patients with very high serum FLC ratio may have negligible amounts of M-protein in the urine. In these cases, the high FLC ratio may not be due to overproduction, as occurs in a malignancy, but may rather be related to impaired renal excretion due to abnormal dimerization of the light chains. Such patients should nevertheless be referred to hematology for counseling and monitoring.

● FLC levels ≥1500 mg/L – An abnormal FLC ratio with very high FLC concentrations is almost always a consequence of a malignant plasma cell disorder, primarily MM. Such high concentrations can cause acute kidney injury and a significant reduction in survival. Obtain urgent specialty consultation, if not already involved. (See "Kidney disease in multiple myeloma and other monoclonal gammopathies: Etiology and evaluation" .)

Serum free light chain levels normal  —  An abnormal FLC ratio with normal total light chains has no major clinical consequence and does not need to be followed.

Free light chain ratio normal

Serum free light chain levels increased  —  Elevation of kappa and/or lambda FLCs in the presence of a normal serum FLC ratio usually indicates polyclonal proliferation of plasma cells (as may occur with infection or inflammation) and/or renal insufficiency:

● Excess production of both FLCs in response to immune stimulation (eg, acute infection, active systemic autoimmune disease).

● Reduced excretion of both FLCs in mild renal insufficiency (eg, reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] ≥45 mL/min per 1.73 m 2 ).

However, the presence of a monoclonal gammopathy is not excluded since patients with clonal plasma cell disorders can have a normal FLC ratio, and therefore, if there is clinical suspicion, SPEP and immunofixation are needed. Obtain hematology consultation if the patient has clinical features of a plasma cell dyscrasia ( table 3 ) and/or a monoclonal increase in immunoglobulins identified by another method.

If there are no clinical features concerning for plasma cell dyscrasia, manage acute illness and obtain serum FLC testing approximately three months after resolution of acute illness. Any persistently abnormal FLC results should be evaluated by a specialist with expertise in plasma cell disorders.

Serum free light chain levels normal  —  This is the normal result. However, if there is clinical suspicion, it must be recognized that a normal FLC ratio does not exclude a monoclonal gammopathy (eg, MGUS, SMM, solitary plasmacytoma). Approximately 60 percent of patients with MGUS and 30 percent of patients with SMM have normal FLC ratios. The FLC assay does not measure light chains that are bound to immunoglobulins in intact immunoglobulins. It is only abnormal if clonal light chains are secreted in an unbound "free" form.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

In individuals with normal kidney function, the normal reference range for serum kappa light chains is 3.3 to 19.4 mg/L; for lambda light chains, 5.71 to 26.3 mg/L; and for the kappa to lambda ratio, 0.26 to 1.65. In patients with renal impairment, the normal reference range for the FLC ratio varies based on the level of renal impairment ( table 4 ). Serum FLC values obtained with different assay methods are not reliably comparable. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the age-adjusted reference range reported for that result.
