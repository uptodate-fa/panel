INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Tryptase, a biomarker of mast cells, can be measured in serum to help diagnose suspected anaphylaxis or to help identify an underlying primary disorder. Alpha and beta mast cell tryptases exist in mature and pro (precursor) forms. Commercial immunoassays measure all forms of alpha- and beta-tryptases, reported as "total tryptase." An immunoassay for mature tryptases (alpha and beta) has limited availability in a specialty setting. In most settings, total tryptase is the only assay available. The predictive value of total tryptase is markedly improved by comparing an acute with a nonacute (baseline) sample. (See 'Acute (event-related) sample' below and 'Nonacute (baseline) sample' below.)

Obtain consultation with an allergy specialist to help identify and treat secondary allergic as well as primary mast cell disorders. (See "Anaphylaxis: Confirming the diagnosis and determining the cause(s)", section on 'Referral to an allergy/immunology specialist' .)

Acute (event-related) sample  —  If total tryptase is measured to evaluate an acute, possibly anaphylactic event, the acute sample, optimally, should be collected between 30 to 120 minutes after clinical onset, though it may be informative for up to four hours. The magnitude of tryptase elevation generally reflects the following:

● Extent of mast cell activation and clinical severity (primarily the magnitude of hypotension).

● Timing of sample collection in relation to onset of signs and symptoms (with markedly reduced sensitivity after four hours).

● Nature of the anaphylactic stimulus – Typically, parenteral triggers (eg, chemotherapy drugs, monoclonal antibodies, targeted therapies, antibiotics, and insect stings) or oral medications cause higher tryptase elevations than an oral exposure to an ingested food.

The absence of a significant elevation in the acute tryptase level does not rule out local mast cell activation. To interpret the acute tryptase level, a baseline tryptase level must be obtained. (See 'Nonacute (baseline) sample' below.)

Other conditions in the clinical differential diagnosis of possible anaphylaxis (eg, vasovagal reactions, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, septic shock, seizures, myocardial shock, benign flushing, or carcinoid syndrome) are not normally associated with acute elevations in serum tryptase levels.

Nonacute (baseline) sample  —  Baseline tryptase levels should be determined on samples collected either before the acute event or ≥24 hours after all signs and symptoms have resolved. (A serum sample collected prior to the possible anaphylactic event for an unrelated test can sometimes be retrieved from the clinical laboratory if stored either frozen or in a refrigerator.)

If the patient is being evaluated for a possible anaphylactic event, review the acute, event-related sample (or obtain an acute, event-related sample if only the nonacute, baseline sample has been collected):

● If the acute tryptase level is at least 20 percent above baseline plus 2 ng/mL (ie, ≥[2 + 1.2 x baseline value]), reflecting acute release of tryptase by mast cell degranulation, then the diagnosis of systemic anaphylaxis is confirmed ( table 1 ), even if the acute tryptase level is in the normal range. (See "Laboratory tests to support the clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis", section on 'Elevations in anaphylaxis' .)

● If anaphylaxis is suspected based on the clinical presentation, and the acute tryptase level is less than this calculated value (ie, <[2 + 1.2 x baseline value]), repeat the baseline tryptase level.

If the baseline (nonacute) tryptase levels are persistently elevated (on at least two occasions) following an acute elevation in tryptase, a primary disorder should be considered ( table 2 ):

● Hereditary alpha-tryptasemia is an autosomal dominant genetic condition in which affected members have high normal or elevated baseline total tryptase levels (>8 ng/mL). The prevalence in the general population may be as high as 6 percent in those with European ancestry. This genetic trait increases the severity of anaphylaxis. (See "Hereditary alpha-tryptasemia", section on 'Diagnosis' .)

● Systemic mastocytosis is a disorder of clonal mast cell hyperplasia. It affects approximately 1:10,000 adults, approximately 70 percent of whom have a baseline tryptase level >20 but some (10 to 15 percent) with a level in the normal range (11.4 ng/mL or less). (See "Mastocytosis (cutaneous and systemic) in adults: Epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis", section on 'Systemic mastocytosis' .)

● Monoclonal mast cell activation syndrome has some features of systemic mastocytosis, but it lacks the full criteria needed to diagnose systemic mastocytosis. Baseline tryptase levels can be normal or elevated. (See "Mastocytosis (cutaneous and systemic) in adults: Epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis", section on 'Disorders with similar clinical manifestations' and "Mast cell disorders: An overview", section on 'Monoclonal mast cell activation syndrome' .)

Elevated baseline tryptase levels are also observed in patients with an underlying hematologic clonal myeloid disorder (eg, acute myeloid leukemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, or hypereosinophilic syndrome), metastatic cancers (eg, lung or breast), severe chronic kidney disease, or other disease states. The magnitude of an elevated baseline tryptase level generally reflects the total body burden of mast cells and/or the overproduction of tryptase per mast cell, but not release of tryptase by mast cell activation-degranulation. (See "Laboratory tests to support the clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis", section on 'Elevations in nonanaphylactic patients' and "Mastocytosis (cutaneous and systemic) in adults: Epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis" .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for baseline serum tryptase is ≤11.4 ng/mL in most laboratories. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
