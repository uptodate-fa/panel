INITIAL EVALUATION — 

C-reactive protein (CRP) is an acute phase reactant that is used to identify the presence and degree of inflammation. CRP is not specific to any disease, and it cannot distinguish infection from other causes of acute and chronic inflammation. (See "Acute phase reactants", section on 'Laboratory evaluation'.)

CRP assays are broadly separated into standard assays and high-sensitivity assays. Clinicians should be aware of which assay was used, and, ideally, the same assay should be used to interpret serial measurements.

●Standard CRP assays – Standard CRP assays are reported in units of mg/dL or mg/L. The lower limit of detection is approximately 3 to 5 mg/L, a level above the concentration observed in most apparently healthy individuals. The assays are a sensitive but nonspecific measure of inflammation.

●High-sensitivity CRP assays – High-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) assays are routinely reported in units of mg/L. The limit of detection (≤0.3 mg/L) is lower than that of traditional assays. This level is within the range normally seen in apparently healthy individuals. Along with traditional risk factors for cardiovascular disease, hs-CRP is sometimes used to assess the risk of future cardiovascular events; there is a linear association with higher risk at higher hs-CRP levels.

Standard CRP assay — An elevated CRP must be interpreted in the context of the reason it was requested, a comprehensive history and physical examination, other laboratory test results, and whether the patient is clinically ill. Abnormalities generally reflect the presence and intensity of an inflammatory process, including infection, trauma, infarction, systemic autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, and various neoplasms. There is no universally agreed upon CRP level that is especially concerning. The likelihood of a bacterial infection is greater with a higher CRP level. However, the magnitude of elevation does not identify the etiology.

In certain circumstances, it may be helpful to compare the results of CRP testing with erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and other laboratory results. (See "High erythrocyte sedimentation rate in adults", section on 'Mildly elevated ESR 20 to 40 mm/hour'.)

Further evaluation is based on the clinical presentation and reason for initial testing (ie, diagnosis, monitoring of disease activity, or as a prognostic marker). Repeat testing is primarily useful to follow the course of illness. However, it may be helpful to repeat CRP testing if no cause has been found, the timing of which depends on the acuity of illness.

The following thresholds are for general guidance.

CRP >100 mg/L — Markedly elevated levels of CRP >100 mg/L (10 mg/dL) are most commonly associated with bacterial infection, with neoplastic, autoimmune, and autoinflammatory diseases being less common. The diagnosis is often apparent at the time of clinical assessment. Patients with this degree of elevation are usually acutely ill and require timely evaluation and management.

CRP between 10 and ≤100 mg/L — CRP concentrations between 10 mg/L and ≤100 mg/L indicate clinically significant inflammation. Patients may have nonspecific symptoms, and the diagnosis (eg, occult infection, early disease) may not be apparent at the time of initial clinical assessment. However, CRP values in this range should prompt further evaluation for an underlying inflammatory process.

CRP between 3 and ≤10 mg/L — CRP concentrations between 3 mg/L (0.3 mg/dL) and ≤10 mg/L indicate what is commonly referred to as low-grade inflammation due to metabolic stress (eg, diabetes, obesity, and smoking) and may also reflect minor degrees of inflammation (eg, periodontitis, treated rheumatoid arthritis).

hs-CRP assay — hs-CRP is sometimes used for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk stratification. CRP is a known factor in the development of atherosclerosis and subsequent ASCVD events. However, the use of hs-CRP to screen the general population for cardiovascular risk is controversial. While some experts recommend the routine measurement of hs-CRP at the time of cholesterol screening (to be used as adjunctive data in the overall assessment of cardiovascular risk), the marginal value added to that determined by screening for other coronary risk factors is small in the individual patient (figure 1). (See "Acute phase reactants", section on 'C-reactive protein'.)

Although predictive value is independent of other established risk factors, elevated values must be considered in the context of traditional cardiovascular risk factors and other medical conditions. We consider hs-CRP primarily for those with intermediate cardiovascular risk (eg, 10 to 20 percent risk at 10 years) as determined using traditional risk factors and one or more of the available risk calculators. Due to the variability of hs-CRP values in an individual over time, the average of two measurements, fasting or nonfasting, should be obtained, optimally two weeks apart. This approach provides a more stable estimate of cardiovascular risk than a single measurement. hs-CRP levels are used to estimate cardiovascular disease risk; they are not therapeutic targets. (See "C-reactive protein in cardiovascular disease" and "Cardiovascular disease risk assessment for primary prevention: Risk calculators" and "Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk assessment for primary prevention in adults".)

The following thresholds are for general guidance for patients of intermediate cardiovascular risk who are not acutely ill:

hs-CRP >3 mg/L — Elevated cardiovascular risk can be defined as >3 mg/L. Values in the range of 3 to 9.9 mg/L can be found in approximately 30 percent of the American population. A value >10 mg/L should prompt a search for a source of infection or inflammation.

hs-CRP 1 to 3 mg/L — Average cardiovascular risk can be defined as 1 to 3 mg/L.

hs-CRP <1 mg/L — There is no standardized CRP value that can be considered normal. However, hs-CRP <1 suggests a low risk for cardiovascular disease.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

CRP levels vary with age, sex, and race. In an hs-CRP assay, the normal reference range is generally ≤3 mg/L (≤0.3 mg/dL). In a standard CRP assay, cited reference ranges for CRP vary greatly from one laboratory to another, to a degree that cannot be explained on a biologic or technical basis. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
