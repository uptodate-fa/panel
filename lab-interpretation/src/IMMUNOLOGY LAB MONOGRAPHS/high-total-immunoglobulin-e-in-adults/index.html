INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Increased serum total immunoglobulin E (IgE) is seen in some parasitic and viral infections, allergic or atopic diseases, some primary immunodeficiencies, certain inflammatory diseases, some malignancies, and other disorders ( table 1 ).

Total IgE levels are measured to help diagnose and treat allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) and to evaluate a few rare disorders that are associated with very elevated levels of total IgE, but typically do not add useful information to the evaluation of common allergic or atopic diseases or parasitic infections. Laboratory tools for diagnosis of parasitic infections associated with elevated total IgE levels (eg, ascariasis, schistosomiasis, and strongyloidiasis) include stool testing and serology rather than total IgE levels. (See "The biology of IgE" and "The relationship between IgE and allergic disease" and 'Diagnosis of allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis' below.)

Total serum IgE levels may not be helpful for monitoring patients receiving anti-IgE therapy (ie, omalizumab). Although omalizumab therapy typically increases total IgE levels, the IgE assay does not distinguish free IgE from IgE complexed to the drug.

IgE-mediated allergic disorders  —  Patients with allergic conditions, such as asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, or food allergy, often have higher serum levels of total IgE compared with the general population, but there is significant overlap. Unlike allergen-specific IgE, quantification of total serum IgE provides no information about which atopic condition the patient may have or to what allergens the patient is sensitive. (See "Overview of skin testing for IgE-mediated allergic disease" .)

Diagnosis of an IgE-mediated allergy is based on the following three components:

● Identification of an allergen to which a patient is exposed

● Demonstration of IgE specific to that allergen using skin testing or an allergen-specific IgE immunoassay

● Confirmation that the symptoms occur when the patient is exposed to the allergen

Review in consultation with an allergist to determine the need for further evaluation (eg, skin testing, measurement of specific serum IgE) of patients suspected of IgE-mediated allergic disease.

Diagnosis of allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis  —  Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) is one of the few disorders in which total IgE values are used directly in diagnosis and treatment ( table 2 ). The ubiquitous mold Aspergillus fumigatus causes immunologic-mediated damage in the lung, typically in patients with asthma or cystic fibrosis.

ABPA is suspected in patients with asthma or cystic fibrosis with recurrent exacerbations; a chest radiograph or chest computed tomography showing mucus plugging, upper or middle lobe consolidation, or central bronchiectasis; or an eosinophil count >500/m 3 . The total serum IgE level is generally ≥500 international units/mL. Levels of total IgE increase during, and may precede, exacerbations of ABPA and fall with successful therapy. (See "Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis" .)

Obtain (review):

● Total blood eosinophil count (generally elevated >500 cells/microL)

● Specific IgE and IgG antibodies to A. fumigatus

● Chest imaging

● Allergy and pulmonary consultation, as appropriate

Other rare disorders associated with very elevated levels of total IgE include:

● Lymphocytic variant hypereosinophilic syndrome (L-HES) – Although serum IgE and gamma globulin levels should be determined, increases are neither necessary nor specific for diagnosis of L-HES. (See "Hypereosinophilic syndromes: Clinical manifestations, pathophysiology, and diagnosis", section on 'T cell lymphocytic variants' .)

● Autosomal dominant hyperimmunoglobulin E syndrome – Hyperimmunoglobulin E recurrent infection syndrome (HIES) is a rare disorder characterized by elevated serum IgE levels (typically 1000 to >50,000 international units/mL) and a rash similar to atopic dermatitis. (See "Autosomal dominant hyperimmunoglobulin E syndrome" .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

Normal serum levels in adolescents and adults range from undetectable to 100 international units/mL (244 ng/mL). IgE levels change with age. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
