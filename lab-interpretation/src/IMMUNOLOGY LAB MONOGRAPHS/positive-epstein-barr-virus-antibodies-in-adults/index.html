INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is a widely disseminated herpes virus that is spread by intimate contact between EBV shedders and susceptible individuals. Approximately 90 to 95 percent of adults are eventually EBV-seropositive. EBV, the primary agent of infectious mononucleosis, persists asymptomatically for life in most infected adults and may be associated with the development of B cell lymphoma, T cell lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma. (See "Virology of Epstein-Barr virus" and "Infectious mononucleosis" .)

● Diagnosis of infectious mononucleosis - EBV-specific antibodies are the gold standard for diagnosis of infectious mononucleosis and are favored at many institutions as the initial test for the diagnosis. Heterophile antibody testing (Monospot) is a reasonable alternative diagnostic test in settings where EBV-specific antibodies are not available, or if obtaining test results in <1 hour is clinically useful. However, false negative heterophile antibody results may occur.

If there is a strong clinical suspicion of EBV infection and the heterophile test is negative, the heterophile test may be repeated since testing can be negative early in the clinical illness. Alternatively (or in addition), EBV-specific antibodies can be measured to establish the diagnosis, as they do not suffer the problem of heterophile-negative infection.

EBV antibodies may also be measured to establish EBV infection as the cause of the following presentations:

• Clinical variants – fever, elevated liver function tests, and/or lymphadenopathy without pharyngitis

• Neurologic syndromes occurring weeks after initial symptom onset

● EBV antibody profile - An EBV antibody profile may consist of three components: immunoglobulin M (IgM) and immunoglobulin G (IgG) viral capsid antigen (VCA) and IgG Epstein-Barr virus nuclear antigen (EBNA). The results of an EBV antibody profile need to be interpreted within the appropriate clinical context ( algorithm 1 ):

• Although IgM and IgG antibodies directed against VCA have high sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of infectious mononucleosis (97 and 94 percent, respectively), false-positive IgM results may occur with infection with other herpes viruses (eg, cytomegalovirus [CMV]) and other illnesses associated with an intense immune response. IgG results may be falsely positive if the patient recently received immune globulin-containing EBV IgG antibodies.

• IgG antibodies to VCA and EBNA are present for life in patients following infectious mononucleosis as routine latent infection is established. They are not reliable markers of chronic active EBV infection, a rare hematologic disorder due to prolonged active EBV viremia. (See "Clinical manifestations and treatment of Epstein-Barr virus infection", section on 'Chronic active EBV infection' .)

• Patients with symptoms of persistent fatigue alone should not use positive EBV serologic markers alone as causal attribution. (See "Clinical features and diagnosis of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome" .)

IgM VCA positive  —  Detection of IgM VCA antibodies in a compatible clinical presentation suggests that acute EBV infection is likely. IgG VCA antibodies are also usually detectable at the onset of clinical illness because of the long incubation period.

IgG EBNA negative  —  If IgG EBNA antibodies are negative when IgM VCA antibodies are detected, diagnosis of acute EBV infection is almost certain. Expression of EBNA antibodies requires the establishment of proteins related to EBV latency that occurs no earlier than four weeks after the onset of initial mononucleosis symptoms.

IgG EBNA positive  —  The interpretation of positive IgM VCA and IgG EBNA antibodies depends on the timing of testing in relation to the onset of clinical illness:

● Onset of mononucleosis syndrome ≤4 weeks – Even if IgM VCA is positive, the presence of IgG EBNA antibodies within four weeks of mononucleosis symptom onset excludes acute primary EBV infection. EBNA antibodies usually take 6 to 12 weeks after symptom onset to appear. In this scenario, a positive VCA IgM may reflect infection occurring at a time more distant than six weeks or maybe a false positive, as IgM assays can turn transiently positive at times of general intense immune responses to other infections.

In this setting, IgG EBNA antibodies indicate prior EBV infection. Evaluate for other causes of the mononucleosis-like syndrome. Additional tests may include:

• CMV IgG and IgM antibodies or CMV polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (see "Overview of diagnostic tests for cytomegalovirus infection" )

• HIV fourth-generation testing (see "Acute and early HIV infection: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis" )

• Toxoplasma gondii IgG and IgM antibodies (see "Diagnostic testing for toxoplasmosis infection" )

● Onset of mononucleosis syndrome >4 weeks – Detection of both IgM VCA antibodies and IgG EBNA antibodies after four weeks of symptom onset suggests recent EBV infection (within the past three months before IgM VCA antibodies have waned). IgM VCA antibody levels usually decline approximately three months after the onset of clinical illness. (See "Infectious mononucleosis" .)

IgM VCA negative  —  The absence of IgM VCA antibodies suggests that acute EBV infection is unlikely. IgG VCA antibodies persist for life.

IgG EBNA positive  —  If IgG EBNA antibodies are positive when IgM VCA is negative, past EBV infection is most certain. In the setting of past infection, IgG VCA antibodies will also be positive. However, occasionally immunocompetent individuals may have negative IgG VCA antibodies. In this setting, it is unclear if isolated IgG EBNA positivity is a true past infection or false-positive EBNA.

IgG EBNA negative  —  If both IgG EBNA and IgM VCA antibodies are negative, detectable IgG VCA antibodies indicate a recent or past EBV infection. IgG EBNA antibodies usually develop after recent infection has resolved in the majority of individuals. However, a minority of individuals (approximately 5 to 10 percent) never develop EBNA antibodies.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

Serologies are reported as either positive or negative. A negative test for IgM and IgG VCA and IgG EBNA antibodies indicates no prior exposure in immunocompetent hosts. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
