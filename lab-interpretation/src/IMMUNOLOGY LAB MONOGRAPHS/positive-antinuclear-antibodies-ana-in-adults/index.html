INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) may be detected in patients with systemic autoimmune diseases (eg, systemic lupus erythematosus [SLE]), organ specific autoimmune diseases (eg, autoimmune thyroid disease), as well as infectious diseases and malignancies ( table 1 ). ANAs may also be detected in healthy individuals. (See "Measurement and clinical significance of antinuclear antibodies" .)

Interpretation of a positive ANA test requires knowing the pretest probability of ANA-associated diseases (based on a complete history and physical examination). The higher the prior probability that a patient has an ANA-associated autoimmune disease (eg, SLE), the more likely the results of the ANA test will assist in supporting the diagnosis. If a test for ANA is ordered in settings of low pretest probability (eg, to evaluate vague musculoskeletal pain or fatigue), then the likelihood that a positive test will indicate the presence of an ANA-associated autoimmune disease is markedly decreased. (See "Measurement and clinical significance of antinuclear antibodies" .)

Symptoms and signs that are suggestive of a rheumatologic disease include:

● Inflamed joints

● Dry eyes and dry mouth

● Muscle weakness

● Erythematous skin lesions or skin tightness

● Raynaud phenomenon

● Pleural or pericardial effusion

Additional steps in assessing the clinical importance of a positive test for ANA include:

● Reviewing medications – Certain drugs (eg, procainamide, hydralazine, minocycline, diltiazem, isoniazid, quinidine, methyldopa, chlorpromazine, and monoclonal antibodies directed against tumor necrosis factor) may induce autoantibodies. A patient who is using these medications may have a positive ANA in the absence of evidence of drug-induced lupus erythematosus (LE). (See "Drug-induced lupus", section on 'Diagnostic approach' .)

● Obtaining or reviewing routine laboratory tests to identify leukopenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, renal dysfunction, or nephritis, which may occur in patients with systemic autoimmune diseases:

• Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

• Creatinine

• Urinalysis with urine sediment

Subsequent testing to further evaluate a positive test for ANA depends on the type of assay that is used to detect ANA. Therefore, it is necessary that each clinician be aware of the method used by the laboratory to detect these autoantibodies. The two most commonly used methods to detect antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) are:

● Indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) using the human epidermoid carcinoma (HEp-2) cell line as substrate (preferred).

● Solid-phase assays, such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), fluorescent microsphere assay, and immunoline assays.

ANA testing by indirect immunofluorescence  —  The results of ANA testing by indirect immunofluorescence are reported in terms of titer and staining pattern. (See "Measurement and clinical significance of antinuclear antibodies", section on 'Interpreting a positive test for ANA using indirect immunofluorescence' .)

There is considerable controversy concerning the ideal dilution considered to indicate a "positive" ANA test. An international committee of experts recommended that a serum dilution that detects ANA in 5 percent of healthy individuals (usually ≥1:160) should be considered an abnormal test result. For historical reasons, some laboratories continue to report dilutions of 1:40 and 1:80 as positive, even though 30 and 15 percent (respectively) of the normal population will have ANAs at these lower titers. Of note, a positive test for ANA at a dilution of ≥1:80 is considered an "entry criterion" for the SLE classification criteria set by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR). (See "Measurement and clinical significance of antinuclear antibodies", section on 'Definition of a positive result' .)

Titer ≥1:160  —  For patients with an ANA titer ≥1:160, obtain additional tests for specific autoantibodies (depending on the clinical presentation and staining pattern) using solid phase assays:

● Anti-ds-DNA antibody – SLE.

● Anti-histone antibody – SLE, drug-induced LE.

● Anti-Sm antibody – SLE or mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD).

● Anti-U1 RNP antibody – MCTD, SLE; also seen in some patients with rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren's disease, systemic sclerosis, polymyositis.

● Anti-SS-A/Ro antibody – Sjögren's disease, SLE, polymyositis, systemic sclerosis, subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus, primary biliary cholangitis, neonatal lupus syndrome.

● Anti-SS-B/La antibody – When present with SS-A/Ro, anti-SS-B/La antibodies are relatively specific for Sjögren's disease but are also present in some patients with SLE. When anti-SS-B/La antibodies are present in the absence of anti-SS-A/Ro antibodies, their clinical significance is uncertain.

● Additional antibodies, including anti-Scl-70 antibodies (for diffuse systemic sclerosis) and anti-Jo-1 antibodies (for idiopathic inflammatory myositis) should be ordered, if appropriate for the clinical presentation.

In women who may become pregnant, measure antibodies directed against SS-A/Ro using a solid phase assay. In the setting of anti-SS-A/Ro antibodies, there is an increased risk of neonatal lupus syndrome, even when the mother is asymptomatic. (See "The anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antigen-antibody systems" .)

● Specific autoantibodies positive – If there is moderate to high suspicion of a systemic autoimmune disease and any of the specific autoantibodies are positive, it is appropriate to refer the patient to a rheumatologist.

● Specific autoantibodies negative – Negative testing for specific autoantibodies, using a screening panel of solid phase assays, does not exclude the possibility that an ANA-positive patient may have an autoimmune disease. The routine panel of solid phase assays may not detect all of the clinically important autoantibodies. The advisability of repeat testing or other tests depends on the clinical scenario and clinical suspicion of ANA-associated disease.

Consider:

• Systemic autoimmune diseases, including SLE, systemic sclerosis, dermatomyositis, and idiopathic inflammatory myositis. Additional testing with more specialized panels of antigens and referral to a rheumatologist may be appropriate.

• Organ-specific autoimmune disease, including autoimmune thyroid disease (chronic autoimmune [Hashimoto's] thyroiditis or Graves' disease) or autoimmune liver disease (autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis [cirrhosis]) ( table 1 ). Obtain (review):

- TSH, thyroid peroxidase antibodies – Thyroid test abnormalities may require further evaluation and treatment of the underlying thyroid disorder. (See "Diagnosis of and screening for hypothyroidism in nonpregnant adults" and "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism" .)

- Liver biochemical tests – (See "Overview of autoimmune hepatitis" and "Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and prognosis of primary biliary cholangitis" .)

For patients in whom no diagnosis is established, some experts monitor for development of autoimmune disease in selected patients (eg, those with positive ANA in high titer, a family history of rheumatic disease, and/or nonspecific arthralgia). Monitoring may include biannual or annual reassessment of symptoms and physical findings as well as CBC with differential, creatinine, and urinalysis to identify anemia, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, and/or nephritis.

Titer <1:160  —  The international consensus is that only an ANA ≥1:160 should be considered to be positive. Historically, however, ANA detected at dilutions of 1:40 and 1:80 have been considered abnormal, and many clinical laboratories have continued this tradition. Approximately 30 and 15 percent of healthy individuals have ANA at dilutions of 1:40 and 1:80, respectively. Given the high prevalence of low titer ANA in the general population, such results are often of little clinical significance. An exception to this general rule involves women who may become pregnant.

● In all women with a low titer ANA who may become pregnant, measure antibodies directed against SS-A/Ro using a solid phase assay.

There may be high titer antibodies directed against SS-A/Ro even when the ANA titer is low (or even absent) because the SS-A/Ro antigens are present in low concentration in the traditional Hep2 cell substrate used to detect ANA. There is an increased risk of neonatal lupus syndrome in babies born to women with circulating anti-SS-A/Ro antibodies, even if the mother has no symptoms of an autoimmune disease. (See "The anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antigen-antibody systems" .)

● To guide further evaluation in all other patients, the clinical presentation and the results of other laboratory tests should be given greater weight than the results of ANA testing alone.

ANA testing by solid phase assay  —  Solid phase assays (including ELISA, immunoline assays, and addressable laser bead immunoassay [ALBIA]) provide semiquantitative results that may be described as being within a "positive," "indeterminate," or "negative" range for each specific autoantibody tested. The results of solid phase assays are reported in international units.

Solid-phase assays use a panel of autoantigens to detect the presence of specific autoantibodies in patient serum. The panel of antigens used in solid phase assays may include some or all of the following: SS-A/Ro, SS-B/La, Sm, U1 RNP, Scl-70, PM-Scl, Jo-1, centromere B, histones, ribosomal P, and dsDNA. The presence of autoantibodies directed against one or more of the antigens is considered a positive test. Solid phase assays do not test for the presence of all clinically important autoantibodies.

● When the ANA are detected using solid phase assay and there is a clinical suspicion of ANA-associated autoimmune disease, it is appropriate to refer the patient to a rheumatologist for further evaluation.

● If ANA are not detected using a solid phase assay in patients who are suspected of having an ANA-associated autoimmune disease, testing for ANA by indirect immunofluorescence may be helpful.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The results of ANA by indirect immunofluorescence are reported as a titer and pattern of staining. An abnormal test result is the titer at which less than 5 percent of the serum samples from healthy individuals produces a positive result (usually a dilution ≥1:160). Some laboratories continue to report dilutions of 1:40 and 1:80 as positive. When ANAs are measured using solid phase assays, the presence of autoantibodies is reported in international units, with normal reference range provided by the laboratory.
