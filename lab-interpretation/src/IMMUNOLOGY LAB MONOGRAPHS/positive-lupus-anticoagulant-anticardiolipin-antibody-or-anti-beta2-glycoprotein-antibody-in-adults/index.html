INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The major antiphospholipid antibody (aPL) detection tests are the lupus anticoagulant (LA), anticardiolipin (aCL) antibodies, and anti-beta2-glycoprotein I (anti-beta2-GP I) antibodies. These tests should be performed and interpreted together, in conjunction with clinical information.

Initial testing of aPL antibodies is typically performed to:

● Help diagnose antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) at the time of a clinical event (eg, arterial and/or venous thrombosis, specific pregnancy morbidities) ( figure 1 ).

● Help diagnose patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or screen patients with known SLE for aPL antibodies ( table 1 and table 2 ).

● Evaluate a persistently prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) in individuals undergoing coagulation testing for another reason. An aPL antibody can cause a falsely prolonged aPTT by interacting with protein/aPL complexes in the sample/reagent.

Interpretation of a positive aPL test takes into account the type, isotype, titer, persistency, and number of positive aPL tests. aPL may be present with conditions other than APS or systemic autoimmune disease (eg, acute infection, HIV infection, malignancy, exposure to certain drugs). (See "Antiphospholipid syndrome: Diagnosis", section on 'Other conditions associated with antiphospholipid antibodies' .)

SUSPECTED ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME  — 

APS is a clinical syndrome defined by two major components ( figure 1 ):

● Occurrence of at least one clinical feature – venous or arterial thrombosis, or a specific pregnancy morbidity.

● Presence in the plasma of one or more of the following aPLs on two or more occasions at least 12 weeks apart:

• A positive LA test.

• A positive aCL (immunoglobulin G [IgG] and/or immunoglobulin M [IgM]) titer ≥40 units/mL or greater than the 99 th percentile for the laboratory testing.

• A positive anti-beta2-GP I (IgG and/or IgM) titer ≥40 units/mL or greater than the 99 th percentile for the laboratory testing.

APS occurs either as a primary condition or in the setting of an underlying disease, usually SLE. (See "Clinical manifestations of antiphospholipid syndrome" and "Antiphospholipid syndrome: Diagnosis", section on 'Diagnostic evaluation' .)

Confirm initial positive tests  —  If at least one aPL test is positive in a patient who meets at least one of the clinical criteria ( figure 1 ), the patient has possible APS. Tests for aPL must be repeated to confirm the diagnosis of APS. Life-saving interventions and presumptive therapy of complications of APS (eg, anticoagulation for thrombi) should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

Obtain in ≥12 weeks:

● All three aPL assays in patients not receiving anticoagulants.  

● aCL and anti-beta2-GP I enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) in patients still receiving anticoagulants. aCL and anti-beta2GPI antibody test results are unaffected by anticoagulation. However, LA testing in the acute setting may be inaccurate in patients receiving an anticoagulant or in the setting of a large thrombus. Communicate with a consulting specialist. (See "Clinical use of coagulation tests", section on 'Patient on anticoagulant' .)

It may be appropriate to pursue additional laboratory testing or to evaluate patients for other possible causes of thromboembolism and/or adverse pregnancy outcomes while awaiting confirmatory aPL testing.

Review tests usually available at the time of presentation:

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

● Prothrombin time (PT)/international normalized ratio (INR) and aPTT

● Serum creatinine

● Urinalysis

Specialty consultation is advised for individuals with any of the following:

● Suspected APS.

● aPL-related clinical events (eg, venous or arterial thrombosis) and aCL and/or anti-beta2-GP 1 antibody levels that are elevated above the reported reference range but below 40 units/mL.

● Clinical features that may be associated with APS even in the absence of a vascular thrombosis or APS-defining pregnancy morbidity (eg, livedo reticularis [and particularly livedo racemose], otherwise unexplained thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, valvular heart disease, or renal thrombotic microangiopathy).

Confirmatory aPL tests positive  —  The diagnosis of APS is confirmed if at least one clinical criterion is met and at least one aPL test on two or more occasions ≥12 weeks apart is positive, as long as there is no alternative diagnosis to explain the clinical findings ( figure 1 ).

Confirmatory aPL tests negative  —  Transiently elevated levels of IgG or IgM aCL, as well as a positive LA test, can occur in the setting of some infections or drug exposures. Transient aPL antibodies of the IgM isotype of aCL are rarely associated with thrombosis. Assess for other causes of thrombosis or pregnancy complications.

For the majority of patients who have negative confirmatory testing, further repeat antibody testing is usually not indicated unless the clinical suspicion for APS is especially high. In this clinical setting, obtain consultation with a clinician with expertise in APS.

SUSPECTED SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS  — 

The immunologic criteria for the diagnosis of SLE include detection of aPL ( table 1 and table 2 ). Thus, aPL are often obtained as part of the diagnostic evaluation of SLE. Thromboembolic disease can complicate SLE, particularly in the context of aPL. (See "Systemic lupus erythematosus in adults: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Laboratory testing' and "Systemic lupus erythematosus in adults: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Clinical manifestations' .)

In patients with suspected SLE, review other tests usually available at the time of presentation:

● CBC with differential and platelet count may reveal leukopenia, mild anemia, and/or thrombocytopenia

● Serum creatinine may suggest kidney dysfunction

● Urinalysis with urine sediment may reveal hematuria, pyuria, proteinuria, and or cellular casts

● Serum protein electrophoresis may demonstrate a hypergammaglobulinemia that is suggestive of a systemic inflammatory process

In addition to the routine laboratories described above, the following laboratory tests support the diagnosis of SLE if abnormal:

● Antinuclear antibodies (ANAs, ideally by indirect immunofluorescence testing)

● Anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA)

● CH50 or C3 and C4 complement levels

● Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and/or C-reactive protein (CRP)

● Urine protein-to-creatinine ratio

Obtain rheumatologic consultation.

POSITIVE aPL WITHOUT CLINICAL FEATURES OF APS OR SLE  — 

Healthy individuals occasionally have transient aPL. This situation includes individuals undergoing coagulation screening for an unrelated indication who are found to have an LA as the cause of a persistently prolonged aPTT. Repeating the abnormal tests to verify persistence is the appropriate first step. (See "Antiphospholipid syndrome: Diagnosis", section on 'Positive antiphospholipid antibodies without antiphospholipid syndrome' .)

Repeat testing provides information about potential risk assessment; multiple strongly positive tests, particularly in individuals with other thrombosis risk factors, are associated with an increased risk of developing APS. In contrast, a single borderline positive test that is negative on confirmation is likely to be clinically irrelevant.

aPL may be seen following exposure to certain drugs (eg, amoxicillin, procainamide, phenytoin) or with some acute bacterial or viral infections.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

LA is a functional assay, reported as either positive or negative. aCL and anti-beta2-GP I are expressed in GPL (IgG phospholipid) standardized international units and in MPL (IgM phospholipid) standardized international units. The reference range depends on the assay and the laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
