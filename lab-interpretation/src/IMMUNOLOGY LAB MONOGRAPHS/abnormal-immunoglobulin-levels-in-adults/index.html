INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Quantitative serum immunoglobulins G, A, and M (IgG, IgA, and IgM) are routinely measured as part of the evaluation of monoclonal gammopathies (eg, multiple myeloma [MM]) and immune deficiencies (eg, recurrent infection, often sinus or pulmonary). It is important to understand the clinical indication for ordering immunoglobulin levels, as a small percentage of the otherwise normal population statistically will fall either above or below laboratory reference ranges.

Abnormal immunoglobulin levels must be interpreted in the context of the reason for the test, a comprehensive history (including review of medications) and physical examination, initial laboratory tests, and whether the patient is clinically ill. Further evaluation depends on whether immunoglobulin levels are high or low.

Immunoglobulin(s) high  —  Increased immunoglobulin levels may be due to polyclonal (reactive) and/or monoclonal (or, rarely, biclonal) plasma proliferative processes ( table 1 ). Electrophoresis is usually required to interpret an elevated immunoglobulin class as polyclonal versus monoclonal. If there is a monoclonal elevation of immunoglobulins, immunofixation is also required to characterize the monoclonal protein (M-protein).

Review (obtain):

● Complete blood count with differential and platelet count.

● Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and/or C-reactive protein (CRP).

● Chemistry panels, including kidney function, glucose, albumin, total protein, and liver biochemical tests.

● Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) with immunofixation. (See "Abnormal serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) in adults" .)

● Twenty-four-hour urine protein electrophoresis (UPEP) with immunofixation or serum free light chains (FLCs). (See "Abnormal serum free light chains in adults" .)

M-protein identified  —  If an M-protein is identified (eg, serum IgG, IgA, or IgM monoclonal immunoglobulin), diagnostic considerations include monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), MM, macroglobulinemia, primary systemic amyloidosis, and related paraproteinemias or dysproteinemias. Obtain hematology consultation to guide further evaluation and management:

● If IgG or IgA, consider MM or MGUS. (See "Multiple myeloma: Clinical features, laboratory manifestations, and diagnosis" and "Diagnosis of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance" .)

● If IgM, consider Waldenström macroglobulinemia. (See "Epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis of Waldenström macroglobulinemia" .)

● If light chain fragments are detected in both serum and urine immunofixation and serum FLC assay, consider primary systemic amyloidosis. (See "Overview of amyloidosis", section on 'AL amyloidosis' .)

M-protein not identified  —  If there is no evidence of an M-protein as the cause of high immunoglobulins, the patient has a polyclonal process, most often due to infectious, inflammatory, or reactive processes. (See "Laboratory methods for analyzing monoclonal proteins", section on 'Polyclonal gammopathy' .)

Important examples include:

● Liver disease – Cirrhosis, chronic hepatitis

● Immune-mediated disease – Rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus

● Chronic infection – Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), osteomyelitis, sepsis

● Hematologic disorders, hematologic and nonhematologic malignancy

Evaluate and manage the underlying cause(s) usually apparent at the time of clinical presentation. Consider specialty consultation based upon additional clinical history and laboratory findings.

Immunoglobulin(s) low  —  The presence of low immunoglobulin(s) raises the possibility of an immunodeficiency disorder. In adults, low immunoglobulins are usually acquired (secondary immunodeficiency). The etiology of secondary immunodeficiency may be apparent at the time of clinical presentation. (See "Clinical manifestations, epidemiology, and diagnosis of common variable immunodeficiency in adults", section on 'Secondary hypogammaglobulinemia' .)

Review (obtain):

● Complete blood count with differential and platelet count.

● ESR or CRP – Nonspecific elevations in acute-phase reactants are more likely to be seen in the setting of infectious and inflammatory disorders and suggest the need for further evaluation.

● Chemistry panels, including kidney function, glucose, albumin, total protein, and liver biochemical tests.

● Urinalysis with microscopy for evaluation of proteinuria, casts, or cells.

● HIV screening. (See "Screening and diagnostic testing for HIV infection in adults", section on 'Indications for testing' .)

● Repeat immunoglobulin levels to confirm that the values are persistently low and that a laboratory error has not occurred. Measurement should be performed approximately three months after resolution of any concomitant illness that could cause secondarily low immunoglobulin levels (eg, nephrotic syndrome or protein-losing enteropathy).

Secondary immunodeficiency  —  Diagnostic considerations include any of the following acquired processes:

● Infection (eg, HIV).

● Metabolic disturbance (eg, diabetes, kidney disease).

● Immunosuppression due to drugs (most commonly B cell-depleting therapies [eg, rituximab, CAR T- and other cell therapies, bi-specific T cell engagers], FcRn monoclonal therapies, cytotoxic therapies, long-term glucocorticoids, and select antiepileptics).

● Malignancies.

● Immune-mediated disease (eg, inflammatory bowel disease).

● Loss of immunoglobulins (eg, through gastrointestinal tract, chylothorax, urine, skin [eg, burns]).

Primary immunodeficiency  —  If primary immunodeficiency is suspected and a secondary cause for hypogammaglobulinemia is eliminated, more advanced immunologic tests are performed, typically in a graded fashion and in consultation with an immunologist. (See "Primary humoral immunodeficiencies: An overview" and "Laboratory evaluation of the immune system", section on 'Antibody deficiency and defects' .)

Evaluation and management in consultation with a clinical immunologist may include:

● Assessment of vaccine responsiveness and recommendations for vaccination.

● Genetic testing to identify the cause of a primary immunodeficiency.

● Evaluation and monitoring for associated disorders (eg, autoimmune, gastrointestinal, chronic lung disease, and malignant disorders).

● Administration of immunoglobulin replacement therapy or prophylactic antibiotics, as indicated.

● Assessment of eligibility for bone marrow stem cell transplantation or gene therapy.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for IgG in adults is approximately 800 to 1500 mg/dL, for IgA 90 to 325 mg/dL, and for IgM 45 to 150 mg/dL. There are several methods for determining serum immunoglobulin levels, and laboratories use different systems. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the age - adjusted reference range reported by the laboratory.
