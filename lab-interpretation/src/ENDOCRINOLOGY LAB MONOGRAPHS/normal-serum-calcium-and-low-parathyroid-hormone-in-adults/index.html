INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The combination of a normal serum calcium and a low parathyroid hormone (PTH) level is a relatively uncommon finding. It may come to medical attention during the evaluation of a previously noted abnormal serum calcium (and repeat calcium is now normal) or during the evaluation of low bone mineral density, at which time PTH levels were obtained.

It is important to confirm that the serum total calcium concentration accurately reflects the physiologically important ionized (or free) calcium concentration. As an example, in a patient with hypoalbuminemia, the total calcium may be normal, but the corrected calcium (or ionized calcium) may be high. Even in the presence of a normal serum albumin, changes in blood pH can alter the equilibrium constant of the albumin-calcium complex, with acidosis reducing the binding and alkalosis enhancing it. In critically ill or postsurgical patients, in particular, correcting total calcium for albumin is not necessarily accurate because of changes in pH and affinity of calcium binding (see "Relation between total and ionized serum calcium concentrations" ):

● Obtain repeat PTH with calcium and albumin or with ionized calcium:

• In patients with hypo- or hyperalbuminemia, the measured total calcium concentration can be corrected for the abnormality in albumin, using ( calculator 1 ) for mg/dL or ( calculator 2 ) for standard international units. There are a number of formulas that have been used to correct the total calcium for serum albumin concentrations, but none appear to be universally acceptable when examined for their correlation with ionized calcium.

• If a laboratory known to measure ionized calcium reliably is available, measure the serum ionized calcium in patients with acid base disturbances.

● If available, review previous serum calcium values for trends or previous high or low values.

If PTH is persistently low and repeat calcium (corrected for albumin or ionized) is normal, causes may include:

● Early hypoparathyroidism (typically there is a history of head and neck surgery or autoimmune disease, and calcium is in the lower end of the reference range). (See "Hypoparathyroidism" .)

● High vitamin D and calcium intake in supplements or milk ingestion.

● Calcitriol intake (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D suppresses PTH, but calcium may be normal).

● Hypomagnesemia. (See "Hypomagnesemia: Clinical manifestations of magnesium depletion", section on 'Abnormalities of calcium metabolism' .)

● Idiopathic hypercalciuria: Excessive intestinal calcium absorption may result in a rise in serum calcium (but within the normal range) and suppression of PTH. (See "Kidney stones in adults: Epidemiology and risk factors", section on 'High urine calcium' .)

Evaluation to determine the potential etiology includes:

● Review history, including medications

● Obtain 24-hour urinary calcium, creatinine

● Repeat calcium (corrected for albumin or ionized) and PTH in approximately three months

The evaluation of a low PTH with a high or low calcium is reviewed in other Lab Interpretation monographs. (See "High serum calcium and low or low-normal parathyroid hormone in adults" and "Low serum calcium and low or low-normal parathyroid hormone in adults" .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal range for serum calcium is approximately 8.6 to 10.2 mg/dL (2.15 to 2.54 mmol/L). In patients with hypo- or hyperalbuminemia, the measured calcium concentration should be corrected for the abnormality in albumin, using ( calculator 1 ) for mg/dL or ( calculator 2 ) for standard international units. The normal range for intact PTH is approximately 10 to 65 pg/mL (1.1 to 6.9 pmol/L). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
