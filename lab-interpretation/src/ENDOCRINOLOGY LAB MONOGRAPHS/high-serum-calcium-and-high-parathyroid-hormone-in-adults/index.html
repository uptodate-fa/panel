IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

The degree of hypercalcemia, along with the rate of rise of serum calcium concentration, determines symptoms and urgency of therapy. An acute rise to 12 to 14 mg/dL (3 to 3.5 mmol/L) may cause marked changes in sensorium, which requires more aggressive measures.

Rapidly identify and begin immediate treatment if any of the following are present (see "Treatment of hypercalcemia", section on 'Definitions of severity'):

●Symptoms of hypercalcemia (lethargy, confusion, stupor, coma, muscle weakness) regardless of the degree of hypercalcemia.

●Complications of hypercalcemia (acute kidney injury, hypokalemia and other metabolic abnormalities predisposing to delirium and/or cardiac arrhythmias).

●Total calcium concentration >14 mg/dL (3.5 mmol/L) regardless of symptoms.

This degree of hypercalcemia in the setting of an elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) level may be the result of parathyroid crisis, which is rare. Parathyroid crisis may occur in patients with previously documented primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) that is not severe. In others, it may be the first evidence of parathyroid disease. Parathyroid carcinoma may present with severe hypercalcemia and markedly elevated PTH levels.

It is uncommon for patients with hypercalcemia of malignancy to have elevated PTH levels, but this finding may occur rarely in individuals with hypercalcemia of malignancy and concomitant PHPT or in individuals with PTH-secreting tumors, which are also rare. (See "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and evaluation" and "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Clinical manifestations", section on 'Parathyroid crisis'.)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Perform additional testing to confirm the diagnosis and to guide management.

Confirm diagnosis of primary hyperparathyroidism — Review medications to assess for drugs that may be associated with PHPT:

●Thiazide diuretics – Thiazide diuretics reduce urinary calcium excretion and, therefore, can cause mild hypercalcemia (up to 11.5 mg/dL [2.9 mmol/L]). They can also unmask previously unrecognized mild hyperparathyroidism. If a patient is taking a thiazide diuretic, discontinue if possible and remeasure calcium and PTH three months later. Persistent hypercalcemia (with elevated or high-normal PTH) after drug withdrawal suggests that the thiazide has unmasked PHPT. (See "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and evaluation", section on 'Drugs'.)

●Lithium – Lithium therapy can cause mild hypercalcemia, most likely due to increased secretion of PTH due to an increase in the set point at which calcium suppresses PTH release. If the lithium can be stopped without exacerbating the psychiatric condition, the hypercalcemia may resolve within one to four weeks, particularly if the duration of lithium use had been relatively short (eg, less than a few years). Lithium can also unmask previously unrecognized mild hyperparathyroidism. (See "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Pathogenesis and etiology", section on 'Lithium therapy'.)

In patients with persistent hypercalcemia:

●A frankly elevated PTH concentration is likely the result of PHPT.

●A minimally elevated PTH level is also most likely the result of PHPT or, less commonly, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH).

To help distinguish between these two possibilities in patients with minimally elevated PTH levels and to inform management of patients with PHPT, measure:

●Urinary calcium excretion (24-hour urinary calcium and creatinine or calcium-to-creatinine ratio)

●25-hydroxyvitamin D

Before the diagnosis of FHH can be contemplated, any coexisting vitamin D deficiency must be corrected, as this can lower urinary calcium excretion in patients with PHPT. (See "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and evaluation", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

Evaluation to inform management — For patients diagnosed with asymptomatic PHPT, additional evaluation is necessary to make subsequent management decisions. (See "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and evaluation", section on 'Additional evaluation to determine management' and "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Management".)

Although patients with symptomatic PHPT should have parathyroid surgery, the widespread identification of asymptomatic individuals raises the question of the need for and timing of surgical intervention in this population. In order to make management recommendations for patients with asymptomatic PHPT, measure:

●24-hour urinary calcium and creatinine or calcium-to-creatinine ratio (if not previously measured)

●Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (if not previously measured)

●Serum creatinine and estimate the glomerular filtration rate to assess for renal compromise

●Bone mineral density (BMD) to determine if it is low (the presence of low BMD is important for making recommendations for parathyroid surgery or observation with monitoring in asymptomatic patients)

Some, but not all, UpToDate authors and editors also obtain imaging studies of the following:

●Kidneys to assess for occult kidney stones

●Spine to assess for asymptomatic vertebral compression fracture

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal range for serum calcium is approximately 8.6 to 10.2 mg/dL (2.15 to 2.54 mmol/L). In patients with hypo- or hyperalbuminemia, the measured calcium concentration should be corrected for the abnormality in albumin, using (calculator 1) for mg/dL or (calculator 2) for standard international units. The normal range for intact PTH is approximately 10 to 65 pg/mL (1.1 to 6.9 pmol/L). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
