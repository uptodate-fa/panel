INITIAL EVALUATION — 

A high serum parathyroid hormone (PTH) concentration in a patient with confirmed hypocalcemia is most consistent with secondary hyperparathyroidism.

Secondary hyperparathyroidism occurs when the parathyroid gland appropriately responds to a reduced level of extracellular calcium. PTH concentrations rise, and calcium is mobilized by increasing intestinal absorption and bone resorption. Thus, it is characterized biochemically by elevated PTH and normal or low corrected calcium concentrations.

Secondary hyperparathyroidism may occur in patients with (table 1) (see "Etiology of hypocalcemia in adults" and "Overview of chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD)", section on 'Disorders of calcium balance'):

●Acute or chronic kidney disease – Most common

●Inadequate calcium intake or absorption (eg, vitamin D deficiency, celiac disease, bariatric surgery)

●Reduced bone resorption (due to potent antiresorptive drugs such as bisphosphonates, denosumab), particularly in the setting of low calcium intake

●End-organ (kidney, bone) resistance to PTH (eg, pseudohypoparathyroidism)

Most patients have chronic hypocalcemia and are asymptomatic or only mildly symptomatic. Review history, including medications.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal range for serum calcium is approximately 8.6 to 10.2 mg/dL (2.15 to 2.54 mmol/L) and for intact PTH approximately 10 to 65 pg/mL (1.1 to 6.9 pmol/L). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
