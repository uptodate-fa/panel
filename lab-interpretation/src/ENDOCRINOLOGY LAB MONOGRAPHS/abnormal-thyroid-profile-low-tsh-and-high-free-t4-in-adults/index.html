ALGORITHM  — 

The evaluation of a low thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) depends upon whether the TSH was measured to monitor levothyroxine therapy ( algorithm 1 ) or to evaluate for thyroid dysfunction ( algorithm 2 ).

Because of the changes in thyroid physiology during pregnancy, thyroid function tests in pregnant women should be interpreted using population-based, trimester-specific reference ranges for TSH and assay method and trimester-specific reference ranges for serum free thyroxine (T4). (See "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Trimester-specific reference ranges' .)

MONITOR LEVOTHYROXINE THERAPY  — 

One of the more common reasons for measuring TSH is to monitor levothyroxine therapy ( algorithm 1 ):

● Primary hypothyroidism – For patients with treated primary hypothyroidism, therapy may be monitored with the TSH alone. Most patients with low TSH typically require a dose reduction. If the TSH is slightly below normal (eg, 0.05 to 0.3 mU/L), a small dose reduction of 12 to 25 mcg/day is usually sufficient, and measurement of free T4 is not necessary. For TSH values below 0.05 mU/L, measurement of free T4 is helpful to assess the degree of overtreatment and appropriate percentage reduction in dose. (Some laboratories reflexively measure the free T4 whenever the TSH is abnormal). If the free T4 is only slightly elevated, a small dose reduction of 12 to 25 mcg/day may be sufficient. An alternative is to reduce the dose by 15 percent by omitting one pill a week. If the free T4 is twice the average free T4 (eg, 2.8 ng/dL in an assay with a normal range of 0.9 to 1.9 ng/dL), it may be necessary to reduce the dose by 50 percent (if the target is a mid-normal free T4).

TSH should always be repeated six to eight weeks after dose adjustments to determine the need for further adjustment. (See "Treatment of primary hypothyroidism in adults", section on 'Adjustment of maintenance dose' .)

● Thyroid cancer – For patients taking levothyroxine to prevent recurrence of thyroid cancer, therapy may be monitored with the TSH alone. The goal is often an undetectable or subnormal serum TSH concentration, depending on the clinical situation.

The free T4 may be slightly elevated when the TSH is purposely suppressed to subnormal levels in patients with thyroid cancer. If the goal for TSH is <0.05 mU/L, the free T4 may be normal or elevated, and we measure the free T4 to assess whether therapy may be excessive. If the free T4 is only slightly elevated, it may not be possible to reduce the dose and maintain a TSH <0.05 mU/L. If the free T4 level is very high, the dose should be reduced to the lowest dose that is able to maintain the TSH at goal. (See "Laboratory assessment of thyroid function", section on 'Monitoring levothyroxine therapy' and "Differentiated thyroid cancer: Overview of management", section on 'Ongoing management based on dynamic risk stratification' .)

● Central hypothyroidism – For patients with central (secondary) hypothyroidism due to pituitary or hypothalamic disease, the dose of T4 should be adjusted according to the patient's symptoms and serum free T4 (not TSH), aiming to maintain the serum free T4 concentration slightly higher than mid-normal. Serum TSH cannot be used to monitor therapy, as it is suppressed to <0.1 mU/L in nearly all patients taking doses of T4 that raise their serum free T4 concentrations to normal. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Treatment' .)

EVALUATE THYROID DYSFUNCTION  — 

Except for laboratory error or interference (eg, biotin supplements), all patients with low serum TSH and high free T4 (and/or triiodothyronine [T3]) concentrations have overt primary hyperthyroidism ( algorithm 2 ). Some clinicians measure total T3 if not previously measured because the pattern of thyroid function test abnormalities may suggest a specific diagnosis. In addition, T3 may be useful for monitoring. (See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Determining the etiology' and "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Diagnosis' .)

Ingestion of a single dose of biotin (5 to 10 mg) can cause spurious results in certain commercial thyroid test immunoassays (that use biotinylated antibodies). The resulting biochemical findings suggest a diagnosis of hyperthyroidism; however, discontinuation of biotin supplements results in resolution of the erroneous biochemical abnormalities. In patients taking biotin supplements, thyroid tests should be repeated at least two days after discontinuation of the supplements.

Once the diagnosis of hyperthyroidism has been established, the cause of the hyperthyroidism should be determined, if not apparent based on clinical presentation. Diagnostic testing can include the following, depending on available expertise and resources:

● Measurement of thyrotropin receptor antibodies (TRAb, also called TSI, TBII, or TBI)

● Determination of the radioactive iodine uptake (contraindicated during pregnancy and lactation) ( table 1 )

● Measurement of thyroidal blood flow on ultrasonography

(See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Determining the etiology' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

In nonpregnant adults, the normal reference range for serum TSH is approximately 0.5 to 4.5 mU/L, and for serum free T4, approximately 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL (10.3 to 23.2 pmol/L). The normal reference ranges can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
