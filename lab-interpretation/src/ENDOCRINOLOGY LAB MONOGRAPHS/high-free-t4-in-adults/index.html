EVALUATE THYROID DYSFUNCTION — 

In the absence of pituitary or hypothalamic disease, if steady-state conditions exist, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) should be sufficient to assess thyroid function. Otherwise, free thyroxine (T4) is measured with TSH, or, if the TSH has already been determined to be abnormal, free T4 is measured with a repeat TSH, and they are interpreted together. If the TSH is subnormal, measurement of triiodothyronine (T3) may also be useful.

Free T4 assays measure the free or unbound hormone that is available for uptake into cells, whereas total T4 assays measure both bound and unbound (free) T4. Most commercial laboratories use an automated free T4 assay. Total T4 is not typically measured, although it is useful during pregnancy when free T4 assays may be inaccurate due to changes in binding proteins. (See "Laboratory assessment of thyroid function", section on 'Laboratory tests used to assess thyroid function' and "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Thyroid adaptation during normal pregnancy'.)

Nonpregnant adults

TSH low — Except for laboratory error or interference (eg, biotin supplements), all patients with low serum TSH and high free T4 (and/or T3 concentrations) have overt primary hyperthyroidism. (See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

Ingestion of a single dose of biotin (5 to 10 mg), marketed over the counter to improve hair, skin, and nails, can cause spurious results in certain commercial thyroid test immunoassays (that use biotinylated antibodies). The resulting biochemical findings suggest a diagnosis of hyperthyroidism; however, discontinuation of biotin supplements results in resolution of the erroneous biochemical abnormalities. In patients taking biotin supplements, thyroid tests should be repeated at least two days after discontinuation of the supplements.

Once the diagnosis of hyperthyroidism has been established, the cause of the hyperthyroidism should be determined, if not apparent based on clinical presentation. Diagnostic testing can include the following, depending on available expertise and resources:

●Measurement of thyrotropin receptor antibodies (TRAb, also called TSI, TBII, or TBI)

●Determination of the radioactive iodine uptake (contraindicated during pregnancy and lactation) (table 1)

●Measurement of thyroidal blood flow on ultrasonography

(See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Determining the etiology'.)

TSH normal — In the setting of a high free T4, a serum TSH within the normal reference range (unsuppressed) is usually abnormal, particularly in a patient with symptoms of hyperthyroidism (eg, palpitations, tremor, heat intolerance):

●Patients with clinical signs of hyperthyroidism, a "normal" (unsuppressed) TSH, and a high free T4 may have:

•TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma

•Resistance to thyroid hormone

Measure:

•Repeat TSH

•Repeat free T4

•Total or free T3

If an abnormality persists, obtain endocrinology consultation for further evaluation and management.

●Patients who appear euthyroid on examination may have euthyroid hyperthyroxinemia due to one of several abnormalities in serum thyroid hormone-binding proteins that result in high serum total (and sometimes free) T4 concentrations and may or may not (depending on the abnormality) also cause high T3 concentrations. The most common cause of high T4 (and high T3), but not high free T4 (and free T3), concentrations in most assays is thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) excess due to estrogens (eg, pregnancy and oral contraceptives).

Measure:

•Repeat TSH

•Total T4

•TBG (or, if available, a T3 uptake, with calculation of the free T4 index using the thyroid hormone-binding ratio (THBR) or index (THBI)

A persistently normal TSH, high total T4, low T3 uptake or THBR, and normal or mildly elevated serum free T4 index is consistent with euthyroid hyperthyroxinemia. The free T4 should be normal, but the results are assay dependent. Although many assays will report slightly abnormal values, measurement of free T4 by equilibrium dialysis by a reference lab is usually normal. Patients with euthyroid hyperthyroxinemia do not require treatment. (See "Euthyroid hyperthyroxinemia and hypothyroxinemia", section on 'Binding protein abnormalities' and "Euthyroid hyperthyroxinemia and hypothyroxinemia", section on 'Determining the etiology'.)

Amiodarone therapy may also cause an elevated free T4 with a normal (or elevated) TSH, particularly early in the course of therapy. (See 'TSH high' below.)

TSH high — The combination of high TSH and high free T4 is relatively uncommon, and patients with these thyroid function tests may have clinical signs and symptoms of hyperthyroidism (eg, palpitations, tremor, heat intolerance).

Patients with high TSH and free T4 may have:

●Nonadherence to treatment for hypothyroidism with increased intake of levothyroxine prior to blood sampling (free T4 may be high or normal)

●TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma (TSH may be high or inappropriately normal)

●Resistance to thyroid hormone

●Assay interference due to the presence of heterophilic antibodies or autoantibodies to TSH

Amiodarone therapy may also cause an elevated TSH and free T4, particularly early in the course of therapy. In patients with normal underlying thyroid function, serum TSH normalizes after three to six months of therapy, whereas free T4 may remain slightly elevated or in the upper normal range. (See "Amiodarone and thyroid dysfunction", section on 'Transient changes in thyroid function tests'.)

Measure:

●Repeat TSH

●Repeat free T4

●Total or free T3

If abnormality persists, obtain endocrinology consultation for further evaluation and management. (See "TSH-secreting pituitary adenomas", section on 'Diagnosis' and "Resistance to thyroid hormone and other defects in thyroid hormone action", section on 'Laboratory findings'.)

Pregnant women — Because of the changes in thyroid physiology during pregnancy, thyroid function tests should be interpreted using population-based, trimester-specific reference ranges for TSH and assay method and trimester-specific reference ranges for serum free T4. Free T4 measurements may be unreliable in some patients in the second and third trimesters due to changes in binding proteins during pregnancy and other factors. In such settings where free T4 measurements appear discordant with TSH measurements, total T4 should also be measured.

The upper reference ranges for total T4 and total T3 shift upward by approximately 5 percent per week, beginning at week 7. At approximately 16 weeks, normal total T4 (and total T3) levels can be as high as 1.5-fold times the upper limit of the reference ranges for nonpregnant women (due to TBG excess). Free T4 measurements may be mildly elevated in the first trimester because of thyroidal stimulation by human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). (See "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Trimester-specific reference ranges'.)

If the free T4 (or total T4) exceeds the normal range for pregnancy:

●Measure repeat TSH, repeat free T4, and free T3 (or total T4 and total T3).

●If there is a clinical suspicion of hyperthyroidism, obtain consultation with a clinician experienced with managing thyroid dysfunction during pregnancy.

(See "Hyperthyroidism during pregnancy: Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and causes", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

MONITOR LEVOTHYROXINE THERAPY — 

Measurement of free T4 alone is used to titrate the levothyroxine dose in patients with central hypothyroidism due to hypothalamic or pituitary disease. Free T4 is less often used to monitor levothyroxine treatment in patients with primary hypothyroidism or thyroid cancer:

●Central hypothyroidism – For patients with central hypothyroidism and high free T4, the dose of levothyroxine should be reduced, and the free T4 measured again six to eight weeks after dose reduction, aiming to maintain the serum free T4 concentration in the upper 50 percent of the normal range. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Treatment'.)

●Primary hypothyroidism or thyroid cancer – It is usually not necessary to measure free T4 in patients being monitored for the treatment of primary hypothyroidism or thyroid cancer if the goal for treatment is a TSH within the normal range. However, in patients with thyroid cancer whose TSH goals are subnormal ("suppressive therapy," if the TSH is below 0.05 mU/L), it is helpful to measure the free T4 to assess the extent to which treatment may be excessive.

The normal range for free T4 in patients taking levothyroxine is shifted to slightly higher values, and minimal elevations in free T4 may be associated with normal TSH values. When the goal is a subnormal TSH in a patient with thyroid cancer, the decision to reduce the levothyroxine dose depends on the severity of their disease and the levels of TSH and free T4. If the levothyroxine dose is reduced, TSH and free T4 should be remeasured approximately two months later to determine if the desired therapeutic target has been achieved. (See "Treatment of primary hypothyroidism in adults", section on 'Adjustment of maintenance dose' and "Differentiated thyroid cancer: Overview of management", section on 'Ongoing management based on dynamic risk stratification'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for serum free T4 in nonpregnant adults is approximately 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL (10.3 to 23.3 pmol/L), and for total T4, 5 to 12 mcg/dL (64 to 154 nmol/L). The normal reference ranges can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
