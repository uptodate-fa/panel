IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

A low or inappropriately normal serum parathyroid hormone (PTH) concentration in a patient with confirmed hypocalcemia is most consistent with hypoparathyroidism. The degree of hypocalcemia, along with the rate of fall of serum calcium concentration, determines symptoms and urgency of therapy. (See "Hypoparathyroidism", section on 'Diagnosis and evaluation' and "Treatment of hypocalcemia", section on 'Severe symptomatic and/or acute hypocalcemia' .)

Rapidly identify and begin immediate treatment with intravenous (IV) calcium if any of the following are present:

● Severe symptoms of hypocalcemia – Carpopedal spasm, tetany, seizures.

● Complications of hypocalcemia – Heart failure or prolonged QT interval.

● Acute decrease in corrected serum calcium concentration to ≤7.5 mg/dL (≤1.9 mmol/L), which can increase the risk of complications. • Measure serum calcium every four to six hours.

• Review electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine. Confirm normal serum potassium, as profound hypokalemia may induce muscle cramping and, infrequently, tetany.

• Measure serum magnesium and replete if low. (See 'Assess for hypomagnesemia' below.)

IV calcium is not warranted as initial therapy for asymptomatic hypocalcemia in patients with impaired renal function in whom correction of hyperphosphatemia and of low circulating 1,25-dihyroxyvitamin D are usually the primary goals. (See "Overview of chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD)", section on 'Disorders of calcium balance' .)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to identify and treat the underlying cause ( table 1 ). A low (or inappropriately normal) serum PTH concentration in a patient with hypocalcemia is most consistent with hypoparathyroidism or hypomagnesemia. Autosomal dominant hypocalcemia, a disorder characterized by an activating mutation in the calcium-sensing receptor gene or its downstream pathway, is a rare diagnosis. (See "Hypoparathyroidism", section on 'Differential diagnosis' .)

Assess for hypomagnesemia  —  Obtain the following tests if not already measured:

● Magnesium

● Phosphate (typically elevated in hypoparathyroidism)

● Electrolytes, BUN, creatinine

Hypomagnesemia (plasma magnesium concentration below 1 mEq/L [1.2 mg/dL or 0.5 mmol/L]) causes hypocalcemia by inducing PTH resistance or deficiency. Mild hypomagnesemia (plasma magnesium concentration between 1.1 and 1.3 mEq/L [1.3 to 1.6 mg/dL or 0.55 to 0.65 mmol/L]) can also lower the plasma calcium concentration, but the change in calcium is quite small (0.2 mg/dL [0.05 mmol/L]).

Occasionally, patients with normal plasma magnesium concentrations have hypocalcemia that improves with magnesium therapy, possibly due to cellular magnesium depletion. Thus, magnesium supplementation may be indicated in patients with unexplained hypocalcemia who are at risk for hypomagnesemia (eg, chronic malabsorption or alcoholism), even when serum magnesium concentrations are normal.

Hypocalcemia should resolve within minutes or hours after restoration of normal serum magnesium concentrations if hypomagnesemia was the cause of the hypocalcemia. (See "Hypomagnesemia: Evaluation and treatment" .)

Hypoparathyroidism  —  Most patients with persistent hypocalcemia, a low or inappropriately normal PTH level, hyperphosphatemia, and normal magnesium have hypoparathyroidism (PTH deficiency).

Acquired hypoparathyroidism is most often the result of postsurgical or autoimmune damage to the parathyroid glands ( table 2 ). (See "Hypoparathyroidism", section on 'Diagnosis and evaluation' .)

Once hypoparathyroidism is diagnosed, obtain (see "Hypoparathyroidism", section on 'Management' ):

● 24-hour urine calcium and creatinine to establish a baseline urine calcium excretion prior to initiating therapy

● Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25[OH]D) to correct underlying vitamin D deficiency, which is prevalent in the population

● Autoantibodies against the parathyroid glands in selected patients with suspected autoimmune hypoparathyroidism (a personal or family history of autoimmune diseases, no history of head and neck surgery)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal range for serum calcium is approximately 8.6 to 10.2 mg/dL (2.15 to 2.54 mmol/L) and for intact PTH approximately 10 to 65 pg/mL (1.1 to 6.9 pmol/L). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
