INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Renin can be measured in terms of its enzymatic activity (plasma renin activity [PRA]) or its mass (plasma renin concentration [PRC]) (see "Assays of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system" ). Renin (PRA or PRC) and plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) are typically obtained in the evaluation of a patient with:

● A suspected secondary cause of hypertension (eg, primary hyperaldosteronism)

● Unexplained hypokalemia or hyperkalemia

● Adrenal insufficiency, to distinguish primary (high renin, low PAC) from secondary (normal renin, normal or low PAC) adrenal insufficiency

The most common cause of low renin and low PAC is a diet high in sodium content.

Associated with hyperkalemia  —  The combination of low renin and low PAC is often associated with hyperkalemia and a mild metabolic acidosis with a normal anion gap (ie, hyperchloremic acidosis). In this clinical setting, the most common cause is the syndrome of hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism. Potential causes of hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism include (see "Etiology, diagnosis, and treatment of hypoaldosteronism (type 4 RTA)" ):

● Diabetes and renal insufficiency – Most often occurs in patients 50 to 70 years of age with diabetic nephropathy or chronic tubulointerstitial disease who have mild to moderate renal insufficiency.

● Medications – May occur in patients taking nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or calcineurin inhibitors.

● Acute glomerulonephritis – May occur in patients with postinfectious glomerulonephritis. Recovery of renal function is associated with restoration of normal potassium balance.

The evaluation depends upon the clinical scenario and may include measurement of electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose, and glycated hemoglobin.

Associated with hypokalemia  —  The combination of suppressed renin and a low PAC and hypokalemia indicates the presence of some nonaldosterone mineralocorticoid. These patients are usually hypertensive. The differential diagnosis includes:

● Some types of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (deficiencies of 11-beta-hydroxylase [CYP11B1, P450c11] or 17-alpha-hydroxylase [CYP17, P450c17]) cause hypertension and hypokalemia because of hypersecretion of the mineralocorticoid deoxycorticosterone. Familial cortisol resistance has a similar presentation. (See "Uncommon congenital adrenal hyperplasias", section on '11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency' .)

● Chronic licorice root ingestion, the rare genetic syndrome of apparent mineralocorticoid excess, and severe cases of Cushing's syndrome, in which cortisol acts as the primary mineralocorticoid. In the last setting, there may also be hypersecretion of other mineralocorticoids, such as deoxycorticosterone and corticosterone. (See "Apparent mineralocorticoid excess syndromes (including chronic licorice ingestion)" .)

● A deoxycorticosterone-producing adrenal tumor, which can usually be detected by computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). (See "Diagnosis of primary aldosteronism", section on 'Other causes of hypertension and hypokalemia' .)

● Similar findings in the absence of mineralocorticoid excess are seen in Liddle's syndrome, which is caused by gain-of-function mutations in the beta or gamma subunits of the amiloride-sensitive collecting tubule sodium channel, resulting in increased sodium reabsorption, potassium wasting, hypertension, and hypokalemia. Clinical genetic testing is available. (See "Genetic disorders of the collecting tubule sodium channel: Liddle syndrome and pseudohypoaldosteronism type 1" .)

Obtain medication and dietary history. Assess for cushingoid features and signs of androgen excess. If there is clinical suspicion of apparent mineralocorticoid excess, obtain serum 11-deoxycorticosterone and corticotropin (ACTH) levels as well as 24-hour urinary cortisol and cortisone excretion. (See "Apparent mineralocorticoid excess syndromes (including chronic licorice ingestion)", section on 'Diagnosis' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range is based on a morning blood sample from a seated individual:

● PRA: approximately 1 to 4 ng/mL per hour (0.8 to 3 nmol/L per hour)

● PRC: 8 to 35 mU/L

● PAC with unrestricted salt intake: 5 to 30 ng/dL (140 to 830 pmol/L)

Levels of renin and PAC may be affected by sodium intake, age, time of day, upright posture, and medication use. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
