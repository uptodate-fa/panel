ALGORITHM — 

The evaluation of an abnormal thyroid profile depends upon whether the testing was performed to monitor levothyroxine therapy (algorithm 1) or to evaluate thyroid dysfunction (algorithm 2).

MONITOR LEVOTHYROXINE THERAPY — 

For patients with treated primary hypothyroidism, therapy may be monitored with the thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) alone (algorithm 1). If the TSH is above normal, some laboratories reflexively measure the free thyroxine (T4). (For patients with central [secondary] hypothyroidism, serum free T4 [not TSH] is used to monitor therapy).

Assess for:

●Compliance.

●Medications that may reduce absorption of levothyroxine (eg, calcium carbonate, iron sulfate, omeprazole) (table 1).

●Other factors that may increase thyroid hormone requirements (eg, weight gain, pregnancy, estrogen therapy).

Confirm that levothyroxine is taken daily on an empty stomach with water, ideally an hour before breakfast, and that medications that interfere with its absorption are taken several hours after the dose.

If adherence to the medication regimen is confirmed (and the most recent dose adjustment was at least four to six weeks prior), patients with primary hypothyroidism and high TSH typically require a dose increase. If the TSH is slightly elevated (eg, 5 to 10 mU/L), a small increase of 12 to 25 mcg/day is usually sufficient. If the TSH is ≥10 to <20 mU/L, a larger dose increase (eg, 25 to 50 mcg/day) is usually necessary. (See "Treatment of primary hypothyroidism in adults", section on 'Adjustment of maintenance dose'.)

Free T4 measurements can help determine appropriate dose increases when TSH is ≥20 mU/L, since the magnitude of TSH elevation in hypothyroid patients can be quite variable. If steady-state conditions exist, the TSH is ≥20 mU/L, and the free T4 is half the mean value of the normal reference range (eg, a free T4 of 0.7 ng/dL in an assay with a normal range of 0.9 to 1.9 ng/dL), the levothyroxine dose may need to be doubled, while a patient with a similar TSH and a free T4 that is in the lower third of the normal range may only require a 20 percent dose increase. The serum TSH should be remeasured six to eight weeks after any change in dose. Further dose adjustments may be needed.

EVALUATE THYROID DYSFUNCTION — 

In most individuals, measurement of TSH alone is sufficient for the initial evaluation of suspected thyroid dysfunction in the ambulatory setting. (If pituitary or hypothalamic disease is known or suspected, both serum TSH and free T4 should be measured for the initial evaluation).

If the TSH is high, it should be repeated along with a serum free T4. If the TSH remains high and the free T4 is low, primary hypothyroidism is likely. However, in certain settings (eg, pregnancy, chronic illness), the interpretation of TSH and free T4 requires additional consideration (algorithm 2). Subsequent testing may be indicated depending on the clinical setting.

Ambulatory setting, nonpregnant — The majority of patients with a high TSH (confirmed with repeat measurement) and low free T4 have primary overt hypothyroidism. Initiate levothyroxine. (See "Diagnosis of and screening for hypothyroidism in nonpregnant adults", section on 'Diagnosis' and "Disorders that cause hypothyroidism" and "Treatment of primary hypothyroidism in adults", section on 'Initial dose'.)

The most common cause of primary hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient areas of the world is chronic autoimmune (Hashimoto's) thyroiditis, indicated in most patients by the presence of high serum concentrations of antibodies to thyroid peroxidase (TPO) (table 2). We do not routinely measure TPO antibodies in patients with primary overt hypothyroidism because almost all have chronic autoimmune thyroiditis.

In patients with known hypothalamic or pituitary disease or symptoms and signs of other hormonal deficiencies, and a low free T4 with a slightly high TSH (eg, up to 10 mU/L), central hypothyroidism should be suspected. In this setting, the slightly high TSH is due to pituitary secretion of an immunologically active, but less biologically active, TSH molecule. TSH may be high, normal, or low in central hypothyroidism. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'TSH slightly high'.)

To distinguish between primary and central hypothyroidism in patients with suspected central hypothyroidism, measure:

●TPO antibodies

●Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), along with estradiol or testosterone in women and men, respectively

●Prolactin

Obtain an endocrinology consult for further evaluation and management if central hypothyroidism is suspected.

Hospitalized or ill patients — In general, thyroid function should not be assessed in seriously ill patients unless there is a strong suspicion of thyroid dysfunction, since there are many other factors in acutely or chronically ill euthyroid patients that influence thyroid function tests.

Some hospitalized patients have transient elevations in serum TSH concentrations (up to 20 mU/L) during recovery from nonthyroidal illness.

Small reductions in binding proteins should not alter free T4 values, and these values are usually normal in the absence of thyroid dysfunction in patients whose illness is not severe. The effect of more severe nonthyroidal illness (and drugs used to treat such illness) on "direct" free T4 measurements may be method dependent and may result in low values. (See "Thyroid function in nonthyroidal illness", section on 'Evaluation'.)

In a hospitalized or seriously ill patient with low free T4 and:

●TSH between upper limit of normal and <20 mU/L – Initiate levothyroxine if there is clinical suspicion of hypothyroidism. If uncertain, and the patient appears to be recovering from the underlying illness, repeat the TSH and free T4 in one to two weeks. Thyroid function tests may improve in patients recovering from nonthyroidal illness.

●TSH ≥20 mU/L – Initiate levothyroxine.

Myxedema coma is a rare presentation of hypothyroidism. The diagnosis of myxedema coma should be considered in any patient with coma or depressed mental status who also has hypothermia, hyponatremia, and/or hypercapnia, particularly in a patient with a thyroidectomy scar, a history of radioiodine therapy, or hypothyroidism. The serum T4 concentration is very low, and the serum TSH concentration high in patients with myxedema coma due to primary hypothyroidism. (See "Myxedema coma", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

Pregnant women — In pregnant women with high TSH (initial and repeated values) and low (or normal) free T4, initiate levothyroxine and obtain consultation with a clinician experienced with managing thyroid dysfunction during pregnancy. Do not delay treatment if TSH with free T4 was obtained as the initial measurement, and the tests cannot be repeated immediately.

Because of the changes in thyroid physiology during normal pregnancy and because there are substantial population differences in the TSH upper reference limit, thyroid function tests should be interpreted using population-based, trimester-specific reference ranges for TSH and assay method and trimester-specific reference ranges for serum free T4. When population-based, trimester-specific reference ranges are not available, 4.0 mU/L should be considered the upper limit of normal in pregnant women. (See "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Trimester-specific reference ranges' and "Hypothyroidism during pregnancy: Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and treatment", section on 'Indications for treatment'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

In nonpregnant adults, the normal reference range for serum TSH is approximately 0.5 to 4.5 mU/L and for serum free T4 approximately 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL (10.3 to 23.2 pmol/L). The normal reference ranges can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
