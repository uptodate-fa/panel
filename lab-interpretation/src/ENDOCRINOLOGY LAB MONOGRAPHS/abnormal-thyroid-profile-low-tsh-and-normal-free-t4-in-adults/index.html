ALGORITHM — 

The evaluation of a low thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) depends upon whether the TSH was measured to monitor levothyroxine therapy (algorithm 1) or to evaluate for thyroid dysfunction (algorithm 2).

Because of the changes in thyroid physiology during pregnancy, thyroid function tests in pregnant women should be interpreted using population-based, trimester-specific reference ranges for TSH and assay method and trimester-specific reference ranges for serum free thyroxine (T4). (See "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Trimester-specific reference ranges'.)

MONITOR LEVOTHYROXINE THERAPY — 

One of the more common reasons for measuring TSH is to monitor levothyroxine therapy (algorithm 1):

●Primary hypothyroidism – For patients with treated primary hypothyroidism, therapy may be monitored with the TSH alone. If the TSH is abnormal, some laboratories reflexively measure the free T4. Patients with a low TSH typically require a dose reduction. For patients with low TSH and normal free T4 values, an initial reduction in dose of 12 to 25 mcg/day is usually sufficient. TSH should always be repeated six to eight weeks after dose adjustments to determine the need for further adjustment.

●Thyroid cancer – For patients taking levothyroxine to prevent recurrence of thyroid cancer, the goal is often an undetectable or subnormal serum TSH concentration, depending on the clinical situation. The free T4 may be normal or mildly elevated. (See "Laboratory assessment of thyroid function", section on 'Monitoring levothyroxine therapy' and "Differentiated thyroid cancer: Overview of management", section on 'Ongoing management based on dynamic risk stratification'.)

●Central hypothyroidism – For patients with central (secondary) hypothyroidism due to pituitary or hypothalamic disease, the dose of T4 should be adjusted according to the patient's symptoms and serum free T4 (not TSH), aiming to maintain the serum free T4 concentration slightly higher than mid-normal. Serum TSH cannot be used to monitor therapy, as it is suppressed to <0.1 mU/L in nearly all patients taking doses of T4 that raise their serum free T4 concentrations to normal. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Treatment'.)

EVALUATE THYROID DYSFUNCTION — 

Most patients who have low serum TSH concentrations but normal (often high-normal) serum free T4 concentrations have subclinical hyperthyroidism (algorithm 2). However, assay interference (eg, biotin supplements) or triiodothyronine (T3) toxicosis are alternative diagnoses (see "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Diagnosis'):

●Inquire about biotin ingestion. In patients taking biotin, repeat thyroid tests (TSH, free T4, and total T3 [free T3 in women taking estrogen]) at least two days after discontinuation.

Ingestion of a single dose of biotin (5 to 10 mg) can cause spurious results in certain commercial thyroid test immunoassays (those using biotinylated antibodies). The resulting biochemical findings suggest a diagnosis of hyperthyroidism; however, discontinuation of biotin supplements results in resolution of the erroneous biochemical abnormalities. (See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Assay interference with biotin ingestion'.)

●In patients not taking biotin or estrogens, measure total T3 to assess for overt hyperthyroidism due to T3 toxicosis. In women taking estrogen, measure free T3 (rather than total T3) as estrogens increase thyroid binding globulin, which may result in high total T3 but not free T3 levels.

Other, less likely causes of low TSH with normal free T4 and T3 in the ambulatory setting include central hypothyroidism or recovery from hyperthyroidism. Due to a shift in the distribution of TSH values, approximately 3 percent of healthy Black Americans have a serum TSH <0.4 mU/L, although it would be unusual for the TSH to be <0.1 mU/L (see "Subclinical hyperthyroidism in nonpregnant adults", section on 'Differential diagnosis'):

●Subclinical hyperthyroidism – Because the serum TSH concentration can be transiently reduced, a serum TSH measurement, along with a free T4 and T3 (free T3 in women taking estrogen), should be repeated after one to three months to confirm the diagnosis. (See "Subclinical hyperthyroidism in nonpregnant adults", section on 'Etiology' and "Subclinical hyperthyroidism in nonpregnant adults", section on 'Identifying the cause'.)

●Central hypothyroidism – Some patients with central hypothyroidism have low serum TSH and normal (but usually low or low-normal) free T4 and T3 concentrations. Suspect central hypothyroidism particularly in patients with known hypothalamic or pituitary disease and when there are coexisting deficiencies or symptoms and signs of other hormonal deficiencies (eg, amenorrhea in a young woman). (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Clinical features'.)

If central hypothyroidism is suspected, evaluate for coexisting pituitary hormone abnormalities. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Evaluation'.)

•8:00 AM cortisol

•Prolactin

•Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and estradiol in premenopausal women

•FSH in postmenopausal women

•LH and testosterone in men

•Endocrinology consultation for further evaluation and management

●Recovery from hyperthyroidism – Serum TSH concentrations may remain low for two to three months (rarely longer) after normalization of serum T4 and T3 concentrations in patients treated for hyperthyroidism or recovering from hyperthyroidism caused by thyroiditis. (See "Graves' hyperthyroidism in nonpregnant adults: Overview of treatment", section on 'Monitoring after treatment'.)

●Glucocorticoids – Pharmacologic doses of glucocorticoids (>20 mg per day of prednisone or its equivalent) inhibit TSH secretion. Free T4 and T3 are typically normal in ambulatory patients. Reassess thyroid function tests after dose reduction (to ≤5 mg/day or its equivalent) or discontinuation.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

In nonpregnant adults, the normal reference range for serum TSH is approximately 0.5 to 4.5 mU/L, and for serum free T4 approximately 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL (10.3 to 23.2 pmol/L). The normal reference ranges can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
