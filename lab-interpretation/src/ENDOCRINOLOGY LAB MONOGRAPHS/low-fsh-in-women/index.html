ALGORITHM — 

(algorithm 1)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) is typically measured to assess irregular or absent menstrual cycles or as part of the initial evaluation of infertility (see "Female infertility: Evaluation"):

●Confirm patient is not using combined estrogen-progestin contraceptives – Of note, FSH will be low in women using combination estrogen-progestin contraception containing ethinyl estradiol and a progestin because the combination contraception suppresses FSH. Amenorrhea in women using combination contraception may be related to the hormone formulation rather than an underlying problem, and an alternative hormonal formulation could be considered if the patient prefers monthly menses. If assessment of hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis is necessary, discontinue the combination contraceptive for approximately one month (during which time the patient might require an alternative nonhormonal form of contraception).

●Repeat FSH with concomitant estradiol (E2; on day 3 of a menstrual cycle, if possible). For patients with prolonged amenorrhea, perform laboratory testing on a random day.

●Review other tests that are part of the initial evaluation of irregular or absent menstrual cycles (see "Evaluation and management of secondary amenorrhea"):

•Serum human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)

•Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)

•Prolactin

A positive hCG indicates pregnancy. Abnormalities in TSH and prolactin should be addressed. (See "Management of hyperprolactinemia" and "Laboratory assessment of thyroid function", section on 'Clinical use of thyroid function tests'.)

For women with negative hCG and normal prolactin and TSH, the diagnosis is based upon FSH and E2 results. If the diagnosis remains uncertain, obtain consultation with a clinician who has expertise in reproductive endocrinology:

●FSH low, E2 low or normal – Low or normal E2 associated with low (or normal) FSH suggests hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, either functional (more common) or structural. Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea is based upon the findings of amenorrhea, low serum gonadotropins and E2, and, usually, evidence of a precipitating factor (exercise, low weight, stress). Hypothalamic amenorrhea can also be seen with systemic illness, such as celiac disease and type 1 diabetes mellitus.

As in every woman with new-onset amenorrhea, other causes (eg, pituitary or hypothalamic structural disease) should first be excluded before diagnosing functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (table 1). Among pituitary disorders that cause secondary amenorrhea, prolactin-secreting pituitary adenomas are the most common. Hypothalamic tumors (eg, craniopharyngiomas, lymphomas) and infiltrative diseases (eg, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, sarcoidosis) may result in decreased gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) secretion, low or normal serum gonadotropin concentrations, and amenorrhea. However, these lesions are uncommon. Most women with infiltrative disease of the hypothalamus who have amenorrhea will have one or more neurologic symptoms, such as severe headache, change in personality, or marked mood changes (see "Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea: Evaluation and management"):

•If type 1 diabetes is suspected, measure fasting blood sugar or glycated hemoglobin (hemoglobin A1C).

•If celiac disease is suspected, measure immunoglobulin A antibodies against tissue transglutaminase (tTG-IgA).

•If there is no clear explanation for hypogonadotropic amenorrhea (eg, eating disorder, excessive exercise) or if there are neurologic symptoms (eg, visual field defects, headaches), obtain pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Other specific tests may be done, depending upon the clinical history. As an example, iron studies to test for hemochromatosis should be performed if there is an appropriate family history or if the patient has manifestations of iron overload (bronzed skin, diabetes mellitus, or unexplained heart or liver disease). However, hemochromatosis is less likely to cause a pituitary dysfunction in women than men.

●FSH low, E2 high

•Use of transdermal estrogen – High E2 with low FSH could be consistent with use of transdermal E2. FSH and E2 levels are not routinely measured to monitor hormone therapy. In women taking hormone therapy, titrate estrogen dose to alleviate symptoms or use the lowest dose to preserve bone density in patients who experience side effects. (See "Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea: Evaluation and management", section on 'Estrogen replacement'.)

•E2-secreting ovarian cyst or granulosa cell tumors – E2 can be high in the presence of an ovarian cyst that is secreting E2 or, very rarely, in granulosa cell tumors. These types of ovarian abnormalities usually cause irregular bleeding rather than amenorrhea. If there is pelvic pain or fullness, a pelvic examination and ultrasound may be needed.

•Androgen-secreting tumors – In women with androgen-secreting tumors (adrenal or ovarian), E2 may be high. FSH should be suppressed. In women in menopause or the menopausal transition who should have elevated FSH, the FSH level is unlikely to be suppressed into the normal premenopausal range by an androgen-secreting tumor, but it may be lower than expected for a menopausal woman. If there is clinical evidence of hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne, scalp hair loss [alopecia]), other laboratory tests (eg, serum total testosterone) should be measured. (See "Clinical presentation and evaluation of adrenocortical tumors" and "Sex cord-stromal tumors of the ovary: Epidemiology, clinical features, and diagnosis in adults".)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal range for FSH varies with the menstrual cycle, ranging from 2 to 9 milli-international units/mL during the follicular/luteal phases and 4 to 22 milli-international units/mL at midcycle. Therefore, in premenopausal women, an FSH concentration that is <2 milli-international units/mL is considered low. In menopausal women, the normal range is generally >30 milli-international units/mL. The upper threshold for a normal FSH concentration is laboratory dependent; cutoff values of 10 to 30 milli-international units/mL have been reported because of use of different FSH assay reference standards and assay methodologies. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
