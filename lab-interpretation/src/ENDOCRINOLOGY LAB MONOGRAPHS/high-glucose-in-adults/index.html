IMMEDIATE ACTION

DKA/HHS — Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) are the most serious hyperglycemic complications of diabetes. They require prompt recognition and management (table 1):

●Suspect DKA in patients with rapidly evolving severe symptoms (eg, polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain) and glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), most often in the 350 to 500 mg/dL (19.4 to 27.8 mmol/L) range.

●Suspect HHS in patients with insidiously developing severe symptoms (eg, polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, lethargy, mental obtundation, coma) and marked hyperglycemia (glucose ≥600 mg/dL [33.3 mmol/L]).

If DKA or HHS is suspected, provide hemodynamic support, correct fluid and electrolyte deficits, and determine potential precipitating factors by performing appropriate laboratory studies, electrocardiogram (ECG), and additional tests (eg, chest radiograph, blood cultures, urine culture, depending on the clinical presentation) (table 2). (See "Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state in adults: Clinical features, evaluation, and diagnosis", section on 'Diagnostic evaluation' and "Diabetic ketoacidosis in adults: Treatment".)

Hyperglycemia associated with severe illness and glucocorticoid treatment — In adults without known diabetes, transient hyperglycemia may occur during severe illness, including myocardial infarction, sepsis, and other infectious diseases (eg, coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID-19]) and in the setting of glucocorticoid treatment. This is sometimes referred to as stress hyperglycemia and is a consequence of many factors, including increased serum concentrations of cortisol, catecholamines, glucagon, and growth hormone, which lead to increased gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, and insulin resistance. Although not all patients go on to develop diabetes, stress hyperglycemia may be a marker of abnormal glucose tolerance and increased risk for developing diabetes. In the critical care setting, insulin infusions and intermittent short-acting insulin are typically required to manage acute hyperglycemia. (See "Glycemic control in critically ill adult and pediatric patients".)

Patients should be assessed for diabetes after recovery or cessation of steroid therapy. Measurement of glycated hemoglobin (A1C) at the time of the initial hyperglycemia can help determine whether the hyperglycemia is, in fact, acute (A1C either normal or minimally elevated) or whether hyperglycemia has been present for some time (elevated A1C).

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

For patients without severe illness, a single high glucose level requires a timely evaluation to determine the presence of diabetes.

●Patients without a prior history of diabetes typically require repeat glucose testing or an A1C to confirm or exclude a diagnosis of diabetes.

●In patients already known to have diabetes, continue to manage diabetes. Glycemic management is monitored with A1C along with either intermittent capillary blood sampling and the use of a glucose meter or with devices designed for continuous glucose monitoring. (See "Glucose monitoring in the ambulatory management of nonpregnant adults with diabetes mellitus".)

Mild to moderate symptoms of hyperglycemia present — If symptoms of hyperglycemia (eg, polyuria, polydipsia, blurred vision) are present, an elevated random plasma glucose level may be sufficient to diagnose diabetes:

●Plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) – In the presence of symptoms, a random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) is diagnostic of diabetes. Manage diabetes. For patients with newly diagnosed (but potentially previously unrecognized) diabetes, assess for diabetes-related complications. (See "Clinical presentation, diagnosis, and initial evaluation of diabetes mellitus in adults", section on 'Evaluation' and "Overview of general medical care in nonpregnant adults with diabetes mellitus", section on 'Evaluation' and "Initial management of hyperglycemia in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus".)

●Plasma glucose <200 mg/dL – Obtain fasting plasma glucose or A1C.

Symptoms of hyperglycemia absent — Most patients are asymptomatic, and hyperglycemia is noted on routine laboratory evaluation. Patients incidentally noted to have hyperglycemia should have repeat testing. Glucose samples should be collected in tubes that inhibit glycolysis and should not be left at room temperature prior to assay. (See "Clinical presentation, diagnosis, and initial evaluation of diabetes mellitus in adults", section on 'Asymptomatic hyperglycemia' and "Screening for type 2 diabetes mellitus and prediabetes".)

Fasting sample — If the initial sample was drawn while fasting (no caloric intake for at least eight hours), further evaluation depends on the level:

●Fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L) – Repeat fasting sample on subsequent day.

●Fasting glucose <126 – Repeat fasting glucose in one year. Identify and manage diabetes and cardiovascular disease risk factors (eg, obesity, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking).

If obtaining a fasting glucose is inconvenient, A1C can be measured instead. (See 'Diagnosis of diabetes' below.)

Nonfasting sample — If the initial test was a nonfasting plasma glucose, obtain:

●Fasting plasma glucose

or

●A1C

Simultaneously obtaining a fasting blood sugar and A1C is a reasonable option, particularly in patients with the highest risk of diabetes (eg, multiple risk factors for diabetes). If obtaining a fasting specimen is inconvenient, A1C alone can be used to diagnose diabetes. (See 'Diagnosis of diabetes' below.)

Diagnosis of diabetes — The following results are consistent with a diagnosis of diabetes (table 3):

●Fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L) on two occasions

●A1C ≥6.5 percent (48 mmol/mol) on two occasions

●Fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L) and A1C ≥6.5 percent (48 mmol/mol) measured at or around the same time  

If fasting plasma glucose is ≥126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L) or A1C is ≥6.5 percent (48 mmol/mol), repeat the test that is indicative of diabetes. Additional testing may be warranted to explore possible reasons for discordance (eg, improper sample handling [glucose], conditions that alter red blood cell lifespan [A1C]). (See "Clinical presentation, diagnosis, and initial evaluation of diabetes mellitus in adults", section on 'Asymptomatic hyperglycemia' and "Measurements of chronic glycemia in diabetes mellitus", section on 'Unexpected or discordant values'.)

For patients with newly diagnosed (but potentially previously unrecognized) diabetes, evaluate for diabetes-related complications (table 4), cardiovascular risk factors, and, when indicated, evaluate for comorbid conditions (eg, sleep apnea, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, depression). If not measured in the past one year, obtain:

●Fasting lipid profile

●Liver function tests (aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], alkaline phosphatase)

●Serum creatinine (with estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR])

●Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (spot urine)

Perform an annual assessment of risk criteria (blood pressure, lipid profile, smoking history) to identify patients who might benefit from more intensive cardiovascular risk factor management. (See "Overview of general medical care in nonpregnant adults with diabetes mellitus", section on 'Evaluation'.)

Additional evaluation and management of patients with newly diagnosed diabetes is discussed separately. (See "Clinical presentation, diagnosis, and initial evaluation of diabetes mellitus in adults", section on 'Evaluation' and "Initial management of hyperglycemia in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus".)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for fasting glucose is approximately 70 to 99 mg/dL (3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L). Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based on the reference range reported with that result.
