EVALUATION — 

Interpretation of serum testosterone measurements in males should take into consideration its diurnal fluctuation, which reaches a maximum at approximately 8:00 AM; its day-to-day variability; and its suppression by food or glucose ingestion. Measurements should be made in the morning, ideally between 8:00 to 10:00 AM, after an overnight fast. Males with acute or subacute illness, or taking a short course of opioids or glucocorticoids for acute pain or illness, should not be assessed for hypogonadism, as they will have a transient functional secondary hypogonadism. Optimally, total testosterone should be measured using an assay certified as accurate and reliable (eg, by the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Hormone Standardization [CDC HoSt] Program). (See "Clinical features and diagnosis of male hypogonadism", section on 'Candidates for testing'.)

Repeat measurement early morning — Measure early morning (8:00 to 10:00 AM), fasting total testosterone. If a single 8:00 to 10:00 AM value is low or borderline low, the measurement should be repeated once or twice before making the diagnosis of hypogonadism. (See "Clinical features and diagnosis of male hypogonadism", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

Measurement of the serum free testosterone concentration is indicated when it is suspected that an abnormality in sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) concentration coexists in someone being evaluated for hypogonadism, primarily in patients with obesity (decreases SHBG in proportion to degree of obesity) or in older males (age increases SHBG slightly). Free testosterone measurement is also recommended when an initial total testosterone concentration is near the lower limit of the normal range. (See "Clinical features and diagnosis of male hypogonadism", section on 'Free testosterone'.)

If free testosterone is measured, one of the following methods is preferred:

●Measurement by equilibrium dialysis – The most reliable method. Samples should be sent to one of the many commercial laboratories that specialize in endocrine testing, but it is essential to specify that the test should be performed by equilibrium dialysis and to use that laboratory's test code for testosterone by equilibrium dialysis.

●Calculated free testosterone (from the total testosterone, SHBG, and albumin concentrations).

Free testosterone measured by an automated platform analog immunoassay method, the assay commonly offered by hospital and commercial laboratories, does not provide accurate measurements of free testosterone with values that are an order of magnitude lower than the results of equilibrium dialysis. Analog methodology gives misleading information and should never be ordered. Bioavailable testosterone, which is a technically challenging assay, is also not recommended, as there are insufficient comparison data (with results of equilibrium dialysis) to permit interpretation.

Total and free testosterone decrease with increasing age, although true hypogonadism is uncommon in otherwise healthy older males. If the testosterone is unequivocally and reproducibly low in an older male, the evaluation is the same as in younger males. (See "Approach to older males with low testosterone", section on 'Evaluation for possible hypogonadism'.)

Testing to determine etiology — If the 8:00 to 10:00 AM serum testosterone is persistently below normal, measure:

●Serum luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), to distinguish primary from secondary hypogonadism:

•LH and FSH elevated – Consistent with primary hypogonadism (table 1).

-In males without a known cause of primary hypogonadism (eg, cancer treatment with alkylating agent or radiation, trauma, mumps orchitis) and if consistent clinical findings (eg, testes size <6 mL) are present, obtain karyotype to assess for Klinefelter syndrome.

•LH and FSH not elevated – Consistent with secondary hypogonadism (table 2).

Evaluate for other pituitary hormonal deficiencies. Obtain:

-Total thyroxine (T4) or free T4

-8:00 AM cortisol

Evaluate for possible causes. Measure:

-Prolactin.

-Transferrin saturation and ferritin to assess for hemochromatosis. Transferrin saturation is the ratio of serum iron to total iron binding capacity (TIBC), expressed as a percentage.

Determine need for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the pituitary:

-In males with secondary hypogonadism, MRI should always be performed if the patient has other pituitary hormonal abnormalities (eg, secondary hypoadrenalism, secondary hypothyroidism or hyperprolactinemia), a visual field abnormality, or other neurologic abnormalities.

-If there is no other evidence of pituitary or hypothalamic disease, finding a mass lesion on MRI is more likely the younger and healthier the patient and the lower the serum testosterone concentration. Moderately low serum testosterone concentrations (eg, 200 to 300 ng/dL [6.9 to 10.4 nmol/L]) are relatively common in males >65 years old and are usually not associated with sellar mass lesions, so an MRI should not be ordered in this situation unless associated with other evidence suggesting a sellar lesion. Illness, like age, lowers the serum testosterone level and therefore might influence the decision to order an MRI.

(See "Clinical features and diagnosis of male hypogonadism", section on 'Additional testing to determine etiology'.)

Semen analysis — A semen analysis is also part of the evaluation of hypogonadism if the patient is pursuing fertility or has been diagnosed with infertility. (See "Clinical features and diagnosis of male hypogonadism", section on 'Semen analysis'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

Measurement of the serum total (free plus protein-bound) testosterone concentration is usually an accurate reflection of testosterone secretion. The normal range in adult males varies depending on the total testosterone assay and reference population used. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result. In a survey of 120 laboratories in the United States, median values for the lower to upper limits of reference range were 241 to 827 ng/dL (8.4 to 28.7 nmol/L). For accurate and reliable United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-certified total testosterone assays, the harmonized reference range is 264 to 916 ng/dL (9.2 to 31.8 nmol/L).
