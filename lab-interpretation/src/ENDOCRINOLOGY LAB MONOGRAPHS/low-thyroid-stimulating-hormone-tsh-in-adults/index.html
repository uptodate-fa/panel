ALGORITHM — 

The evaluation of a low thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) depends upon whether the TSH was measured to monitor levothyroxine therapy (algorithm 1) or to evaluate for thyroid dysfunction (algorithm 2).

MONITOR LEVOTHYROXINE THERAPY — 

One of the more common reasons for measuring TSH is to monitor levothyroxine therapy (algorithm 1):

●Primary hypothyroidism – Patients with primary hypothyroidism and low TSH typically require a dose reduction. If the TSH is slightly below normal (eg, 0.05 to 0.3 mU/L), a small dose reduction of 12 to 25 mcg/day is usually sufficient, and measurement of free thyroxine (T4) is not necessary. For TSH values below 0.05 mU/L, measurement of free T4 is helpful to assess the degree of overtreatment and appropriate percentage reduction in dose. TSH should always be repeated six to eight weeks after dose adjustments to determine the need for further adjustment. (See "Treatment of primary hypothyroidism in adults", section on 'Adjustment of maintenance dose'.)

●Thyroid cancer – For patients taking levothyroxine to prevent recurrence of thyroid cancer, the goal is often an undetectable or subnormal serum TSH concentration, depending on the clinical situation. However, if the TSH is <0.05 mU/L, serum free T4 should be measured to assess the degree of potentially excessive therapy. If the serum free T4 is slightly elevated, the dose should be reduced by 12 to 25 mcg/day with repeat TSH and free T4 in six to eight weeks to confirm that the TSH is still in the target range. Larger initial reductions in the dose are necessary if the free T4 levels are very high. (See "Laboratory assessment of thyroid function", section on 'Monitoring levothyroxine therapy' and "Differentiated thyroid cancer: Overview of management", section on 'Ongoing management based on dynamic risk stratification'.)

●Central hypothyroidism – For patients with central (secondary) hypothyroidism due to pituitary or hypothalamic disease, the dose of T4 should be adjusted according to the patient's symptoms and serum free T4 (not TSH), aiming to maintain the serum free T4 concentration slightly higher than mid-normal. Serum TSH cannot be used to monitor therapy, as it is suppressed to <0.1 mU/L in nearly all patients taking doses of T4 that raise their serum free T4 concentrations to normal. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Treatment'.)

EVALUATE THYROID DYSFUNCTION — 

When a low TSH is found in a patient not known to have thyroid disease, the initial evaluation typically includes repeating the measurement, along with a serum free T4 and total triiodothyronine (T3). In women taking estrogen, measure free T3 (rather than total T3) as estrogens increase thyroid binding globulin, which may result in high total T3 but not free T3 levels. Additional testing may be indicated depending on the clinical setting and the free T4 and T3 levels (algorithm 2).

Hospitalized or ill patients — In general, TSH should not be assessed in seriously ill patients unless there is a strong suspicion of thyroid dysfunction, since there are many other factors in acutely or chronically ill euthyroid patients that influence thyroid function tests. Euthyroid patients with nonthyroidal illness, especially those receiving high-dose glucocorticoids (>20 mg/day prednisone or its equivalent) or dopamine, may have low serum TSH, although in the absence of thyrotoxicosis, it would be unusual for the TSH to be undetectable in an assay with a detection limit of 0.01 mU/L. In addition, many hospitalized/ill patients have low or low-normal serum T4 and low T3 concentrations. (See "Thyroid function in nonthyroidal illness" and "Drug interactions with thyroid hormones", section on 'Drugs that affect TSH secretion'.)

In hospitalized or ill patients in whom TSH measurement is necessary (eg, a sick patient with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation), assays that have a detection limit of 0.01 mU/L should be used to assess thyroid function. If TSH is found to be low, obtain the following tests to distinguish between nonthyroidal illness and hyperthyroidism:

●Repeat TSH

●Total T4

●Free T4

●Total T3

(See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Critically ill hyperthyroid patients' and "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Evaluation' and "Thyroid function in nonthyroidal illness", section on 'TSH subnormal'.)

Pregnant women — The diagnosis of true hyperthyroidism during pregnancy may be difficult because of the changes in thyroid function that occur during normal pregnancy. In addition, because there are population differences in the TSH lower reference limit, thyroid function tests should be interpreted using population-based, trimester-specific reference ranges for TSH and assay method and trimester-specific reference ranges for serum free T4.

In the absence of population and trimester-specific normal ranges, reduce the lower limit of the reference range of TSH by approximately 0.4 mU/L, corresponding to a TSH lower limit of approximately 0.1 mU/L. (See "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Trimester-specific reference ranges'.)

If the TSH level is <0.1 mU/L:

●Measure free T4 and free T3 (or total T4 and total T3)

●Obtain endocrinology consultation for further evaluation and management

(See "Hyperthyroidism during pregnancy: Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and causes", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

Ambulatory setting, nonpregnant

High free T4 and/or T3 — Except for laboratory error or interference (eg, biotin supplements), all patients with low serum TSH and high free T4 and/or T3 concentrations have overt primary hyperthyroidism. (See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

Ingestion of a single dose of biotin (5 to 10 mg) can cause spurious results in certain thyroid test assays. The resulting biochemical findings suggest a diagnosis of hyperthyroidism; however, discontinuation of biotin supplements results in resolution of the erroneous biochemical abnormalities. In patients taking biotin supplements, thyroid tests should be repeated at least two days after discontinuation of the supplements.

Once the diagnosis of hyperthyroidism has been established, the cause of the hyperthyroidism should be determined, if not apparent based on clinical presentation. Diagnostic testing can include the following, depending on available expertise and resources:

●Measurement of thyrotropin receptor antibodies, which are present in Graves disease (TRAb, also called TSI, TBII, or TBI)

●Determination of the radioactive iodine uptake (contraindicated during pregnancy and lactation) (table 1)

●Measurement of thyroidal blood flow on ultrasonography

(See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Determining the etiology'.)

Normal free T4 and T3 — Most patients who have low serum TSH concentrations but normal (often high-normal) serum free T4 and T3 concentrations have subclinical hyperthyroidism. Other, less likely causes of low TSH with normal free T4 and T3 in the ambulatory setting include central hypothyroidism or recovery from hyperthyroidism. Due to a shift in the distribution of TSH values, approximately 3 percent of healthy Black Americans have a serum TSH <0.4 mU/L, although it would be unusual for the TSH to be <0.1 mU/L (see "Subclinical hyperthyroidism in nonpregnant adults", section on 'Differential diagnosis'):

●Subclinical hyperthyroidism – Because the serum TSH concentration can be transiently reduced, a serum TSH measurement, along with a free T4 and T3, should be repeated after one to three months to confirm the diagnosis. (See "Subclinical hyperthyroidism in nonpregnant adults", section on 'Etiology' and "Subclinical hyperthyroidism in nonpregnant adults", section on 'Identifying the cause'.)

●Central hypothyroidism – Some patients with central hypothyroidism have low serum TSH and normal (but usually low or low-normal) free T4 and T3 concentrations. The majority of patients have coexisting deficiencies in other pituitary hormones, although isolated TSH deficiency may also occur. Evaluate for coexisting pituitary hormone abnormalities. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Evaluation'.)

●Recovery from hyperthyroidism – Serum TSH concentrations may remain low for two to three months (rarely longer) after normalization of serum T4 and T3 concentrations in patients treated for hyperthyroidism or recovering from hyperthyroidism caused by thyroiditis. (See "Graves' hyperthyroidism in nonpregnant adults: Overview of treatment", section on 'Monitoring after treatment'.)

●Glucocorticoids – Pharmacologic doses of glucocorticoids (>20 mg per day of prednisone or its equivalent) inhibit TSH secretion. Free T4 and T3 are typically normal in ambulatory patients. Reassess thyroid function tests after dose reduction (to ≤5 mg/day of prednisone or its equivalent) or discontinuation.

Low free T4 and T3 — In an ambulatory setting, the combination of low TSH with low free T4 and T3 is uncommon and may be due to central hypothyroidism. In patients with central hypothyroidism, the serum free T4 and T3 are low-normal or frankly low and serum TSH may be low, inappropriately normal (for the low T4), or slightly high (5 to 10 mU/L) due to secretion of biologically inactive TSH. The majority of patients have coexisting deficiencies in other pituitary hormones, although isolated TSH deficiency may also occur.

Obtain endocrinology consult to evaluate for coexisting pituitary hormone abnormalities. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Evaluation'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for serum TSH is approximately 0.5 to 4.0 mU/L in nonpregnant adults but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
