ALGORITHM — 

(algorithm 1)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

When a low thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) is found in a patient not known to have thyroid disease, the initial evaluation typically includes repeating the measurement, along with a serum free thyroxine (T4) and total triiodothyronine (T3) (or free T3 in women taking estrogen). The combination of low TSH and low free T4 is consistent with central hypothyroidism. However, in certain settings (eg, pregnancy, chronic or severe illness), the interpretation of TSH and free T4 requires additional consideration. Subsequent testing may be indicated depending on the clinical setting.

Ambulatory setting, nonpregnant — In an ambulatory setting, the combination of low TSH with low free T4 is uncommon and may be due to central hypothyroidism. However, individuals with chronic illness or receiving high-dose glucocorticoids (>20 mg/day prednisone or its equivalent) may have a similar pattern.

Suspect central hypothyroidism particularly in patients with known hypothalamic or pituitary disease and when there are symptoms and signs of other hormonal deficiencies (eg, amenorrhea in a young woman). (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Clinical features'.)

If central hypothyroidism is suspected, obtain (see "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Evaluation'):

●8:00 AM cortisol

●Prolactin

●Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and estradiol in premenopausal women

●FSH in postmenopausal women

●LH and testosterone in men

●Endocrinology consult for further evaluation and management

Hospitalized or severely ill patients — In general, free T4 and TSH should not be assessed in seriously ill patients unless there is a strong suspicion of thyroid dysfunction, since there are many other factors in acutely or chronically ill euthyroid patients that influence thyroid function tests. Euthyroid patients with nonthyroidal illness, especially those receiving high-dose glucocorticoids (>20 mg/day prednisone or its equivalent) or dopamine, may have low serum TSH and low or low-normal serum free T4 (and low T3) concentrations (see "Thyroid function in nonthyroidal illness" and "Drug interactions with thyroid hormones", section on 'Drugs that affect TSH secretion'):

●In patients without a strong suspicion of thyroid dysfunction, obtain repeat thyroid tests (TSH, free T4) after recovery/discharge.

●In patients with a strong suspicion of thyroid dysfunction while hospitalized, TSH assays that have a detection limit of 0.01 mU/L should be used to assess thyroid function:

•Repeat TSH

•Repeat free T4

•Obtain total T4 and total T3

If true central hypothyroidism due to hypothalamic or pituitary disease remains in the differential diagnosis, measurement of serum cortisol can be helpful, as it would be elevated in critical illness and low (or inappropriately normal) in patients with true central hypothyroidism. (See "Thyroid function in nonthyroidal illness", section on 'Evaluation'.)

Pregnant women — Because of the changes in thyroid physiology during pregnancy, thyroid function tests should be interpreted using population-based, trimester-specific reference ranges for TSH and assay method and trimester-specific reference ranges for serum free T4 (see "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Trimester-specific reference ranges' and "Hypothyroidism during pregnancy: Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and treatment", section on 'Diagnosis'):

●In the first trimester, the combination of low free T4 with a normal or low TSH is very uncommon. If confirmed, obtain endocrinology consultation.

●In the second or third trimester, low free T4 with normal or low TSH is likely due to pregnancy-related changes in binding proteins and limitations in the free T4 assay. Measurement of total T4 can be used as reassurance that the low free T4 is an assay issue. At approximately 16 weeks, total T4 (and T3) levels during pregnancy are 1.5-fold higher than in nonpregnant women (due to thyroxine-binding globulin [TBG] excess).

REFERENCE RANGE — 

In nonpregnant adults, the normal reference range for serum TSH is approximately 0.5 to 4.5 mU/L, and for serum free T4 approximately 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL (10.3 to 23.2 pmol/L). The normal reference ranges can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
