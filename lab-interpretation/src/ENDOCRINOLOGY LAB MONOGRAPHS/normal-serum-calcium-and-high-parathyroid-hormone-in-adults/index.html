INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The combination of a normal serum calcium and a high parathyroid hormone (PTH) may come to medical attention in the setting of chronic kidney disease or as part of an evaluation for low bone mineral density, during which time PTH levels are drawn and found to be elevated in the absence of hypercalcemia.

It is important to confirm that the serum total calcium concentration accurately reflects the physiologically important ionized (or free) calcium concentration. As an example, in a patient with hypoalbuminemia, the total calcium may be normal, but the corrected calcium ( calculator 1 ) or ionized calcium may be high. Even in the presence of a normal serum albumin, changes in blood pH can alter the equilibrium constant of the albumin-calcium complex, with acidosis reducing the binding and alkalosis enhancing it. In critically ill or postsurgical patients, in particular, correcting total calcium for albumin is not necessarily accurate because of changes in pH and affinity of calcium binding (see "Relation between total and ionized serum calcium concentrations" ):

● Obtain repeat PTH with calcium and albumin or with ionized calcium:

• In patients with hypo- or hyperalbuminemia, the measured total calcium concentration can be corrected for the abnormality in albumin, using ( calculator 1 ) for mg/dL or ( calculator 2 ) for standard international units. There are a number of formulas that have been used to correct the total calcium for serum albumin concentrations, but none appears to be universally acceptable when examined for their correlation with ionized calcium.

• If a laboratory known to measure ionized calcium reliably is available, some experts prefer to measure the serum ionized calcium, particularly in patients with acid base disturbances.

● If available, review previous values for serum calcium for trends or previous high or low values.

If PTH is persistently high and repeat calcium (corrected for albumin or ionized) is normal, causes may include:

● Secondary hyperparathyroidism – Secondary hyperparathyroidism, characterized biochemically by elevated PTH and normal or low corrected calcium concentrations, is the most common explanation for these laboratory findings. In secondary hyperparathyroidism, the parathyroid gland is not diseased/abnormal. Rather, it occurs when the parathyroid gland appropriately responds to a reduced level of extracellular calcium. PTH concentrations rise, and calcium is mobilized by increasing intestinal absorption and increasing bone resorption. In many cases, this secondary increase in PTH leads to the normalization of serum calcium (thus, the findings of high PTH with normal calcium concentration).

Secondary hyperparathyroidism may occur in patients with ( table 1 ):

• Acute or chronic kidney disease (secondary hyperparathyroidism may first become evident when the estimated glomerular filtration rate drops below 60 mL/min/1.73 m 2 , and its prevalence rises as kidney function declines).

• Inadequate calcium intake and/or absorption (vitamin D deficiency, celiac disease, bariatric surgery).

• Idiopathic hypercalciuria (due to a defect in renal tubular calcium reabsorption).

• Reduced or inhibited bone resorption (including use of some potent antiresorptive agents).

• End-organ resistance to PTH.

(See "Vitamin D deficiency in adults: Definition, clinical manifestations, and treatment", section on 'Clinical manifestations' and "Overview of chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD)", section on 'Overview' .)

● Primary hyperparathyroidism – Unlike secondary hyperparathyroidism, in which the parathyroid glands are functioning normally, in primary hyperparathyroidism, there is abnormal parathyroid hyperfunction:

• Primary hyperparathyroidism with vitamin D deficiency – Coexisting primary hyperparathyroidism and vitamin D deficiency is common. When this occurs, the serum calcium level in the primary hyperparathyroid patient may be reduced (into the normal range in some cases) due to vitamin D deficiency. (See "Vitamin D deficiency in adults: Definition, clinical manifestations, and treatment", section on 'Coexisting primary hyperparathyroidism' .)

• Normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism – Normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism is a variant of primary hyperparathyroidism. It is characterized by consistently normal total and ionized calcium concentrations with an elevated PTH. In order to make a diagnosis of normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism, all secondary causes for hyperparathyroidism must be ruled out. In addition, review of previous records of serum calcium can assist in the diagnosis. (See "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and evaluation", section on 'Secondary hyperparathyroidism' .)

● Drugs – Lithium decreases parathyroid gland sensitivity to calcium and also may reduce urinary calcium excretion. A subset of patients taking chronic lithium therapy develop hypercalcemia with a high serum PTH. However, normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism has also been reported. (See "Primary hyperparathyroidism: Pathogenesis and etiology", section on 'Lithium therapy' .)

Initial evaluation to determine the etiology includes a detailed history of dietary and supplemental calcium intake, medication use, and measurement of:

● Serum creatinine

● 25-hydroxyvitamin D

● Phosphate

If the cause remains uncertain, obtain urinary calcium excretion (24-hour urine for calcium and creatinine to assess calcium sufficiency):

● Urinary calcium will be extremely low in patients with vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism, and it may not normalize for weeks to months as calcium deposition leads to skeletal healing with vitamin D repletion.

● Urinary calcium may be low or normal in individuals with vitamin D deficiency and primary hyperparathyroidism, but it will normalize or become elevated with vitamin D repletion.

● Urinary calcium will be high in patients with idiopathic hypercalciuria, a cause of secondary hyperparathyroidism.

The evaluation of a high PTH with a high or low calcium is reviewed in other Laboratory Interpretation monographs. (See "High serum calcium and high parathyroid hormone in adults" and "Low serum calcium and high parathyroid hormone in adults" .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal range for serum calcium is approximately 8.6 to 10.2 mg/dL (2.15 to 2.54 mmol/L). In patients with hypo- or hyperalbuminemia, the measured calcium concentration should be corrected for the abnormality in albumin, using ( calculator 1 ) for mg/dL or ( calculator 2 ) for standard international units. The normal range for intact PTH is approximately 10 to 65 pg/mL (1.1 to 6.9 pmol/L). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
