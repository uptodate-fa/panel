INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Measurements of serum cortisol are extremely useful for the assessment of adrenal function. However, an elevated serum cortisol does not establish the diagnosis of endogenous hypercortisolism (eg, Cushing syndrome [CS]). For patients with suspected CS, serum cortisol is most often measured after dexamethasone suppression.

Before evaluation for possible CS, it is essential to exclude exogenous glucocorticoid intake and physiologic hypercortisolism, as these patients should not be evaluated for endogenous CS. (See "Establishing the diagnosis of Cushing syndrome", section on 'Overview of diagnostic approach'.)

Exclude exogenous glucocorticoid intake — Patients with chronic exposure to any glucocorticoids may exhibit symptoms and signs of CS (table 1). The most common cause of clinical features of CS is use of prescribed glucocorticoids (including oral, injected, or topical preparations) usually for nonendocrine disease. Glucocorticoids also may be present in supplements for “adrenal support” or herbal preparations, as well as “tonics” and skin bleaching creams. Apart from hydrocortisone, most of these compounds do not cross-react in cortisol assays. However, exogenously administered hydrocortisone can lead to elevations in the cortisol assay. If a patient is taking glucocorticoids, re-evaluate for endogenous CS after discontinuation.

Exclude physiologic hypercortisolism — Hypercortisolism can occur in several disorders other than CS. Clinically, patients with these physiologic forms of hypercortisolism seldom have the cutaneous (ie, easy bruising, thinning, and friability) or muscle (ie, proximal muscle atrophy and weakness) signs of CS. However, they may have some clinical features of CS (eg, hypertension, obesity) that are common in the general population.

Examples of conditions associated with physiologic hypercortisolism that may have some clinical features of CS (eg, hypertension, weight gain, depression or anxiety, diabetes) include:

●Pregnancy

●Patients with severe obesity, especially those with visceral obesity or polycystic ovary syndrome

●Patients with psychologic stress, especially patients with a severe major depressive disorder and melancholic symptoms

●Poorly controlled diabetes mellitus

●Rarely, chronic alcoholism or withdrawal from alcohol

●Physical stress (illness, hospitalization/surgery, pain)

●Obstructive sleep apnea

Examples of conditions associated with physiologic hypercortisolism that are unlikely to have clinical features of CS include:

●Malnutrition, anorexia nervosa

●Intense chronic exercise

●Hypothalamic amenorrhea

●High corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG) (eg, estrogens, including oral contraceptives)

●Glucocorticoid resistance

We suggest against routine testing for CS in patients with possible physiologic hypercortisolism unless they develop predictive features (eg, facial plethora, wide purplish-red stria, proximal muscle weakness, or easy bruising); severe, unexplained symptoms (resistant hypertension, severe osteoporosis); or continue to develop additional features of CS over time. (See "Establishing the diagnosis of Cushing syndrome", section on 'Exclude physiologic hypercortisolism'.)

In general, physiologic hypercortisolism resolves when the associated condition resolves. Therefore, it is best to treat the underlying condition first and then monitor the patient for the development of predictive, severe, or multiple CS symptoms. Test for CS if needed (as indicated by symptoms) when the associated condition resolves.

Suspected Cushing syndrome — The possible presence of CS is suggested by certain symptoms and signs (eg, facial plethora, wide purple-red striae, proximal muscle weakness, easy bruising) (table 1). Other findings (eg, obesity, hypertension, osteoporosis, menstrual changes, glucose intolerance) are common in the general population. However, CS may be present if they occur at an unusual stage of life (eg, hypertension or osteoporosis in a young person) or if multiple and progressive features are present (eg, worsening control of diabetes or hypertension).

Most patients with CS have early morning serum cortisol concentrations within or slightly above the normal range. An elevated morning serum cortisol does not establish the diagnosis of CS. In contrast, serum cortisol concentrations one hour after sleep are almost always high (greater than 7.5 mcg/dL [207 nmol/L]) and are often equal to the early morning values (ie, they have an abnormal or absent circadian rhythm).

First-line diagnostic tests — First-line tests used to establish the diagnosis of CS include:

●Late-night salivary cortisol

●24-hour urine free cortisol (UFC)

●1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (DST)

For 24-hour UFC and late-night salivary cortisol, two or more measurements of each test must be abnormal for it to be considered abnormal, because hypercortisolism may be variable. For patients with mild or fluctuating disease, this may require collecting a number of salivary cortisols or UFCs over weeks.

●For patients with a low index of suspicion (eg, woman with oligomenorrhea and hirsutism), obtain one of the first-line tests (with repeated measurement for 24-hour UFC or salivary cortisol).

●For patients with a high index of suspicion, such as those with features predictive of CS, those with multiple symptoms that accumulate over time, or those with symptoms that are unusually severe or unexpected for the patient's age, obtain two or three first-line tests (with repeated measurement for 24-hour UFC or salivary cortisol.

Normal results — If initial testing is normal in an individual with a low index of suspicion for CS, it is unlikely that the patient has CS unless it is extremely mild or cyclic. We do not suggest additional evaluation unless high index of suspicion (eg, symptoms progress or cyclic CS is suspected). In this case, we suggest referral to an endocrinologist for repeat testing and further evaluation.

Any abnormal result — In patients with at least one abnormal test result (which could represent true CS or a false-positive result), additional evaluation is necessary. Additional evaluation may include repeating the initial test or other first-line tests and obtaining an endocrinology consultation. The diagnosis of CS is established when at least two different first-line tests are unequivocally abnormal and cannot be explained by other conditions that cause physiologic hypercortisolism. (See "Establishing the diagnosis of Cushing syndrome", section on 'Any abnormal result' and "Establishing the diagnosis of Cushing syndrome", section on 'Available tests'.)

Once the diagnosis is established, additional evaluation is done to identify the cause of the hypercortisolism, typically in consultation with an endocrinologist. (See "Establishing the cause of Cushing syndrome".)

Adrenal incidentaloma — An adrenal incidentaloma is a mass lesion greater than 1 cm in diameter, serendipitously discovered by radiologic examination, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). All patients with adrenal incidentalomas should be evaluated for the possibility of malignancy and hormonal hyperfunction (eg, dysregulated cortisol secretion or mild CS, pheochromocytoma, primary aldosteronism). Although dysregulated cortisol secretion (ie, glucocorticoid secretory autonomy without specific clinical manifestations of CS, previously termed “subclinical” CS) is the most frequent hormonal abnormality detected in patients with adrenal incidentalomas, random, morning, or late-night serum cortisol levels are not indicated in this setting. Instead, dysregulated cortisol secretion should be ruled out by dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) measurement and performing a 1 mg overnight DST. Of note, the overnight DST should not be performed if the patient is thought to have a pheochromocytoma based upon the initial imaging study. (See "Evaluation and management of the adrenal incidentaloma", section on 'Evaluation for hormonal secretion'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The serum cortisol concentration normally reflects that of corticotropin (ACTH) and, therefore, has circadian rhythmicity. Serum cortisol concentrations are higher in the early morning (approximately 6 AM), ranging from 10 to 20 mcg/dL (275 to 555 nmol/L), and lower in the afternoon, with 4 PM values ranging from 2 to 14 mcg/dL (55 to 386 nmol/L). The concentrations are lowest, less than 5 mcg/dL (140 nmol/L), one hour after the usual time of sleep. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
