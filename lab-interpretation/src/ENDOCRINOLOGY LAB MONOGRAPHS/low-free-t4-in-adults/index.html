EVALUATE THYROID DYSFUNCTION — 

In the absence of pituitary or hypothalamic disease, if steady-state conditions exist, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) should be sufficient to assess thyroid function. Otherwise, free thyroxine (T4) is measured with TSH, or, if the TSH has already been determined to be abnormal, free T4 is measured with a repeat TSH, and they are interpreted together.

Free T4 assays measure the free or unbound hormone that is available for uptake into cells, whereas total T4 assays measure both bound and unbound (free) T4. Most commercial laboratories use an automated free T4 assay. Total T4 is not typically measured but may be useful during pregnancy when free T4 assays may be inaccurate due to changes in binding proteins. (See "Laboratory assessment of thyroid function", section on 'Laboratory tests used to assess thyroid function' and "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Thyroid adaptation during normal pregnancy'.)

Ambulatory setting, nonpregnant adults

TSH high

●TSH ≥10 mU/L – Patients with a persistently elevated TSH (eg, ≥10 mU/L) and a low free T4 have primary hypothyroidism. The most common cause of primary hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient areas of the world is chronic autoimmune (Hashimoto's) thyroiditis, indicated by the presence of high serum concentrations of antibodies to thyroid peroxidase (TPO) (table 1). However, we do not routinely measure TPO antibodies in patients with primary overt hypothyroidism because almost all have chronic autoimmune thyroiditis. (See "Diagnosis of and screening for hypothyroidism in nonpregnant adults", section on 'Diagnosis' and "Disorders that cause hypothyroidism" and "Treatment of primary hypothyroidism in adults", section on 'Initial dose'.)

●TSH upper limit of normal to 10 mU/L – A patient with a low serum total or free T4 concentration (eg, free T4 0.6 to 0.8 ng/dL [7.7 to 10.3 pmol/L]; normal range 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL [10.3 to 23.3 pmol/L]) and a slightly high serum TSH concentration (eg, 5 to 10 mU/L [normal range 0.5 to 5.0 mU/L]) could have either primary or central hypothyroidism. In the latter setting, the slightly high TSH is due to secretion of biologically inactive TSH. Central hypothyroidism should be suspected in patients with known hypothalamic or pituitary disease and when there are symptoms and signs of other hormonal deficiencies. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'TSH slightly high'.)

To distinguish between primary and central hypothyroidism in patients with suspected central hypothyroidism, measure:

•TPO antibodies

•Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), along with estradiol or testosterone (in women and men, respectively)

•Prolactin

Obtain endocrinology consult for further evaluation and management of suspected central hypothyroidism.

TSH normal — In the setting of a low free T4, a serum TSH within the normal reference range may be inappropriately normal due to pituitary or hypothalamic disease (central hypothyroidism). However, certain drugs, especially antiseizure medications (eg, phenytoin and carbamazepine), may be associated with an artifactually low serum free T4 and a normal serum TSH, mimicking central hypothyroidism. The majority of patients with true central hypothyroidism have coexisting deficiencies in other pituitary hormones, although isolated TSH deficiency may also occur.

Obtain endocrinology consult to evaluate for coexisting pituitary hormone abnormalities. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Evaluation'.)

TSH low — In an ambulatory setting, the combination of low TSH (confirmed with repeat measurement) and low free T4 is uncommon and may be due to central hypothyroidism. However, individuals with chronic illness or receiving high-dose glucocorticoids (>20 mg/day prednisone or its equivalent) may have a similar pattern. (See 'Hospitalized or ill patients' below.)

The majority of patients with central hypothyroidism have coexisting deficiencies in other pituitary hormones, although isolated TSH deficiency may also occur. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Evaluation'.)

Obtain endocrinology consult to evaluate for coexisting pituitary hormone abnormalities.

Hospitalized or ill patients — In general, free T4 and TSH should not be assessed in seriously ill patients unless there is a strong suspicion of thyroid dysfunction, since there are many other factors in acutely or chronically ill euthyroid patients that influence thyroid function tests. Euthyroid patients with nonthyroidal illness, especially those receiving high-dose glucocorticoids (>20 mg/day prednisone or its equivalent) or dopamine, may have low serum TSH and low or low-normal serum T4 (and low triiodothyronine [T3]) concentrations (see "Thyroid function in nonthyroidal illness" and "Drug interactions with thyroid hormones", section on 'Drugs that affect TSH secretion'):

●In patients without a strong suspicion of thyroid dysfunction, obtain repeat thyroid tests (TSH, free T4) after recovery/discharge.

●In patients with a strong suspicion of thyroid dysfunction while hospitalized:

•Repeat free T4

•Repeat TSH

•Total T4

If free T4 remains low, TSH is normal or subnormal, and true central hypothyroidism due to hypothalamic or pituitary disease remains in the differential diagnosis, measurement of serum cortisol can be helpful, as it would be elevated in critical illness and low (or inappropriately normal) in patients with true central hypothyroidism. (See "Thyroid function in nonthyroidal illness", section on 'Biochemical tests'.)

If free T4 remains low and TSH is high, treatment with levothyroxine may be appropriate, depending on the TSH and free T4 levels, clinical suspicion of hypothyroidism, and degree of nonthyroidal illness. (See "Thyroid function in nonthyroidal illness", section on 'Biochemical tests'.)

Pregnant women — Because of the changes in thyroid physiology during pregnancy, thyroid function tests should be interpreted using population-based, trimester-specific reference ranges for TSH and assay method and trimester-specific reference ranges for serum free T4. Free T4 measurements may be unreliable in some patients in the second and third trimesters due to changes in binding proteins during pregnancy and other factors. In such settings where free T4 measurements appear discordant with TSH measurements, total T4 should also be measured.

The upper reference ranges for total T4 and total T3 shift upward by approximately 5 percent per week, beginning at week 7. At approximately 16 weeks, normal total T4 (and total T3) levels can be as high as 1.5-fold times the upper limit of the reference ranges for nonpregnant women (due to thyroxine-binding globulin [TBG] excess). (See "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'Trimester-specific reference ranges'.)

If the free T4 is below assay normal using a reference range for pregnancy, further evaluation depends on the TSH levels (initial and repeated values):

●TSH >4 mU/L – Initiate levothyroxine and obtain consultation (if needed) with a clinician experienced with managing thyroid dysfunction during pregnancy.

●TSH normal – In the second or third trimester, this constellation of findings is likely due to pregnancy-related changes in binding proteins and limitations in the free T4 assay. Measurement of total T4 can be used as reassurance that the low free T4 is an assay issue. In the first trimester, the combination of low free T4 with a normal (or low) TSH is very uncommon. If confirmed, obtain endocrinology consultation.

●TSH low – The combination of low TSH and low free T4 during pregnancy is unusual. During the first trimester, TSH is subnormal in up to 15 percent of women due to human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) stimulation of the thyroid, but free T4 is not typically low. During the second and third trimester, however, free T4 may be low due to pregnancy-related changes in binding proteins and free T4 assay limitations. It is possible, especially in a patient with overt hyperthyroidism associated with hyperemesis and high hCG in the first trimester, to have low TSH values that persist into the second trimester. Measurement of total T4 can be used as reassurance that the low free T4 is an assay issue. But if this pattern persists, obtain endocrine consultation.

(See "Hypothyroidism during pregnancy: Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and treatment", section on 'Diagnosis' and "Overview of thyroid disease and pregnancy", section on 'hCG and thyroid function'.)

MONITOR LEVOTHYROXINE THERAPY — 

Measurement of free T4 alone is used to titrate the levothyroxine dose in patients with central hypothyroidism due to hypothalamic or pituitary disease. Free T4 is infrequently used to monitor levothyroxine treatment in patients with primary hypothyroidism:

●Central hypothyroidism – For patients with central hypothyroidism and low free T4, the dose of levothyroxine should be raised, and the free T4 remeasured six to eight weeks later, aiming to maintain the serum free T4 concentration slightly higher than mid-normal. (See "Central hypothyroidism", section on 'Treatment'.)

●Primary hypothyroidism or thyroid cancer – It is usually not necessary to measure free T4 in patients being monitored for the treatment of primary hypothyroidism or thyroid cancer. However, if the TSH is well above normal, it is helpful to measure the free T4 to determine the appropriate dose increase. TSH should be remeasured six to eight weeks after each dose increase. (See "Treatment of primary hypothyroidism in adults", section on 'Adjustment of maintenance dose' and "Differentiated thyroid cancer: Overview of management", section on 'Ongoing management based on dynamic risk stratification'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for serum free T4 in nonpregnant adults is approximately 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL (10.3 to 23.3 pmol/L), and for total T4, 5 to 12 mcg/dL (64 to 154 nmol/L). The normal reference ranges can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
