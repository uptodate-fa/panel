INITIAL EVALUATION — 

There is an inverse relationship between the circulating concentration of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and the number of ovarian follicles, except at the time of ovulation. An elevated FSH (>30 milli-international units/mL) generally occurs when the ovarian pool of follicles is depleted. When the ovary contains no functional follicles, a menopausal state is present, and FSH levels are very elevated. When the number of ovarian follicles is very low, but a few functional follicles are present, the patient is usually in a menopause transition, and the FSH level is modestly elevated or oscillates between normal and elevated.

Assessment of irregular or absent menstrual cycles — FSH is a component of the initial laboratory evaluation of women with irregular or absent menstrual cycles. Other tests that are part of the initial evaluation include (see "Evaluation and management of secondary amenorrhea"):

●Serum human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)

●Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)

●Prolactin

Although abnormalities in hCG, TSH, and prolactin are typically associated with a low or normal FSH, any levels outside the range should be addressed first. (See "Positive hCG in women" and "High thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) in adults" and "Low thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) in adults" and "High prolactin in adults".)  

In the setting of irregular or absent menstrual cycles, a confirmed high serum FSH concentration usually indicates the diagnosis of menopause or primary ovarian insufficiency (POI):

●Age >45 years – In otherwise healthy women, menopause or menopausal transition is likely. Menopause may be diagnosed after 12 months of amenorrhea in the absence of other biologic or physiologic causes. A high serum FSH is not required to make the diagnosis. (See "Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of menopause".)

●Age ≤45 years – Obtain repeat FSH with concomitant estradiol (E2; on day 3 of a menstrual cycle, if possible, or random day sample):

•FSH high, E2 low or normal

-40 to 45 years of age – For otherwise healthy women 40 to 45 years of age with negative hCG and normal prolactin and TSH, menopause or menopause transition is likely.

-<40 years of age – For women under age 40 years, a high serum FSH concentration with a low or normal E2 indicates POI. Most women should be referred to endocrinology for further evaluation and management. Diagnosis is important in order to explain to the patient the variable natural history of the condition, to look for associated diseases, and, most importantly, to treat the hypogonadism. (See "Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of primary ovarian insufficiency (premature ovarian failure)", section on 'Diagnosis'.)

For women <40 years without an obvious precipitating factor for POI (gonadotoxic chemotherapy or radiotherapy), additional testing to rule out the most common etiologies of POI should be performed, including a karyotype to look for Turner syndrome (including mosaicism). In women with 46,XX spontaneous POI, we also suggest testing for anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies and the fragile X premutation (FMR1 gene). Testing for 21-hydroxylase antibodies may serve the dual purpose of screening for autoimmune adrenal insufficiency and making the diagnosis of autoimmune oophoritis. (See "Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of primary ovarian insufficiency (premature ovarian failure)", section on 'Additional evaluation once diagnosis is made'.)

•FSH high, E2 high – In the setting of irregular or absent menstrual cycles, the combination of high FSH and high E2 is uncommon.

Although rare, gonadotropin-secreting pituitary tumors may present with high FSH and high E2 (prolactin may also be elevated). Mild ovarian hyperstimulation has been reported in premenopausal women with these tumors. The slightly, but persistently, elevated serum FSH concentrations lead to recruitment of multiple dominant follicles, high serum E2 concentrations (>500 pg/mL), and thickened endometrium on pelvic ultrasound (potentially suggestive of endometrial hyperplasia). (See "Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of gonadotroph and nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas".)

In women with androgen-secreting tumors (adrenal or ovarian), E2 may be high. FSH should be suppressed, but in women in the menopausal transition with elevated FSH, FSH is unlikely to be suppressed into the normal premenopausal range. (See "Clinical presentation and evaluation of adrenocortical tumors" and "Sex cord-stromal tumors of the ovary: Epidemiology, clinical features, and diagnosis in adults".)

Infertility — In addition to history and physical examination, several tests are performed during the initial evaluation of infertility. An FSH performed on day 3 of the menstrual cycle is widely used for screening ovarian reserve. An elevation of day 3 FSH correlates with poor response to ovarian stimulation during in vitro fertilization (IVF) procedures and poor pregnancy rates. Patients with infertility should be referred to a reproductive endocrinology specialist for evaluation and management. (See "Female infertility: Evaluation".)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal range for FSH varies with the menstrual cycle, ranging from 2 to 9 milli-international units/mL during the follicular/luteal phases and 4 to 22 milli-international units/mL at midcycle. The upper threshold for a normal FSH concentration is laboratory dependent; cutoff values of 10 to 30 milli-international units/mL have been reported because of use of different FSH assay reference standards and assay methodologies. In essentially all assays, however, an FSH >30 milli-international units/mL is seldom observed except in the presence of a depleted pool of follicles (eg, menopause or the menopause transition). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
