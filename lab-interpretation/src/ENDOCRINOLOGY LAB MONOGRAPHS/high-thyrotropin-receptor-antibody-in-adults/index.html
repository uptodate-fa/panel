INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Thyrotropin receptor antibodies (TRAbs) are classified as stimulating, blocking, or neutral. Stimulating antibodies (thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins [TSIs]) cause Graves' disease. Thyroid receptor-blocking antibodies are an unusual cause of hypothyroidism. Some patients will have a mixture of both antibodies, and depending on the relative titers of these antibodies, they may fluctuate between hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism. Neutral antibodies bind the receptor but do not stimulate or block function.

There are two methods for measuring TRAb:

● Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor-binding inhibitor immunoglobulin (TBII or TBI) assays measure stimulating, blocking, and neutral antibodies.

● TSI assays measure only thyroid-stimulating antibodies.

Some third-generation TBI assays are automated and less expensive but are not specific for stimulating antibodies. There are no assays for blocking antibodies available in the United States, but one such assay is available in Europe. When considering the presence of blocking antibodies in patients with fluctuating hyper- and hypothyroidism (or initial hyperthyroidism followed by hypothyroidism), a negative TSI level and a positive TBI level, or a TBI level that is considerably higher than the TSI level, is indirect evidence for the possible presence of blocking antibodies.

Further evaluation and management depends upon the reason for measuring TRAb.

Establishing the cause of hyperthyroidism  —  Elevated TRAbs in a person with hyperthyroidism confirms the diagnosis of Graves' disease. TRAb measurements are a more rapid and cost-effective alternative to obtaining a radioiodine uptake scan when Graves' disease is suspected. TRAbs (either TSI or TBII, TBI assays) are elevated in 96 to 97 percent of patients with Graves' disease. However, a nonelevated level does not rule out Graves' disease, especially in a patient with subclinical hyperthyroidism. If TRAb is not elevated, a radioiodine uptake scan should be obtained. (See "Diagnosis of hyperthyroidism", section on 'Determining the etiology' .)

Predicting fetal/neonatal Graves' disease  —  Pregnant women with hyperthyroidism caused by Graves' disease (active or treated) may have fetuses or neonates with hyperthyroidism due to transplacental crossing of stimulating antibodies. Measurement of maternal serum TRAb helps to predict which infants are at higher risk for development of fetal and neonatal Graves' hyperthyroidism:

● Past history of treated Graves' disease – Euthyroid pregnant women taking thyroid hormone after definitive therapy with radioiodine or surgery have a small risk of transplacental transfer of TRAb. TRAb is measured during the first trimester and, if elevated, at 18 to 22, and again at 30 to 34, weeks of gestation.

Measurement of TRAb is not necessary for women in remission after stopping a course of thionamides, as thyroid function tests that confirm euthyroidism indicate the absence of elevated TRAb levels.

● Present history of Graves' disease – In pregnant women with present history of Graves' disease, TRAb is measured during the first trimester and, if elevated, at 18 to 22 weeks and, if still elevated, at 30 to 34 weeks of gestation.

The fetus or infant is more likely to have Graves' hyperthyroidism when the maternal TRAb value is more than three to five times the upper limit of normal. In this setting, increased monitoring for signs of fetal or neonatal thyrotoxicosis is necessary. TRAb levels often decrease during pregnancy, so that neonatal Graves' disease is a rather rare occurrence. However, TRAb levels rebound 4 to 12 months postpartum, often leading to recurrent or worsening hyperthyroidism in women who have not received definitive therapy. (See "Hyperthyroidism during pregnancy: Treatment", section on 'Fetal or neonatal hyperthyroidism' and "Evaluation and management of neonatal Graves disease", section on 'Diagnosis' .)

Determining the likelihood of remission of Graves' disease  —  After a 12- to 24-month course of thionamide therapy (eg, methimazole), TRAbs are measured to determine the likelihood of remission of the underlying Graves' disease. Patients with normal levels have a greater chance of remission when methimazole is discontinued. Persistently elevated TRAbs are associated with high relapse rates. (See "Thionamides in the treatment of Graves' disease", section on 'Approach to stopping therapy' .)

Establishing the cause of apparent thyroid-associated orbitopathy  —  Some patients who present with apparent thyroid-associated eye disease (including proptosis, periorbital edema, and lid retraction) have normal thyroid function. This can be confusing diagnostically. However, 25 to 75 percent of such patients have high levels of TRAbs (euthyroid Graves' disease). While some patients go on to develop typical Graves' hyperthyroidism over a period of years, some become hypothyroid, and others remain euthyroid. (See "Clinical features and diagnosis of thyroid eye disease", section on 'Clinical features' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for TSI, expressed relative to non-Graves' sera, is either 1±0.3 or 100±30 percent. The normal reference range for TBII or TBI is assay dependent. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
