INITIAL EVALUATION — 

The factor V Leiden (FVL) point mutation increases the risk of venous thrombosis by making factor Va resistant to the first cleavage by activated protein C (APC) (figure 1). This risk is further increased in women using oral contraceptives and during pregnancy and the postpartum period. However, most individuals with FVL mutation have a "laboratory-only abnormality," as only 5 to 10 percent of heterozygous individuals will develop venous thrombosis in their lifetime.

The presence of the FVL mutation can be tested using genomic DNA from peripheral blood cells. Clot-based functional assays (APC resistance assays) can also be used to screen for the presence of the FVL mutation. These commercially available assays using factor V-deficient plasma are highly sensitive and specific for the presence of the FVL mutation for patients who are not receiving any parenteral or oral anticoagulants. If a patient is found to have APC resistance, genetic testing should be performed to confirm the presence of the FVL mutation.

Positive DNA analysis for FVL mutation — Positive genetic testing for the FVL mutation provides definitive evidence for its presence in the heterozygous or homozygous form. Relative risks for a first episode of venous thromboembolism (VTE) are highest in homozygous individuals with FVL or those with compound heterozygosity (FVL plus a different thrombophilic defect). (See "Factor V Leiden and activated protein C resistance", section on 'Clinical manifestations'.)

VTE is the major clinical manifestation of the FVL mutation:

●Contraception counseling of women with the FVL mutation should address the risks of both VTE and unintended pregnancy for all contraceptive methods in order to enable these women to make an informed choice. (See "Contraception: Counseling regarding inherited thrombophilias", section on 'Individualized risk-based counseling'.)

●VTE prophylaxis should be provided as already indicated in the general population (eg, major surgery/trauma, hospitalization).

Routine testing of first-degree relatives of an individual with FVL mutation is not indicated, especially in cases in which the information will not alter management. Situations in which testing of relatives may be appropriate include (see "Hereditary thrombophilia testing in adults without VTE"):

●First-degree relatives of affected individuals in families with FVL mutation and a strong history of thrombosis, including VTE in individuals <50 years of age; individuals with VTE in an unusual location; or individuals with severe, life-threatening VTE.

●Daughters of parents with FVL, in the setting of contraceptive counseling.

●Siblings of individuals who are homozygous for the FVL mutation or compound heterozygotes for FVL and another inherited thrombophilia. In these cases, parents and children may only inherit a single gene defect, whereas siblings are at risk to inherit genetic defects from both parents.

Additional evaluation of the patient is based on whether the patient has a history of thrombosis. This applies to both provoked and unprovoked VTE, but the risk of recurrent VTE is higher if the episode was unprovoked.

Negative personal history of thrombosis — Although asymptomatic carriers of FVL mutation have an increased relative risk of VTE, the absolute annual risk of VTE is low for individuals with heterozygous FVL mutations. Approximately 5 to 10 percent of FVL heterozygotes will experience VTE during their lifetime.

The majority of patients who have an identified FVL mutation, but no history of thrombosis (eg, a "laboratory-only finding"), do not require testing for other prothrombotic mutations. Individuals with a familial history of VTE should try to determine if clinically affected family members have undergone evaluation and been found to have a hereditary defect.

Positive personal history of thrombosis — Patients who have been identified to have the FVL mutation and a history of thrombosis occasionally have other thrombophilic defects, including deficiencies of protein C (PC), protein S (PS), and antithrombin (AT); the prothrombin gene mutation; and elevated levels of factor VIII. Carriers of two defects are at a higher risk for thrombosis than those with a single defect. For individuals with FVL who warrant thrombophilia testing based upon their history and clinical presentation with VTE, obtain (review):

●Genetic testing for the prothrombin G20210A mutation

●Functional PC assay

●Free PS antigen assay

●Functional AT assay (also called AT-heparin cofactor assay)

Results for PC, PS, and AT testing may be inaccurate in the setting of acute VTE or oral anticoagulant therapy (with warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant) (table 1) and may be deferred until the acute episode has resolved and the patient is no longer on anticoagulation. The antigenic levels of PC, PS, and AT, however, are not affected by the direct oral anticoagulants. (See "Factor V Leiden and activated protein C resistance", section on 'Post-diagnosis testing and testing of first-degree relatives'.)

In addition, some functional assays for PS can give falsely low levels in the presence of the FVL mutation.

Abnormal APC resistance — APC assays are performed infrequently due to difficulties with interpretation, need for confirmatory genetic testing, and declining cost of genetic testing. APC resistance assays are generally reported as positive, borderline, or negative.

In some laboratories, a positive result for APC resistance will automatically prompt confirmatory genetic testing for the FVL mutation. If not, obtain genetic testing for the FVL mutation:

●DNA analysis positive for FVL – (See 'Positive DNA analysis for FVL mutation' above.)

●DNA analysis negative for FVL – If the FVL mutation is not detected, APC resistance may be due to a different mutation associated with APC resistance or to a factor that interferes with the assay (eg, lupus anticoagulant, anticoagulation). In such cases, obtain a hematology consultation. (See "Factor V Leiden and activated protein C resistance", section on 'Other causes of aPC resistance'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

Genetic testing is reported as either positive or negative. APC resistance screening for FVL mutation is reported as positive, borderline, or negative. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based on the reference range reported for that result.
