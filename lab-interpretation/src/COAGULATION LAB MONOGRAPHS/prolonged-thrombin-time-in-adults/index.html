ALGORITHM — 

(algorithm 1)

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

If the patient is acutely ill due to coagulopathy and/or bleeding, provide supportive care while awaiting the results of a diagnostic evaluation. Obtain specialty consultation, as appropriate. (See "Approach to the adult with a suspected bleeding disorder", section on 'Laboratory evaluation' and "Approach to the adult with a suspected bleeding disorder", section on 'Positive bleeding history and abnormal screening tests'.)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Unlike the prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), the thrombin time (TT) is not used as an initial screening test for hemostatic abnormalities. The TT is useful for evaluating individuals with prolongation of both the PT and aPTT. It is prolonged if an anticoagulant that inhibits thrombin is present in the sample or in the setting of hypofibrinogenemia or dysfibrinogenemia. The TT measures the final step of coagulation: the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin (figure 1).

The TT should be interpreted in conjunction with the platelet count, PT, aPTT, and the clinical setting. In many patients, the likely diagnosis will be apparent from the history and physical examination. The most common causes of prolonged TT include:

●Medications (heparin and direct thrombin inhibitors)

●Advanced liver disease

●Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

●Fibrinogen disorders

Review tests usually available at the time of presentation:

●Complete blood count (CBC) with platelet count

●PT, aPTT

●Peripheral blood smear (to confirm CBC and to identify any microangiopathic abnormalities consistent with DIC)

●Liver biochemical tests, including alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase

●Plasma fibrinogen

●D-dimer assay (to assess possible DIC)

If a prolonged TT is unexplained, repeat the test using a sample from a peripheral vein (ie, without heparin contamination) and consult with the laboratory specialist or a hematologist.

If an anticoagulant is the suspected cause of a persistently prolonged TT, obtain another functional clotting assay, the reptilase time (RT). The TT is sensitive to thrombin inhibitor anticoagulants (heparin, dabigatran, argatroban) and to fibrinogen abnormalities; the RT is sensitive to fibrinogen abnormalities but not to heparin and the thrombin inhibitor anticoagulants.

Prolonged TT, normal RT — Both heparin and direct thrombin inhibitors prolong the TT but not the RT.

Prolonged TT, prolonged RT — Prolonged TT and prolonged RT may be due to conditions associated with acquired dysfibrinogenemias (usually advanced liver disease and/or DIC) or due to rare inherited fibrinogen disorders. (See "Disorders of fibrinogen".)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The TT test is performed by incubating citrated plasma in the presence of dilute thrombin and measuring the time to clot formation. The normal range varies by laboratory and reagent instrument combination; in most cases, the TT is approximately 14 to 19 seconds. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based on the reference range reported with that result.
