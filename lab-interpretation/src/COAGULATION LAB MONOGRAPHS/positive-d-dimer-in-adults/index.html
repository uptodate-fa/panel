IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

Elevated D-dimer levels are found in a number of clinical situations, especially in hospitalized patients (table 1). The finding of extremely high D-dimer levels should prompt consideration of life-threatening disorders associated with an intense inflammatory response. For patients with COVID-19, a D-dimer level approximately twice normal or higher (eg, >1000 ng/mL [>1 mcg/mL]) is associated with severe disease. (See "COVID-19: Hypercoagulability", section on 'Management' and "Cytokine release syndrome (CRS)" and "COVID-19: Management in hospitalized adults", section on 'Patients with oxygen requirement/severe disease'.)

D-dimer is most often ordered to:

●Exclude deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) in nonpregnant patients with a low or intermediate pretest probability of venous thromboembolism (VTE) (algorithm 1)

●Evaluate for disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), in combination with a clinical assessment and other laboratory testing

Venous thromboembolism — The evaluation of patients with suspected VTE requires calculation of a pretest probability. Even with preferred high-sensitivity assays (eg, D-dimer test performed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA] or immunoturbidimetric techniques), precise quantification of D-dimer does not have sufficient specificity to use alone in the diagnosis of VTE, and does not have sufficient sensitivity to use alone for the exclusion of VTE in patients with high pretest probability. (See "Clinical presentation and diagnostic evaluation of the nonpregnant adult with suspected acute pulmonary embolism", section on 'Determine pretest probability' and "Clinical presentation and diagnosis of the nonpregnant adult with suspected deep vein thrombosis of the lower extremity", section on 'Initial approach (pretest probability)'.)

D-dimer levels increase during the course of a normal pregnancy and slowly decline postpartum. Interpretation of D-dimer levels during pregnancy and the puerperium is complicated by a lack of normal reference ranges in this setting. Thus, D-dimer levels have limited utility for the diagnosis of VTE in pregnancy. (See "Pulmonary embolism in pregnancy: Clinical presentation and diagnosis", section on 'Diagnostic approach'.)

The decision to empirically treat while diagnostic testing is being performed depends upon the clinical suspicion and the pace of diagnostic testing.

Suspected pulmonary embolism — Review:

●Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

●Serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose

●Chest radiograph

Patients with a positive D-dimer and suspected PE require imaging (algorithm 1). A conventional chest computed tomography (CT) without contrast or with contrast that is not timed to visualize the pulmonary arteries is not adequate to exclude PE. Assess feasibility of CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA).

Contraindications to CTPA include:

●Allergy to intravenous contrast.

●High risk of contrast nephropathy.

•Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <45 mL/min/1.73 m2

•Known diabetic nephropathy

•Hypotension

•Advanced heart failure

•Multiple myeloma

If no contraindication, perform CTPA.

●Positive CTPA – PE confirmed; provide treatment

●Inconclusive CTPA – Additional evaluation is warranted

●Negative CTPA – PE excluded

If there is a contraindication to CTPA and the chest radiograph is normal, perform ventilation perfusion (V/Q) scan and correlate with clinical probability of pulmonary emboli (table 2).

●High probability V/Q scan – PE likely.

●Indeterminate V/Q scan – Additional evaluation is warranted, usually lower extremity compression ultrasound (US) with Doppler to evaluate for DVT.

●Normal or near-normal probability V/Q scan – PE unlikely.

If there is a contraindication to CTPA and the chest radiograph is abnormal, perform lower extremity compression US with Doppler (whole leg or proximal leg if whole leg US imaging is not available).

●US positive – DVT confirmed and PE likely; provide treatment.

●US negative or nondiagnostic – Monitor for DVT with serial imaging, as appropriate. If clinical suspicion for PE is high (calculator 1), further imaging and/or empiric anticoagulation should be attempted.

Suspected deep venous thrombosis — Review CBC with differential and platelet count.

Patients with a positive D-dimer and suspected DVT require lower extremity imaging (algorithm 1). Perform lower extremity compression US with Doppler (whole leg or proximal leg if whole leg US imaging is not available).

●US positive – DVT confirmed; provide treatment.

●US nondiagnostic – Individualize further evaluation with serial US studies, as appropriate (calculator 2).

●US negative – DVT is excluded (if whole leg compression US performed); proximal DVT is excluded (if only proximal compression US is performed).

Disseminated intravascular coagulation — DIC is a clinical and laboratory diagnosis, based on findings of coagulopathy and/or fibrinolysis in the appropriate setting (eg, severe infection, malignancy, trauma, obstetric complications, or acute hemolytic transfusion reaction from ABO incompatibility) (table 3). (See "Evaluation and management of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in adults".)

No single laboratory test can accurately confirm or eliminate the diagnosis. Findings such as thrombocytopenia, prolonged prothrombin time (PT) or activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), low fibrinogen, and elevated D-dimer are considered to be relatively sensitive for the diagnosis but not specific. D-dimer is often elevated in the absence of DIC as part of normal pregnancy physiology. (See "Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) during pregnancy: Clinical findings, etiology, and diagnosis".)

Review:

●CBC with differential and platelet count

●Peripheral blood smear for schistocytes and other abnormalities

●PT, aPTT, fibrinogen

Obtain:

●Electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose

●Liver biochemical tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST)

●Factor VIII level in selected patients (often low in DIC and normal to high in severe liver disease)

Acute and chronic DIC represent two ends of a pathogenic balance between the production and the consumption of coagulation factors and platelets (table 4). (See "Evaluation and management of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in adults", section on 'Acute versus chronic DIC'.)

●Diagnosis of acute DIC is supported by laboratory evidence of thrombocytopenia, coagulation factor consumption (eg, prolonged PT, aPTT; low fibrinogen) and fibrinolysis (eg, increased D-dimer) in the appropriate clinical setting, as long as another etiology of these findings does not become apparent.

●Diagnosis of chronic DIC is supported by evidence of fibrinolysis (eg, increased D-dimer) in the absence of another etiology, such as VTE, in an appropriate clinical setting (eg, malignancy or less commonly, a vascular lesion).

Perform serial testing to determine if coagulation and fibrinolysis are worsening or improving.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

D-dimer is reported as either positive (≥ threshold cut-off) or negative (< threshold cut-off), based on the quantitative value reported for the patient and the D-dimer cut-off value reported by the individual laboratory. D-dimer levels increase with age and during normal pregnancy. There are no established normal reference ranges for the antepartum and postpartum periods.
