INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The initial evaluation of a patient with an abnormal prothrombin time (PT)/international normalized ratio (INR) and/or activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) depends on the reason for testing. PT/INR and aPTT are typically obtained to monitor anticoagulation, to monitor disorders known to cause prolongation of PT and/or aPTT (eg, disseminated intravascular coagulation [DIC], liver disease), or to evaluate abnormal bleeding or thrombosis.

Review medications for anticoagulant drugs ( table 1 ):

● For patients receiving anticoagulant drugs, adjust according to recommended therapeutic range. (See "Clinical use of coagulation tests", section on 'Anticoagulant monitoring' and "Warfarin and other VKAs: Dosing and adverse effects" and "Heparin and LMW heparin: Dosing and adverse effects" .)

● For patients with a disorder known to cause prolongation of PT and/or aPTT, evaluate and manage the underlying disorder.

● For patients not receiving anticoagulant drugs and without a known underlying disorder, confirm the abnormal result. If the blood was drawn from a heparinized venous access device, repeat sample from a peripheral vein (ie, without heparin contamination). (See "Clinical use of coagulation tests", section on 'Ensuring accuracy' .)

The evaluation described below presumes that the individual has a normal platelet count (and normal platelet function) and that the prolonged PT/INR or aPTT has been confirmed.

Abnormal or excessive bleeding  —  In patients with active bleeding or a history suggestive of abnormal/excessive bleeding, the pattern of abnormal results provides a presumptive diagnosis that can then be confirmed with specific testing ( table 2 ).

aPTT prolonged and PT/INR normal  —  A prolonged aPTT and a normal PT/INR may be caused by inherited disorders, including deficiencies of factors VIII (hemophilia A, severe von Willebrand disease [VWD]), IX (hemophilia B), or XI. Hemophilia A and B are the most common. This pattern may also be seen in patients during heparin administration ( table 1 ) or with acquired hemophilia (acquired antibodies to factor VIII). Factor VIII inhibitors have been described in association with malignancy, lymphoproliferative disorders, pregnancy, rheumatologic disorders, as well as in the absence of underlying disease ( table 2 ). (See "Approach to the adult with a suspected bleeding disorder", section on 'aPTT prolonged (PT normal)' .)

● Perform aPTT-based mixing study to test for an inhibitor:

• Inhibitor present – Test for heparin (eg, reptilase time) or acquired factor inhibitor. Review evidence for acquired disorders (eg, malignancy, rheumatologic disorder).

• Inhibitor absent – Obtain specialty consultation to guide testing for specific factor deficiencies (eg, factor VIII, von Willebrand factor [VWF] antigen, ristocetin cofactor activity for VWD; factor VIII and IX activity level for hemophilia).

PT/INR prolonged and aPTT normal  —  A prolonged PT/INR and a normal aPTT may be caused by warfarin administration ( table 1 ), severe liver disease (although these patients are likely to have thrombocytopenia as well), DIC, vitamin K deficiency, or uncommon problems with a coagulation factor in the extrinsic pathway (eg, factor VII deficiency, factor VII inhibitor) ( table 2 ).

In a patient with unexplained prolongation of PT/INR, obtain a PT-based mixing study to distinguish between a factor deficiency (eg, factor VII) and an inhibitor (eg, factor VII inhibitor). (See "Approach to the adult with a suspected bleeding disorder", section on 'PT prolonged (aPTT normal)' .)

PT/INR and aPTT prolonged  —  A prolonged PT/INR and aPTT may be caused by anticoagulants, especially when given in excess (eg, heparin, warfarin, argatroban, dabigatran, or the factor Xa inhibitors [apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban]) ( table 1 ), vitamin K deficiency, severe liver disease, DIC, or a deficiency of factors of the common pathway (X, V, II, and fibrinogen). In many cases, the clinical scenario will guide the direction, sequence, and extent of testing.

In a patient with unexplained, prolonged PT/INR and aPTT, obtain a mixing study. This is usually done on the coagulation test, aPTT, or PT that is most abnormal. If both are abnormal to the same degree, consider inhibitor screens for both aPTT and PT to determine whether an inhibitor or deficiency or a combination is responsible for the prolongation. For example, in patients with lupus anticoagulant and associated prothrombin deficiency, testing typically demonstrates prolongation of the aPTT (mixing study will show inhibitor pattern, corrected by adding excess phospholipid) and the PT/INR (mixing study corrects and factor II assay shows prothrombin deficiency due to clearance by prothrombin-specific lupus anticoagulant). (See "Approach to the adult with a suspected bleeding disorder", section on 'PT and aPTT both prolonged' .)

Thrombosis  —  Prolonged PT/INR and/or aPTT is uncommonly associated with thrombosis. In addition to anticoagulation commonly prescribed for thrombosis, conditions associated with prolonged PT/INR and/or aPTT include the following:

● Antiphospholipid syndrome (typically causes prolonged aPTT rather than PT) (see "Antiphospholipid syndrome: Diagnosis" )

● Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT; associated with low platelet count) (see "Clinical presentation and diagnosis of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia" )

● Chronic DIC (see "Evaluation and management of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in adults", section on 'Chronic DIC' )

Bleeding and thrombosis absent (incidental finding)  —  Prolonged PT/INR and/or aPTT without bleeding or thrombosis (eg, incidental finding) may be caused by any of the conditions described above or may be an incidental finding. For example, patients with factor XII deficiency have prolonged aPTT but no bleeding phenotype.

The evaluation includes a medication review and review of the patient's medical condition, which may narrow testing or suggest a sequential evaluation (eg, testing for lupus anticoagulant in an individual with systemic lupus erythematosus and a prolonged aPTT) ( table 2 ).

In patients with unexplained coagulation abnormalities, obtain:

● Repeat complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count, if not done recently

● Liver biochemical tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)

For individuals with persistent abnormalities, the pattern of PT and aPTT may suggest a presumptive diagnosis ( table 2 ) that can then be confirmed with specific testing. (See "Clinical use of coagulation tests", section on 'Prolonged PT and/or aPTT without bleeding or thrombosis' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal ranges for the PT and aPTT vary by laboratory and reagent/instrument combination and, therefore, should be interpreted based upon the reference range reported with that result. In most laboratories, the normal range for the aPTT is approximately 25 to 35 seconds and for the PT approximately 11 to 13 seconds.

The INR is calculated as a ratio of the patient's PT to a control PT obtained using an international reference thromboplastin reagent developed by the World Health Organization (WHO). Unlike the PT, the results of the INR will be similar on a blood sample tested in any laboratory using any thromboplastin reagent/instrument system when calibrated correctly.
