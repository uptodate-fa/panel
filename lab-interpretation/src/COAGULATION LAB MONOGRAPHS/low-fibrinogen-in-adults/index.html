INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Fibrinogen disorders can be quantitative or qualitative ( table 1 ). The fibrinogen level is usually measured by a functional assay with the result expressed as mg/dL (g/L). Low fibrinogen activity may indicate hypofibrinogenemia and/or dysfibrinogenemia. If required, a fibrinogen antigen level and thrombin time (TT) can be obtained to aide in distinguishing between these possibilities. This is typically done under the guidance of a coagulation specialist or hematologist.

Interpretation of a specific abnormal result depends on the reason it was ordered. Patients suspected of an acquired fibrinogen disorder may be acutely ill and may benefit from multispecialty consultation.

Review tests usually available at the time of presentation:

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count and peripheral blood smear report

● Prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)

● Serum chemistries, including electrolytes, creatinine, and liver biochemical tests

● Urinalysis

● D-dimer

Testing for inherited disorders of fibrinogen is generally pursued after exclusion of more common acquired conditions. If a primary disorder is suspected due to family or personal history or incidentally found in evaluating a prolonged PT or aPTT ( table 2 ), obtain consultation with a coagulation specialist. (See "Disorders of fibrinogen", section on 'Heritable (genetic) disorders' .)

Active or poorly controlled bleeding  —  Provide supportive care and address bleeding. Greater bleeding risk occurs in individuals with lower levels of functional fibrinogen, especially <100 mg/dL:

● Fibrinogen levels <100 mg/dL – In patients with active or poorly controlled bleeding or anticipated invasive surgical procedures, hematology consultation may be helpful to guide decision making about transfusion of blood products to maintain the level above the appropriate threshold (eg, to achieve fibrinogen levels to ≥100 mg/dL). (See "Disorders of fibrinogen", section on 'Acute bleeding' and "Disorders of fibrinogen", section on 'Elective surgery' .)

Obtain serial fibrinogen levels and CBC with platelets.

● Fibrinogen levels ≥100 mg/dL – Fibrinogen levels ≥100 mg/dL are often sufficient to prevent clinical bleeding. However, the level of fibrinogen does not always predict which patients will develop bleeding or thrombosis. Address contributing factors, such as thrombocytopenia, depletion of coagulation factors, and/or anticoagulant therapy.

Acute illness with end-organ failure  —  For patients with acute illness with end-organ failure, low fibrinogen indicates a coagulopathy that should be urgently addressed. As examples:

● Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) – Findings of coagulopathy (thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT, aPTT, TT) and/or profound fibrinolysis (markedly increased D-dimer) support the diagnosis of DIC in acutely ill patients with end-organ failure in the absence of another cause ( table 3 ). (See "Evaluation and management of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in adults", section on 'Diagnostic evaluation' .)

● Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) – HLH is an aggressive, life-threatening syndrome of excessive immune activation, most commonly triggered by a hematologic malignancy (eg, non-Hodgkin lymphoma) or infection. Findings of fever with multiple organ involvement, neurologic changes, cytopenia (especially anemia and thrombocytopenia), liver enzyme abnormalities, and very high ferritin levels suggest the diagnosis of HLH; patients may have a coagulopathy due to impaired hepatic synthetic function or DIC. (See "Clinical features and diagnosis of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis" .)

Evaluate for underlying triggers of DIC and/or HLH, as well as other possible causes of bleeding, thrombosis, and/or laboratory abnormalities. Review tests usually available at the time of presentation of an acute febrile illness:

● Blood and fluid cultures

● Radiographic imaging of likely source of infection and/or tissue injury

● Serum ferritin, if suspected HLH

Obtain multidisciplinary specialty consultation, as appropriate.

Chronic liver disease or liver injury  —  Hemostatic abnormalities (eg, coagulation factor defects, varying degrees of thrombocytopenia) in patients with decreased hepatic synthetic function may be indistinguishable from laboratory findings in DIC. D-dimer levels are generally normal or mildly elevated in liver disease and markedly elevated in DIC (due to more profound fibrinolysis). However, significant overlap exists, and patients with chronic liver disease are at increased risk for developing acute DIC, bleeding, and/or thrombosis.

Consider consultation with an expert in liver disease, especially if the patient is acutely ill. (See "Hemostatic abnormalities in patients with liver disease", section on 'Liver disease versus DIC' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The reference range for plasma fibrinogen, measured as either activity or antigen, is approximately 200 to 400 mg/dL (g/L). Measurement of fibrinogen activity is based on the formation of a fibrin clot after the addition of thrombin. Immunologic techniques are used to measure fibrinogen antigen. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based on the reference range reported with that result.
