INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

The diagnosis of HIV infection is established by detecting either antibodies to HIV or viral markers (HIV RNA or p24 antigen) in peripheral blood. The following approach is consistent with recommendations from the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention ( CDC ). (See "Screening and diagnostic testing for HIV infection in adults" and "Initial evaluation of adults with HIV", section on 'Establishing the diagnosis' .)

The preferred test for initial screening is the combination HIV antigen/antibody test ( table 1 ):

● Combination antigen/antibody test – The combination antigen/antibody test can detect HIV-1 and HIV-2 antibodies as well as HIV p24 antigen in early infection when antibody may not yet be present. Although laboratory and point-of-care assays are available, the test designed for laboratory use is preferred due to better sensitivity.

• Negative test – In most settings, if the combination antigen/antibody assay is negative, the person is considered not to have HIV, and no further testing is required. Additional testing should be performed if there are reasons to suspect the test may be a false negative (eg, concern for acute HIV or recent risk or exposure). (See "Acute and early HIV infection: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Diagnosis' .)

• Positive test – If the test is positive, most laboratories will perform a second assay of a different type to confirm the results. If a confirmatory assay has not been performed, the next step is to obtain a second assay. (See 'Confirmatory assays' below.)

● Antibody only test – If the combination test is unavailable, obtain the antibody only test, which detects antibodies to HIV-1. Although laboratory-based antibody tests are available, most antibody-based tests are rapid or self-tests. Confirm positive rapid and self-testing results with a combination antigen/antibody test. If the positive test is a laboratory-based assay, procced to confirmatory assay. (See 'Confirmatory assays' below.)

CONFIRMATORY ASSAYS  — 

HIV strains are classified as HIV-1, responsible for most HIV infections outside of Western Africa, or HIV-2, primarily found in areas of Western Africa. The most commonly used confirmatory assay is the HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay. If the HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay is unavailable, some laboratories may still employ Western blot testing. However, most laboratories have moved away from using Western blot testing.

HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay

● Positive – A positive HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay confirms the results of the screening combination assay and provides information as to whether the patient is infected with HIV-1, HIV-2, or both:

• HIV-1 antibody positive, HIV-2 antibody negative – HIV-1 confirmed

• HIV-1 antibody negative, HIV-2 antibody positive – HIV-2 confirmed

• HIV-1 antibody positive, HIV-2 antibody positive – Both HIV-1 and HIV-2 confirmed

● Negative or indeterminate – A negative or indeterminate test may occur as a result of a recently acquired HIV infection ( table 1 ) or screening test. Obtain plasma HIV RNA testing (qualitative nucleic acid testing [NAT] or quantitative [viral load]).

Western blot IgM and IgG

● Positive HIV-1 Western blot – Suggests HIV infection. If HIV-2 infection is suspected (eg, the patient is from West Africa), further testing with a specific HIV-2 Western blot is recommended. If the HIV-2 Western blot is negative, the patient can be assumed to have HIV-1 monoinfection.

● Negative or indeterminate HIV-1 Western blot – Suggests acute infection or HIV-2 infection. Obtain plasma HIV RNA testing (qualitative or quantitative). Further testing with a specific HIV-2 Western blot test and HIV-2 DNA/RNA should be performed in persons from HIV-2 endemic areas (eg, West Africa).

(See "Acute and early HIV infection: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Diagnosis' and "Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of HIV-2 infection", section on 'Diagnostic issues related to HIV-1 Western blot testing' .)

SUBSEQUENT EVALUATION  — 

Patients with newly diagnosed HIV infection need additional evaluation and management:

● Provide counseling to all patients with suspected or confirmed HIV infection. (See "HIV infection: Risk factors and prevention strategies" .)

● Refer promptly to an appropriate specialist to review treatment options. (See "Acute and early HIV infection: Treatment", section on 'Rationale for initiation of ART in early infection' and "When to initiate antiretroviral therapy in persons with HIV" and "Selecting antiretroviral regimens for treatment-naïve persons with HIV-1: General approach" .)

● Obtain HIV-related testing (ie, plasma viral load, CD4 cell count, HIV genotypic drug resistance testing). (See "Initial evaluation of adults with HIV" .)

● Screen for other infections that may warrant treatment (eg, other sexually transmitted infections) or prophylaxis against reactivation (eg, latent tuberculosis). Screening should also be done for hepatitis A, B, and C. Those who are not immune or infected with hepatitis A and B should be vaccinated against these viruses. (See "Initial evaluation of adults with HIV", section on 'Screening for coinfections' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

HIV screening tests are reported as positive or negative. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
