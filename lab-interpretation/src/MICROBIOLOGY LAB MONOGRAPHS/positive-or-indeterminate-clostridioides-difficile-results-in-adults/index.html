ALGORITHM — 

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) can cause a spectrum of manifestations ranging from diarrhea to more severe colitis to fulminant disease with toxic megacolon (algorithm 1).

IMMEDIATE ACTION: FULMINANT COLITIS — 

Rapidly evaluate patients with life-threatening complications of CDI, including:

●Clinical manifestations of fulminant colitis – Diarrhea, abdominal pain, fever, hypovolemia, lactic acidosis, hypoalbuminemia, elevated creatinine, marked leukocytosis.

●Complications of fulminant colitis – Hypotension, sepsis, renal failure, toxic megacolon, bowel perforation with peritonitis.

●Acute ileus, with little or no diarrhea, usually in severely ill patients with colonic (and possibly small bowel) dilation. In this setting, a rectal swab is sent for CDI diagnostic testing.

Obtain computed tomography (CT) imaging of the abdomen and pelvis and surgical consultation as appropriate. Treat patients with positive or indeterminate results in the appropriate acute care setting. (See "Clostridioides difficile infection in adults: Treatment and prevention" and "Clostridioides difficile infection in adults: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Additional features of severe or fulminant infection'.)

PATIENTS WITHOUT FULMINANT COLITIS — 

The diagnosis of CDI is established in symptomatic patients via a positive stool test for C. difficile toxins or C. difficile gene. Place all patients with suspected or proven CDI on contact precautions. (See "Clostridioides difficile infection in adults: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'When to suspect C. difficile infection'.)

Confirm appropriate sample collection — Confirm appropriate sample collection in a patient with symptoms consistent with CDI. In the absence of fulminant colitis, CDI typically presents as acute diarrhea with ≥3 loose stools in 24 hours with no obvious alternative explanation, particularly in the setting of relevant risk factors (eg, current or recent antibiotic use, current or recent hospitalization, and advanced age).

Only liquid stool should be sent for testing. Asymptomatic individuals with formed stools do not warrant screening for C. difficile carriage, and individuals with asymptomatic carriage do not warrant treatment or contact precautions. (See "Clostridioides difficile infection: Prevention and control".)

There are multiple tests available for diagnosing CDI. The approach to laboratory testing is often determined by an institution's microbiology laboratory protocol (table 1). Specimens for testing based on toxin detection should be processed immediately or kept refrigerated if delay is anticipated. C. difficile toxin degrades at room temperature; false negative toxin results may occur if specimens are not processed appropriately.

Positive test result — A positive test result suggests CDI in a patient with consistent clinical findings (algorithm 1).

If initial testing with nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) alone is positive, some exerts do not perform further evaluation for CDI. When this approach is used, it is imperative to limit testing to people with high suspicion of CDI since a positive NAAT could also reflect asymptomatic carriage. Other experts favor subsequent enzyme immunoassay (EIA testing) for toxins A and B (table 1).

Reliance on NAAT testing exclusively has been associated with overdiagnosis and overtreatment (inability to distinguish CDI from asymptomatic carriage). However, false negative EIA results can occur when there is a true infection with low production of toxin that was not detected on the EIA. In these cases, clinical correlation is required to assess whether there are any possible alternative diagnoses that can explain the patient's symptoms. (See 'Indeterminate (discordant) test results' below and "Clostridioides difficile infection in adults: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Approach to diagnosis'.)

Indeterminate (discordant) test results — Discordant results occur when a positive NAAT results alongside a negative toxin EIA result (either with a NAAT followed by toxin EIA approach or the glutamate dehydrogenase [GDH] antigen and toxin EIA tests followed by NAAT approach) (table 1). (See "Clostridioides difficile infection in adults: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Interpreting discordant results'.)

●Review the individual test findings (table 1).

●Reassess clinically.

●Obtain NAAT if available and not previously performed if the EIA results are discordant (GDH positive, toxin negative; or GDH negative, toxin positive).

●Obtain consultation, if needed, with a clinician who has expertise in diagnosing and treating CDI.

AFTER TREATMENT — 

There is no role for repeat laboratory testing or testing for cure in patients following treatment for CDI; stool assays may remain positive during or after clinical recovery. Persistent or recurrent diarrhea following initial therapy should prompt an evaluation for recurrent CDI and for other causes. (See "Clostridioides difficile infection in adults: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Recurrent disease'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

C. difficile test results are reported as positive, indeterminate (discordant), or negative.
