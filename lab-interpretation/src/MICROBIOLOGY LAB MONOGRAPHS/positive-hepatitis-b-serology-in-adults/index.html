INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs), and total hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc) are ordered to confirm the diagnosis of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection ( algorithm 1 ) or to determine immunity (due to prior infection or to vaccination) ( algorithm 2 ). (See "Hepatitis B virus: Screening and diagnosis in adults" and "Hepatitis B virus: Clinical manifestations and natural history" .)

With few exceptions, HBsAg is positive in patients with acute or chronic HBV infection and negative in those without ( table 1 ).

HBsAg positive  —  HBsAg is the serologic hallmark of HBV infection ( figure 1 ). The combination of HBsAg positive, total anti-HBc positive, and anti-HBs negative is consistent with acute or chronic infection ( algorithm 1 ).

Take measures to prevent transmission of HBV, including:

● Patient education ( table 2 ). (See "Epidemiology, transmission, and prevention of hepatitis B virus infection" .)

● Hepatitis B vaccination of all nonimmune household and sexual contacts. (See "Hepatitis B virus immunization in adults", section on 'Postexposure management' .)

● Treatment and follow-up of personnel after occupational exposure to blood and body fluids. (See "Prevention of hepatitis B virus and hepatitis C virus infection among health care providers" .)

Check at least once for coinfection with other viruses. Obtain:

● Hepatitis C virus (HCV) antibody, hepatitis delta virus (HDV) antibody

● HIV antibody

● Hepatitis A virus (HAV) total antibody (for decision making around HAV vaccination)

Review tests usually available at the time of initial presentation for evidence of hepatitis or advanced liver disease:

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

● International normalized ratio (INR)

● Serum chemistries, including creatinine

● Liver biochemical tests, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)

Address any laboratory abnormality.

Normal liver biochemical tests  —  Normal liver biochemical tests suggest persistent HBV infection of the liver without significant, ongoing necroinflammatory disease. Depending on the duration of HBsAg positivity, virologic factors, and trajectory of liver biochemical tests, possibilities include ( table 3 and algorithm 1 ):

● Acute HBV infection – Liver tests may be normal early in the course or during recovery of HBV infection before seroconversion of HBsAg to anti-HBs.

● Chronic HBV infection – Liver tests may fluctuate (ie, occasionally normal, but most patients have a mild to moderate elevation in serum AST and ALT).

● Chronic inactive HBV infection.

To help determine the phase of acute or chronic hepatitis B infection, obtain (or review) ( algorithm 1 ) (see "Hepatitis B virus: Clinical manifestations and natural history" ):

● Quantitative HBV DNA levels

● Hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg), hepatitis B e antibody (anti-Hbe)

● Repeat liver biochemical tests

Elevated liver biochemical tests  —  Elevated liver biochemical tests suggest HBV infection of the liver with significant, ongoing necroinflammatory disease. Clinical manifestations and the magnitude of the liver biochemical test abnormalities do not establish the diagnosis of acute or chronic HBV infection. (See "Hepatitis B virus: Clinical manifestations and natural history", section on 'Acute hepatitis' and "Hepatitis B virus: Clinical manifestations and natural history", section on 'Chronic hepatitis' .)

Obtain serial testing:

● Liver biochemical tests

● HbeAg, anti-Hbe

● Quantitative HBV DNA

The frequency of monitoring depends on the severity of liver injury, the patient’s immunologic response to infection, and virologic factors.

The diagnosis of chronic HBV infection is based upon the persistence of HbsAg for more than six months ( table 3 ). Thus, repeat HbsAg should be performed in all patients six months after the initial positive test.

In patients with chronic HBV infection:

● Assess for alcohol, drug/toxin exposure, which can exacerbate chronic liver disease

● Educate patient on ways to prevent worsening of liver disease and to prevent transmission to others

● Obtain consultation with an expert in the management of infectious hepatitis

If there is uncertainty whether the patient has acute or chronic hepatitis, obtain a blood test for immunoglobulin M hepatitis B core antibody (IgM anti-HBc). The detection of IgM anti-HBc is usually an indication of acute HBV infection.

IgM anti-HBc positive  —  The detection of HbsAg and IgM anti-HBc suggests acute hepatitis. Other causes of positive HbsAg and IgM anti-HBc include severe cases of HBV reactivation (in a person with the inactive HbsAg carrier state or prior resolved HBV) and acute exacerbation of chronic HBV infection (characterized by intermittent elevations of ALT activity). (See "Hepatitis B virus reactivation associated with immunosuppressive therapy" .)

Obtain consultation with an expert in the management of infectious hepatitis to guide further evaluation and treatment decisions (eg, antiviral therapy), particularly for any patient with:

● Hepatic decompensation, including hepatic encephalopathy and INR >1.5 in the acute HBV setting

● Coinfection with HIV, HDV, and/or HCV

● Protracted course (eg, persistent symptoms or marked jaundice for more than four weeks after presentation)

Ensure appropriate monitoring until recovery (accompanied by the disappearance of HBV DNA, HBeAg to anti-HBe seroconversion, and, subsequently, HBsAg to anti-HBs seroconversion). (See "Hepatitis B virus: Clinical manifestations and natural history", section on 'Sequelae and prognosis of chronic HBV infection' and "Hepatitis B virus: Overview of management" .)

IgM anti-HBc negative  —  The detection of HBsAg with negative IgM anti-HBc suggests chronic HBV infection ( table 3 ).

Obtain repeat hepatitis serologies (HBsAg, total anti-HBc, anti-HBs), timing dictated by clinical assessment.

In patients who recover from acute HBV infection, total anti-HBc (immunoglobulin G [IgG]) replaces IgM anti-HBc, and anti-HBs replaces HBsAg. In those who progress to chronic HBV infection, total anti-HBc persists along with HBsAg, and anti-HBs remains negative.

HBsAg negative  —  HBsAg-negative patients usually do not have acute or chronic HBV infection ( algorithm 2 ). The presence or absence of anti-HBs in this setting determines immune status.

Anti-HBs positive  —  Anti-HBs positivity indicates immunity against HBV infection and noninfectivity. The presence or absence of anti-HBc serology distinguishes natural immunity from immunity due to vaccination ( table 1 ).

● Anti-HBs positive, anti-HBc negative – The combination of undetectable HBsAg, positive anti-HBs, and negative total anti-HBc suggests immunity due to HBV vaccination. (See "Hepatitis B virus immunization in adults", section on 'Postvaccination testing' .)

● Anti-HBs positive, anti-HBc positive – The combination of undetectable HBsAg, positive anti-HBs, and positive total anti-HBc suggests immunity from a past HBV infection. Immunosuppressed patients may require monitoring for HBV reactivation with serial ALT and viral DNA testing. (See "Hepatitis B virus reactivation associated with immunosuppressive therapy" .)

Anti-HBs negative  —  Anti-HBs negativity indicates susceptibility to HBV infection. However, a subset of patients may have occult HBV infection depending upon whether they are positive or negative for other HBV markers, most commonly anti-HBc and/or HBV DNA ( table 3 and algorithm 2 ).

● Anti-HBs negative, anti-HBc negative – The combination of undetectable HBsAg, negative anti-HBs, and negative total anti-HBc indicates susceptibility to HBV infection. Most adults should receive HBV vaccination ( table 4 ). (See "Hepatitis B virus immunization in adults" .)

● Anti-HBs negative, anti-HBc positive – The combination of undetectable HBsAg, negative anti-HBs, and positive total anti-HBc is uncommon in low hepatitis B prevalence areas but can occur in 10 to 20 percent of patients from endemic countries ( table 1 ). Patients with confirmed isolated anti-HBc require further evaluation and may be able to transmit HBV if they have occult HBV infection (ie, detectable HBV DNA). (See "Hepatitis B virus: Screening and diagnosis in adults", section on 'Isolated anti-HBc' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

Hepatitis B serology is reported as either positive or negative. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based on the laboratory reference range reported with that result.
