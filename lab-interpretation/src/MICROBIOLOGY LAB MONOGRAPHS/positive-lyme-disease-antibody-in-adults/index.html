(algorithm 1)

INITIAL EVALUATION

Serologic testing — Lyme disease is a spirochetal infection caused by Borrelia species (B. burgdorferi in North America). In addition to positive serology, the patient must have a compatible clinical syndrome (table 1) and reside in or have recently travelled to an endemic area. Serologic testing by itself cannot establish (or exclude) the diagnosis of Lyme disease. (See "Clinical manifestations of Lyme disease in adults" and "Diagnosis of Lyme disease", section on 'Serologic tests'.)

Limitations of serologic testing include the following:

●Serologic testing cannot distinguish between past or current infections. Antibodies (including either immunoglobulin G [IgG], immunoglobulin M [IgM], or both) can persist for years after Lyme disease has been treated and cured.

●In early disease, serologic testing is not specific for B. burgdorferi, particularly if only the IgM value is positive. False positive IgM testing can be seen in patients with other borrelial diseases (eg, relapsing fever, B. miyamotoi disease), other spirochetal diseases (eg, syphilis, leptospirosis, gingivitis), other bacterial infections, viral illness, and autoimmune diseases. In late disease, particularly Lyme arthritis, the IgG criteria have a high specificity for Lyme disease.

●Serologic testing is often negative in patients with early localized Lyme disease, since the characteristic erythema migrans (EM) lesion often appears prior to development of a diagnostic immune response (picture 1 and picture 2). IgM antibodies usually appear within one to two weeks, and IgG antibodies usually appear within two to six weeks following onset of EM. Initial testing can be obtained at the time of presentation, and, if negative, a repeat test can be performed two to three weeks later. In this setting, patients should be told to inform their provider if they develop clinical findings that become more suggestive of EM (eg, slowly expanding target-like rash); in that case, empiric treatment would be warranted. (See "Diagnosis of Lyme disease", section on 'Erythema migrans (single or multiple)'.)

Serologic testing for Lyme disease requires two tests. The initial serologic test is an enzyme-linked immunosorbent immunoassay (ELISA) or immunofluorescence assay (IFA). A positive initial test should be confirmed with either a Western blot (traditional two-tiered testing) or by a second ELISA that uses a different target (modified two-tiered testing).

Confirmatory Western blot — In the traditional two-tiered testing algorithm, a positive initial ELISA or IFA is followed by a Western blot IgG and IgM test.

●Western blot: IgG negative and IgM negative – A negative Western blot test supersedes the results of a positive ELISA or IFA, and testing should be considered negative unless a characteristic EM rash is present.

In patients without a compatible clinical syndrome, consider the possibility of a false-positive ELISA or IFA result due to cross-reacting antibodies from any of the following:

•Infections due to other Borrelia species (eg, B. miyamotoi), spirochetal diseases (eg, syphilis), Epstein-Barr virus, infective endocarditis, or malaria.

•Autoimmune disorders, especially systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis.

Additional testing in patients with suspected false-positive Lyme serology may include testing for other infections or to autoimmune disorders associated with cross-reacting antibodies.

●Western blot: IgG positive and IgM positive – A positive Western blot test is considered evidence of an encounter with B. burgdorferi. By the time the patient has findings of early disseminated disease, serologic tests are usually positive for both IgM and IgG antibodies to B. burgdorferi. The need for therapy depends upon prior symptoms and prior treatment history. (See "Treatment of Lyme disease".)

●Western blot: IgG positive and IgM negative – A positive IgG Western blot test is considered evidence of an encounter with B. burgdorferi at an indeterminate time point. The need for therapy depends on prior/current symptoms and prior treatment history. By the time the patient has findings of late Lyme disease (eg, arthritis), IgG Western blot is almost always positive, and IgM is often negative.

●Western blot: IgG negative and IgM positive – A positive IgM test may suggest early disseminated disease in patients with consistent clinical symptoms for less than six to eight weeks without treatment if epidemiologic risk factors are present. Those with suspected early disseminated Lyme disease who are initially seronegative for IgG should receive treatment and undergo repeat serologic testing approximately two weeks after the initial evaluation in an effort to confirm the diagnosis.

A positive IgM test alone in patients with symptoms of early infection for greater than six to eight weeks without treatment most likely represents a false-positive result or, possibly, evidence of past treated infection.

Confirmatory second ELISA — In the modified two-tiered system, a positive initial ELISA or IFA is followed by a second ELISA that uses a different target. Testing is consistent with Lyme disease if a patient has positive results on both tests. If the result of the second test is negative, Lyme disease is unlikely. The need for therapy is dependent on the previous history and current clinical signs and symptoms.

Testing for coinfection — Patients with Lyme disease in endemic areas may be coinfected with another tick-borne disease. Other organisms transmitted by Ixodes ticks include: Anaplasma phagocytophilum, Powassan encephalitis virus (also called deer tick virus), and Babesia microti in the United States. (See "Clinical manifestations of Lyme disease in adults", section on 'Coinfection with other tick-borne pathogens'.)

Investigation for coinfection is warranted in patients with fever for more than 24 hours if they are receiving antimicrobial therapy for Lyme disease that does not have activity against possible coinfecting pathogens (eg, amoxicillin and cefuroxime are not active against anaplasma, babesiosis is not treated with any of the preferred oral regimens for Lyme disease). It is also reasonable to test for coinfection in patients with risk factors for severe babesiosis (see "Babesiosis: Microbiology, epidemiology, and pathogenesis", section on 'Risk factors') and in those with unexplained leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia, and/or elevated indirect bilirubin.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

Serologic testing for Lyme disease requires two tests: ELISA (or IFA) followed by a Western blot IgG and IgM test or a modified approach (two enzyme immunoassays, which are reported together as either positive or negative). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
