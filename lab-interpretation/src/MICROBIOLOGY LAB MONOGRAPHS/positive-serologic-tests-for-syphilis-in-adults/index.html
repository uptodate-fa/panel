INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted infection (STI) caused by the spirochete Treponema pallidum . Serologic testing is the mainstay of diagnosis of syphilis because T. pallidum cannot be cultured using traditional in vitro laboratory methods.

There are two types of serologic tests used to diagnose syphilis:

● Nontreponemal lipoidal tests – Nontreponemal tests are semiquantitative tests, reported as reactive when positive, along with a titer. Titers generally reflect the activity of the infection, usually take two to four weeks to develop in response to a new infection, and tend to wane over time even without treatment. Successful therapy accelerates the pace of antibody decline, and nontreponemal tests revert to nonreactive usually by 12 months, although the decline may be slower (12 to 24 months) in some patients. Up to 30 percent of patients who are appropriately treated may continue to have reactive nontreponemal tests. Nontreponemal tests include:

• Rapid plasma reagin (RPR)

• Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL)

• Toluidine red unheated serum test (TRUST)

● Treponemal tests – In the United States, treponemal tests are qualitative tests, based upon the detection of antibodies directed against specific treponemal antigens, reported only as reactive when positive. Different tests target different antigens. Once positive due to syphilitic infection, they tend to remain positive for an extended period in most patients, regardless of treatment. Treponemal-specific tests include:

• Fluorescent treponemal antibody absorption (FTA-ABS)

• Treponema pallidum agglutination (TPPA)

• T. pallidum enzyme immunoassay

• Chemiluminescence immunoassay (CIA)

Both nontreponemal and treponemal tests are required for proper interpretation. Some laboratories reflexly perform the alternative test if the initial test is reactive or equivocal. On rare occasions, both nontreponemal and treponemal tests can be positive due to non-syphilis tropical treponemal infections (eg, yaws [ Treponema pallidum pertenue ], bejel [ Treponema pallidum endemicum ], and pinta [ Treponema pallidum carateum ]); either test may be falsely positive due to an infectious or noninfectious condition affecting immune function (see "Syphilis: Screening and diagnostic testing" and "Syphilis: Treatment and monitoring" ):

● Perform clinical assessment and additional testing – If the initial test was a nontreponemal test, obtain a treponemal test for confirmation. If the initial test was a treponemal test, obtain a nontreponemal test for management.

● Review (or obtain if not previously measured):

• Screen for other STIs (eg, gonorrhea, chlamydia, and, in female patients, trichomonas).

• Fourth-generation HIV screening and hepatitis B virus serology (if not vaccinated or unknown vaccination status).

● Take measures to prevent transmission of syphilis and concomitant STIs, including tracing and treating sexual contacts of patients with infectious syphilis.

● If patients have a prior history of syphilis, review records of prior testing and treatment received. Compare current nontreponemal titers (provided the same test was obtained) with prior titers to determine the need for treatment.

● Ensure that the local health department has been notified if infectious syphilis is identified or if prior syphilis history is unknown or records are unavailable.

Positive treponemal and nontreponemal serologies  —  The combination of an initial positive nontreponemal test and a confirmatory positive treponemal test supports a diagnosis of syphilis:

● No history of treated syphilis – For patients without a history of syphilis, these results are consistent with a new infection that must be treated ( table 1 ). An additional nontreponemal serologic test should be obtained just before starting therapy (ideally, on the first day of treatment) to establish the pretreatment titer.

● History of treated syphilis – For patients with a history of treated syphilis, the interpretation is sometimes less clear and may require guidance from a clinician with expertise in the management of syphilis. Serologies could be consistent with new infection, inadequately treated prior infection, or expected serologic findings post-treatment. The need for treatment depends upon the patient's clinical presentation and the nontreponemal titer. Nontreponemal titers should be compared with the patient's prior post-treatment titer. If possible, titers should be compared using the same test methodology. (See "Syphilis: Screening and diagnostic testing", section on 'Patients with a history of treated syphilis' .)

Positive treponemal/negative nontreponemal serology  —  The combination of an initial positive treponemal test and a subsequent negative nontreponemal test is most often due to a history of successfully treated syphilis but may be due to previously acquired, untreated syphilis (as nontreponemal antibodies can wane over time) or to early infection before antibodies develop (most treponemal antibodies tend to become reactive before nontreponemal antibodies):

● No history of treated syphilis – Obtain a second treponemal test that targets different antigens. If the second treponemal test is positive, treat for late latent syphilis/syphilis of unknown duration. If the second treponemal test is negative, syphilis is unlikely. (The original treponemal test may be reactive due to other spirochetal infections, gingivitis, or other conditions.)

● History of treated syphilis – No further evaluation of asymptomatic individuals is necessary unless concern for re-exposure (multiple sexual partners, illicit drug use).

Positive nontreponemal/negative treponemal serology  —  The combination of an initial positive nontreponemal test and a subsequent negative treponemal test could be a false-positive nontreponemal test due to conditions unrelated to syphilis or due to recent exposure to syphilis (rarely, nontreponemal antibodies may become reactive before treponemal antibodies). The level of titer does not reliably differentiate a false-positive result from a true-positive result in patients with very early syphilis (exposure within six to eight weeks) or non-syphilis tropical treponemal infections.

False-positive nontreponemal tests can be seen in the setting of older age, pregnancy, an acute event (eg, febrile illness, endocarditis, Rickettsial disease), or recent immunization. Test abnormalities attributed to these conditions are usually transitory and typically last for six months or less. Other etiologies include chronic conditions, such as autoimmune disorders (particularly systemic lupus erythematosus), intravenous drug use, chronic liver disease, and underlying HIV disease.

If there is no history of recent exposure, syphilis is unlikely. If there is recent exposure (in rare cases, nontreponemal antibodies may become reactive before treponemal antibodies), repeat treponemal test in two to four weeks.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

In the United States, treponemal tests are qualitative only and are reported as "reactive" or "nonreactive." However, in some places (eg, Europe), treponemal assays may be quantitative. Nontreponemal tests are semiquantitative, and positive results are reported as a titer of antibody (eg, 1:32, which represents the detection of antibody in serum diluted 32-fold). Laboratories should perform and report end-titer dilutions for nontreponemal antibodies. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based on the reference range reported by the laboratory.
