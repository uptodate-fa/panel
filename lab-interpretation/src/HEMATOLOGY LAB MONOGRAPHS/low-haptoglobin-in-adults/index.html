INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Serum haptoglobin is one of several tests obtained in patients with suspected hemolytic anemia. It should be interpreted in conjunction with the following tests:

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential, platelet count, corrected or absolute reticulocyte count

● Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), indirect bilirubin

● Peripheral blood smear

Patients with hemolytic anemia typically have:

● Decreased or absent haptoglobin levels. A low haptoglobin (<25 mg/dL [<2.5 micromol/L)] is likely due to hemolysis, and an undetectable haptoglobin level is almost always due to hemolysis.

● Anemia that is not due to another obvious cause.

● Increased reticulocyte count that is not explained by recent bleeding; repletion of iron, vitamin B12, folate; or administration of erythropoietin.

● Increased LDH and indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin.

The reticulocyte count may not be increased in patients with hemolysis if the bone marrow is unable to respond appropriately due to deficiency of iron, vitamin B12, or folate; inadequate erythropoietin due to chronic kidney disease; or an underlying bone marrow disorder.

Review clinical presentation and available tests to confirm the presence of hemolytic anemia and the suspected cause ( table 1 ).

Worrisome clinical findings present  —  Rapidly identify and triage acutely ill individuals with suspected conditions that require urgent involvement of a specialist, including those with:

● Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (AHTR) (see "Approach to the patient with a suspected acute transfusion reaction", section on 'Immediate actions (all patients)' )

● Likely thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) – Thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic changes (schistocytes) on the peripheral blood smear ( picture 1 ). As examples:

• Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) (see "Diagnostic approach to suspected TTP, HUS, or other thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)" )

• Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) (see "Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome in children" )

• Drug-induced TMA (see "Drug-induced thrombotic microangiopathy (DITMA)" )

● Intravascular hemolysis due to an underlying serious disorder:

• Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) (see "Evaluation and management of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in adults" )

• Hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets (HELLP) in a pregnant woman (see "HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets)", section on 'Diagnosis' )

• Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) (see "Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS)" )

● Severe anemia (eg, hemoglobin <7 g/dL [<70 g/L]) (see "Diagnosis of hemolytic anemia in adults", section on 'Immediate management before the cause is identified' )

• New-onset hemolysis with rapid decline in hemoglobin level from baseline

• Hemodynamic instability or end-organ ischemia

Alert blood bank personnel and obtain hematologic consultation to guide diagnostic evaluation and urgent therapy.

Obtain (or review):

● Type and crossmatch (or screen)

● Prothrombin time (PT)

● Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)

● Liver biochemical tests (including total and indirect bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST])

● LDH

● Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine

● Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test (DAT)

● Analysis of serum sample for hemoglobinemia, urine dipstick for hemoglobinuria, if suspected AHTR

Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

Worrisome clinical findings absent  —  Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to determine whether clinically significant hemolytic anemia is present and, if so, the cause ( table 1 ). (See "Diagnosis of hemolytic anemia in adults" .)

The clinical presentation, laboratory results, and peripheral blood smear findings guide further testing and timing of specialty involvement. As examples:

● Patients with lifelong anemia and classic findings on peripheral blood smear or positive family history of hemolytic anemia should be evaluated for a hereditary hemolytic anemia. Examples include:

• Hemoglobinopathy (eg, sickle cell disease, thalassemia). (See "Methods for hemoglobin analysis and hemoglobinopathy testing" .)

• Enzyme deficiency (eg, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency). (See "Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency" .)

• Membrane defect (eg, hereditary spherocytosis). (See "Hereditary spherocytosis" .)

● Patients with unexplained acquired hemolytic anemia should have a DAT (Coombs test) to differentiate immune-mediated from nonimmune mediated hemolysis.

● In the absence of other laboratory evidence of hemolysis, the patient may have low haptoglobin due to hepatic insufficiency, abdominal trauma, malnutrition, pregnancy, or protein loss from the kidney or gastrointestinal tract. However, haptoglobin is not routinely measured to diagnose these conditions.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for haptoglobin is approximately 83 to 267 mg/dL (8.3 to 26.7 micromol/L) but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
