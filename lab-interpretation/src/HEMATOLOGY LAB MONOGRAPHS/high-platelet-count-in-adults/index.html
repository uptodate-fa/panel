ALGORITHM — 

Thrombocytosis may be encountered in the course of evaluating other clinical findings or as an incidental abnormality on a complete blood count (CBC) and differential. The urgency of evaluation is guided by the patient's clinical condition, the presence of worrisome findings, and the degree of thrombocytosis. No specific platelet count per se constitutes an emergency, but we suggest evaluating a patient with a platelet count ≥1,000,000/microL within days (algorithm 1).

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

Rapidly identify patients with any degree of thrombocytosis and worrisome clinical findings, including:

●Clinical emergencies:

•Hemodynamic instability, respiratory compromise, acute thrombosis, bleeding

•Leukemic blasts, leukoerythroblastosis on peripheral blood smear

●Signs and symptoms that suggest a myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN):

•Vasomotor symptoms – Erythromelalgia, flushing, pruritus

•Constitutional symptoms – Unexplained fever, night sweats, weight loss

•Unusual thrombotic presentations (eg, unusual or multiple sites; <45 years old; unexplained, unprovoked, or recurrent thrombosis)

•Splenomegaly

Review prior CBCs, if available.

Obtain (review):

●Repeat CBC differential with platelet count

●Electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine

●Liver biochemical tests

●Peripheral blood smear

Obtain hematology consultation to evaluate and manage the platelet count in patients with suspected hematologic malignancy.

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Patients not requiring immediate action still require a timely clinical evaluation to determine the cause of the thrombocytosis and the risk to the patient. Initial evaluation should identify patients who are more likely to have an autonomous (ie, malignant or inherited) disorder rather than a reactive process (table 1).

Obtain (review):

●Prior CBCs, if available

●Repeat CBC with differential with platelet count to confirm the level of thrombocytosis

For many patients, the initial evaluation will define the cause. Reactive processes account for most cases of thrombocytosis in all age groups and clinical settings. Common causes of reactive thrombocytosis include (table 1):

●Anemia/blood loss – Iron deficiency, blood loss, hemolysis

●Infection – Viral, bacterial, mycobacterial, and fungal causes

●Noninfectious inflammation – Malignancy, rheumatologic conditions, trauma, reactions to medications

●Postsplenectomy – Postsplenectomy or functional asplenia (eg, sickle cell disease)

For these patients, no further testing or follow-up is required if one or two subsequent CBCs reveal that the platelet count has responded as expected in the setting of reactive thrombocytosis. The interval between confirmatory CBCs will vary with clinical setting. (See "Approach to the patient with thrombocytosis", section on 'Diagnosis established by initial evaluation'.)

If initial evaluation confirms but does not explain the cause of thrombocytosis, additional evaluation is necessary.

Assess the duration and trajectory of the thrombocytosis.

Obtain (review):

●Peripheral blood smear to assess platelet morphology, to detect other hematologic abnormalities, and to exclude spurious thrombocytosis

●Ferritin to assess for iron deficiency and reactive thrombocytosis (some experts also include serum iron and iron binding capacity)

Subsequent evaluation may identify the following:

●Ferritin <15 ng/mL – A low serum ferritin level is diagnostic of iron deficiency. Assess for the source of deficiency and replete iron. (See "Determining the cause of iron deficiency in adolescents and adults", section on 'Evaluation for the cause'.)

●Ferritin above normal – Elevated ferritin may reflect an inflammatory condition (ferritin is an acute phase reactant) or iron overload. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and/or C-reactive protein (CRP) may be useful in a patient suspected of having an inflammatory process. (See "Approach to the patient with thrombocytosis", section on 'Diagnosis established by initial evaluation'.)

●Abnormal peripheral blood smear – Detection of worrisome findings (eg, leukemic blasts, nucleated red blood cells, teardrop cells) on the blood smear requires prompt evaluation by a hematologist. (See "Approach to the patient with thrombocytosis", section on 'Abnormal blood smear'.)

In addition, obtain hematologic consultation if the cause of the high platelet count is not determined by the initial evaluation, there is a suspected hematologic malignancy, there is a family history of unexplained thrombocytosis, or if the platelet count does not respond as expected with treatment of a reactive cause. (See "Approach to the patient with thrombocytosis", section on 'Referral to hematology'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal reference range for the platelet count is approximately 150,000 to 450,000/microL but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
