REASON FOR TEST — 

Antiglobulin testing (also known as Coombs testing) includes the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and indirect antiglobulin test (IAT).

●DAT – Detects antibodies attached to the surface of red blood cells (RBCs)

●IAT – Detects antibodies in the patient's plasma or serum.

Antiglobulin testing is typically performed in the following settings:

●Anemia – In patients with hemolysis or hemolytic anemia, a direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test is performed to determine whether an immune mechanism is responsible. (See 'Immediate action' below and 'Stable patients' below.)

The evaluation for hemolysis (usually available at the time of reporting of Coombs test results) may demonstrate the following findings:

•Increased reticulocyte count that is not explained by

-Recent bleeding

-Repletion of iron, vitamin B12, or folate

-Administration of erythropoietin

•High indirect bilirubin, high lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

•Low or undetectable haptoglobin

Spherocytes (picture 1) on the peripheral blood smear are consistent with immune hemolysis but may not always be appreciated.

●Pretransfusion – In most patients, indirect antiglobulin (indirect Coombs) testing, also called an antibody screen, is performed on the patient sample prior to transfusion of blood products to determine whether alloantibodies are present. (See 'Pretransfusion screening' below.)

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

Patients with a positive direct and/or indirect antiglobulin (Coombs) test who have an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (AHTR) or severe autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) require immediate evaluation and management.

Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction — Acute hemolytic transfusion reactions (AHTRs) occur early during transfusion, often after as little as a few mL of blood have been transfused. Clinical manifestations include fever, chills, flank pain, hemoglobinuria, and oozing from intravenous (IV) sites. (See "Approach to the patient with a suspected acute transfusion reaction", section on 'Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (AHTR)' and "Hemolytic transfusion reactions", section on 'Acute hemolytic transfusion reactions'.)

Rapidly stabilize patients with suspected AHTR:

●Stop the transfusion immediately

●Retain remaining blood product for blood bank testing

●Communicate with the transfusion medicine service or blood bank

●Provide aggressive hydration and diuresis

Obtain:

●Complete blood count (CBC)

●ABO compatibility testing

●Repeat crossmatching

●Urine dipstick for hemoglobinuria

●Prothrombin time (PT), activated thromboplastin time (aPTT)

Severe autoimmune hemolytic anemia — Rapidly stabilize patients with severe AIHA, including those with any of the following:

●Hemoglobin level significantly decreased below the patient's baseline or very low (eg, <7 g/dL [<1.09 mmol/L])

●Symptomatic anemia (eg, lethargy, chest pain, dyspnea with tachycardia)

●Findings of end-organ ischemia (eg, electrocardiogram changes)

Alert the transfusion medicine service or blood bank personnel and obtain hematologic consultation to guide diagnostic evaluation and urgent therapy. (See "Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) in adults", section on 'Initial management' and "Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) in adults", section on 'Stabilization and transfusion for severe anemia'.)

STABLE PATIENTS — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to determine the cause of immune hemolysis (eg, delayed transfusion reaction, autoimmune hemolytic anemia [AIHA], drug-related hemolysis).

Delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction — Delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions (DHTRs) typically occur days to weeks after transfusion of blood products. They are most commonly due to an immune response to a foreign red blood cell (RBC) antigen to which the recipient was previously exposed. (See "Hemolytic transfusion reactions", section on 'Delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions and delayed serologic transfusion reactions'.)

The transfusion medicine service or blood bank will perform antiglobulin (Coombs) testing (both direct and indirect) to identify antibodies in patient plasma/serum against RBC antigens that are capable of causing immune-mediated hemolysis.

Preventing future transfusion reactions depends on identifying the specific RBC antigen to which the patient has become sensitized.

Autoimmune hemolytic anemia — In patients who have not been transfused within the previous three months, a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test (DAT) may indicate AIHA. AIHA is classified into warm and cold antibody types (table 1). Most laboratories that perform antiglobulin (Coombs) testing measure antibodies to immunoglobulin G (IgG) and complement component C3. Together with the clinical picture, the pattern of the antibody reactivity may help to suggest the type of AIHA (table 2).

Obtain hematologic consultation for diagnosis and management.

Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia — Warm AIHA refers to immunologic destruction of RBCs mediated by IgG autoantibodies reacting against antigens on the RBC surface at body temperature.

Diagnosis requires:

●Anemia, usually with spherocytes in the peripheral smear (picture 1)

●Hemolysis – Elevated levels of indirect bilirubin and LDH, reduced levels of haptoglobin

●Positive DAT – Positive anti-IgG and/or anti-C3

Reticulocytosis is usually present, unless there is a reason for lack of bone marrow response such as concomitant iron deficiency. Hemolysis without anemia can occur if reticulocytosis is sufficient to compensate for hemolysis. (See "Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) in adults", section on 'Evaluation and diagnosis'.)

Warm AIHA can be primary (with no underlying disorder) or secondary to another condition such as systemic lupus erythematosus, immunodeficiency, or chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL).

Cold agglutinin disease — Cold agglutinin disease is a type of AIHA triggered by cold exposure. (See "Cold agglutinin disease", section on 'Cold agglutinins'.)

Diagnosis is suspected in patients with one or more of the following:

●Livido reticularis (picture 2), acrocyanosis

●Hemolysis precipitated by cold and/or paradoxically by febrile illnesses, trauma, and surgery

●Positive DAT – Positive anti-C3, usually negative for anti-IgG

●Peripheral blood smear demonstrating RBC agglutination (picture 3)

Diagnosis is confirmed in patients with:

●Cold agglutinin titer ≥64 at 4°C

Drug-related immune hemolysis — Many drugs may be associated with immune hemolytic anemia and a positive Coombs test (indirect and/or direct) (table 3). (See "Drug-induced hemolytic anemia".)

PRETRANSFUSION SCREENING — 

Indirect antiglobulin (Coombs) testing is typically done by the transfusion medicine service or blood bank to determine whether the patient's plasma/serum contains antibodies that could react with transfused red blood cells (RBCs). (See "Pretransfusion testing for red blood cell transfusion", section on 'Antibody screen'.)

A positive antibody screen may be reported to a clinician. In this setting, blood bank personnel perform additional testing to ensure that RBCs in the selected unit do not express the implicated antigen(s). (See "Pretransfusion testing for red blood cell transfusion", section on 'IgG crossmatch (full crossmatch)'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The antiglobulin (Coombs) test assesses agglutination when an antibody on the patient's red blood cells (RBCs) or in patient plasma/serum binds to a RBC antigen (figure 1). When no agglutination is present, the test is negative (normal). The strength of a positive test (ie, agglutination present during testing) is graded 1 to 4+.
