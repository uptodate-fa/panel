ALGORITHM — 

The laboratory may report the reticulocyte count as an absolute number or as a percentage of red blood cells (RBCs). The absolute reticulocyte count has the advantage of accurately reflecting the degree of reticulocytosis regardless of the degree of anemia, whereas in the setting of severe anemia, the reticulocyte percentage should be corrected for the hemoglobin (HGB) or hematocrit (HCT) (algorithm 1).

IMMEDIATE EVALUATION — 

Some patients with especially severe anemia or other worrisome findings may require immediate life-saving interventions before the etiology of high reticulocytes has been identified. (See "Diagnosis of hemolytic anemia in adults", section on 'Immediate management before the cause is identified'.)

Rapidly identify and triage critically ill patients and those who may require urgent involvement by a specialist, including individuals with any of the following:

●Hemodynamic instability, active bleeding, brisk hemolysis.

●Suspected acute hemolytic transfusion reaction.

●Likely thrombotic microangiography (eg, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura [TTP]).

●Intravascular hemolysis due to a serious disorder (eg, acute disseminated intravascular coagulation [DIC]).

●Anemia (eg, HGB <7 g/dL [70 g/L]) that cannot be raised with transfusion and/or symptomatic anemia associated with end-organ ischemia.

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

High reticulocytes imply an accelerated production of RBCs in the bone marrow. Anemia with high reticulocytes reflects an increased erythropoietic response to continued hemolysis or blood loss. Reticulocytosis may also occur in the early phase of recovery after repletion of deficient iron or vitamin B12, after erythropoietin administration, or during recovery from a bone marrow insult.

Determine the reason for ordering a reticulocyte count, whether clinically significant bleeding (which may be occult) and/or a hemolysis is present, and, if so, the cause.

Review complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count (usually ordered at the same time as the reticulocyte count). Obtain prior reports of blood work, including CBC and peripheral blood smear, if available. (See "Low hemoglobin, hematocrit in adults".)

Suspected or known hereditary hemolytic anemia — Pursue specific testing based on clinical assessment and review of available laboratory tests:

●In patients with suspected hereditary hemolytic anemia (clear history of life-long anemia, anemia in multiple family members, and/or classic findings on peripheral smear), obtain hematology consultation to guide specific testing and management.

●In patients with a known hereditary hemolytic anemia (eg, sickle cell disease, thalassemia), compare reticulocyte count with baseline reticulocyte count. If either up or down, obtain hematology consultation to address any change. If unchanged, continue comprehensive health care maintenance (or refer back to primary clinician/treatment team to continue health care maintenance).

Cause of high reticulocytes apparent — The cause of the reticulocytosis may be apparent from the clinical evaluation and review of available laboratory tests:

●Significant blood loss – Identify and manage source of bleeding (eg disorders of gastrointestinal tract, lungs, kidney, or uterus; recovery from surgery or trauma).

●Treatment of nutrient deficiency (eg, vitamin B12, folate, iron, or copper).

●Erythropoietin administration.

●Recovery from bone marrow insult (eg, infection [parvovirus], medication, or alcohol).

●Delayed transfusion reaction from transfusion of blood products within prior four weeks. (See "Hemolytic transfusion reactions", section on 'Delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions and delayed serologic transfusion reactions'.)

●Other conditions known to cause hemolysis:

•Splenomegaly due to severe liver disease, infectious, or neoplastic process.

•Mechanical hemolysis due to heart valve.

•Infections (eg, Bartonella, parasites, Babesia, malaria).

•New medications (eg, drug-induced oxidative hemolysis, drug-related immune or nonautoimmune hemolysis). (See "Drug-induced hemolytic anemia".)

Cause of high reticulocytes inapparent — If the cause is not apparent from the clinical evaluation, evaluate for hemolysis and obtain hematology consultation. (See "Diagnosis of hemolytic anemia in adults", section on 'Post-diagnostic testing to determine the cause'.)

Review tests usually available at the time of reticulocyte reporting:

●CBC with differential and platelet count

●Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

●Indirect bilirubin

●Haptoglobin

●Peripheral blood smear report (and, when possible, clinician review of any abnormal automated report)

Hemolytic anemia is established on the basis of serum markers of hemolysis (increased indirect bilirubin and LDH, decreased haptoglobin), reticulocytosis, and abnormalities on the peripheral smear. However, not all of these findings need to be present. Once the diagnosis of hemolytic anemia is relatively certain, the clinical history, laboratory results, and peripheral blood smear findings guide further testing to determine the cause (table 1 and table 2).

Direct antiglobulin test (DAT, Coombs) is usually performed along with a hemolysis evaluation to identify an immune mechanism.

Positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test — If the DAT is positive, evaluate for immune hemolytic anemia (eg, delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction or autoimmune hemolytic anemia) (table 2 and table 3). (Related Lab Interpretation Monograph(s): "Positive Coombs test, direct and indirect in adults".)

Negative direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test — If the DAT is negative, nonimmune hemolysis is likely. (See "Non-immune (Coombs-negative) hemolytic anemias in adults".)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

Reticulocytes can be expressed as an absolute number or as a percentage of RBCs. If anemia is present, the reticulocyte percentage (but not the absolute count) should be corrected for the degree of anemia or, if severe, the lifespan of reticulocytes in the circulation.

●Absolute reticulocyte count – The normal absolute reticulocyte count is between 25,000 to 75,000/microL (ie, approximately 1 percent of an absolute RBC count of 5,000,000 cells/microL).

●Reticulocyte percentage is the percentage of RBCs that are reticulocytes. The normal reticulocyte percentage in a healthy patient with normal HGB and HCT ranges from 0.5 to 2 percent.

●Corrected reticulocyte percentage is the reticulocyte percentage multiplied by the patient's HCT divided by a normal HCT.

Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
