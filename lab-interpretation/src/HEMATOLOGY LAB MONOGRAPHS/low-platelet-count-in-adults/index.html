ALGORITHM — 

(algorithm 1)

Degrees of thrombocytopenia may be classified by the following platelet thresholds:

●Mild: 100,000 to 150,000/microL

●Moderate: 50,000 to 99,000/microL

●Severe: <50,000/microL

●Very severe: <20,000/microL

Severe thrombocytopenia confers a greater risk of bleeding, particularly in the presence of other risk factors (eg, anticoagulation, antiplatelet agents). Very severe thrombocytopenia confers a greater risk of spontaneous bleeding in most conditions and increases the likelihood for needing urgent treatment. However, the correlation between the platelet count and the risk of bleeding varies according to the underlying condition and may be unpredictable.

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

Rapidly identify patients with thrombocytopenia and any of the following clinical findings that might indicate the need for urgent treatment, including:

●Bleeding and any of the following:

•Severe thrombocytopenia (ie, platelet count <50,000/microL)

•Use of antiplatelet agents or anticoagulants

•Underlying coagulopathy (eg, uremia, liver failure, malabsorption of fat soluble vitamins)

●Need for an urgent/emergency procedure if:

•Platelet count <100,000/microL prior to high-risk procedure (eg, neurosurgery, orthopedic or cardiac surgery)

•Platelet count <50,000/microL prior to lower-risk procedure

●Venous, arterial, or small-vessel thrombosis of any organ, which might indicate:

•Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)

•Vaccine-induced immune thrombotic thrombocytopenia (VITT)

•Thrombotic microangiopathy (eg, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura [TTP], drug-induced thrombotic microangiopathy [DITMA])

•Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

•Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS)

●Life-threatening pregnancy syndrome, which might indicate:

•HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelet count) syndrome

•Preeclampsia with severe features; eclampsia

●Worrisome abnormalities of other cell lines, including:

•Schistocytes, which might indicate microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (picture 1)

•Immature white blood cells or blasts, which might indicate acute leukemia (picture 2)

•Pancytopenia, which might indicate a bone marrow failure syndrome (table 1)

Obtain:

●Blood type and crossmatch (if bleeding or need for urgent surgery)

●Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

●Peripheral blood smear

●Prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), plasma fibrinogen concentration

●Routine chemistries (electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen [BUN], creatinine, glucose)

●Liver biochemical tests (including aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], total and indirect bilirubin)

Urgent interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing. Urgent hematologic consultation is appropriate to diagnose and manage thrombocytopenic emergencies and to interpret the peripheral blood smear.

Take steps to:

●Avoid medications that interfere with platelet function, such as aspirin

●Address contributing factors, such as coagulation abnormalities, sepsis, malignancy

●Coordinate with anesthesiologists, surgeons, or obstetricians before invasive procedures

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to confirm thrombocytopenia and to assess other hematologic abnormalities. The potential causes of thrombocytopenia and need for hematologic consultation differ depending on the clinical setting (table 2). (See "Diagnostic approach to thrombocytopenia in adults", section on 'Definitions and areas of concern'.)

Hospitalized or acutely ill patient — Obtain:

●Repeat CBC with differential and platelet count

●Peripheral blood smear

●PT, aPTT

●Routine chemistries

●Liver biochemical tests

●Hepatitis C virus (HCV) serology

●HIV screening

Review medical history for any of the following:

●Medications started within the last two weeks or a medication taken occasionally and within the previous day, which may indicate drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (table 3).

●Massive transfusion – Significant thrombocytopenia can be seen after 10 to 20 units of blood, with platelet counts <50,000/microL.

●Recent fluid resuscitation for critical illness, which can cause dilutional thrombocytopenia.

●Underlying disorder that explains thrombocytopenia (eg, infection, malignancy).

Additional laboratory testing and consultation should be based on the suspected diagnoses (eg, infection, hemolysis, DIC, HIT) (table 2).

Outpatient setting or seemingly well individual — Before undertaking an extensive evaluation in a seemingly well individual, repeat the platelet count to exclude transient thrombocytopenia and pseudothrombocytopenia (a falsely low platelet count due to clumping) (table 2). A pregnant individual with isolated, incidentally discovered thrombocytopenia that is mild is most likely to have gestational thrombocytopenia. (See "Diagnostic approach to thrombocytopenia in adults", section on 'Repeat CBC' and "Thrombocytopenia in pregnancy".)

Obtain:

●Repeat CBC with differential, platelet count

●Peripheral blood smear examination

In a seemingly well individual, no further evaluation is required if thrombocytopenia is transient or due to a laboratory artifact.

If persistent thrombocytopenia, obtain:

●Liver biochemical studies

●HIV screening

●HCV serology

Individuals with asymptomatic, isolated, incidentally discovered thrombocytopenia (platelet count 100,000 to 150,000/microL) are most likely to have immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and, less commonly, occult liver disease or myelodysplasia. Medications may also be associated with isolated thrombocytopenia (table 3). (See "Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia".)

Additional laboratory testing should be based on likely diagnoses (table 2) and whether other cytopenias are present. In patients with suspected nutrient deficiencies (picture 3), measure vitamin B12 and in selected individuals folate levels (see "Low vitamin B12 and low folate in adults"). A hematologic consultation may be necessary to confirm a new thrombocytopenic disorder (eg, ITP) or to determine another cause.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal adult platelet count reference range is 150,000 to 450,000/microL but can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
