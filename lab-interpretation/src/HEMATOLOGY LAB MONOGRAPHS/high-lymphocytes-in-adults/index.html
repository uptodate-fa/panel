ALGORITHM — 

Lymphocytosis (high lymphocyte count) may be encountered in the course of evaluating other clinical findings or as an incidental abnormality on a complete blood count (CBC) and differential. Lymphocytosis may be the sole abnormality in the CBC and blood smear, or it may be accompanied by other white blood cell (WBC) abnormalities (eg, neutropenia, neutrophilia) or abnormalities in other cell lines (eg, red blood cells and platelets) (algorithm 1).

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

The urgency of the evaluation is guided by the patient's clinical condition, the degree and rate of rise of lymphocytosis (if known), and worrisome findings (eg, blasts) on peripheral blood smear. (See "Approach to the adult with lymphocytosis or lymphocytopenia", section on 'Urgency of evaluation' and "Overview of the treatment of chronic lymphocytic leukemia", section on 'Indications for treatment ("active disease")'.)

Identify and address findings that require urgent attention. As examples:

●Hemodynamic instability, respiratory compromise, or complications of infection.

●Unexplained absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) >30,000 cells/microL.

●Disease-related complications of a known underlying hematologic malignancy (eg, acute lymphocytic leukemia [ALL], chronic lymphocytic leukemia [CLL]).

●Lymphocytosis with eosinophilia and systemic signs and symptoms (eg, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms [DRESS]).

Provide initial therapies and monitoring.

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

In patients not requiring immediate action, the tempo of the initial evaluation and need for specialty consultation depend on the clinical context (see "Approach to the adult with lymphocytosis or lymphocytopenia", section on 'Causes of lymphocytosis'):

●Assess the general health of the individual, including the duration and severity of lymphocytosis, evidence of recent viral infection, offending drugs, or asplenia

●Review prior CBC with differential and platelet count (if available) to assess duration of lymphocytosis, severity, trajectory (improving, worsening, or stable), and whether abnormalities of other cell types or cell lines are present

●Repeat CBC with differential and platelet count to exclude error. If the patient is clinically stable, the lymphocytosis is modest, and no worrisome findings are reported on blood smear, repeat within two to four weeks.

Transient lymphocytosis — Lymphocytosis that resolves within three months is most likely caused by a reactive process (eg, infection). It requires no further evaluation unless lymphocytosis recurs or worrisome clinical findings emerge.

Persistent lymphocytosis — Persistent lymphocytosis warrants further evaluation in patients without a known diagnosis. Persistent lymphocytosis may be due to reactive causes (table 1) and/or malignancy. The presence of anemia, thrombocytopenia, or neutropenia in conjunction with lymphocytosis is suggestive of a malignant process.

If the cause is unknown, obtain (review) initial testing that may help identify associated medical conditions or complications, including:

●Repeat CBC with differential and platelet count in one to three months, as guided by other clinical findings or concerns

●Peripheral blood smear examination

●Chemistries (glucose, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, electrolytes)

●Liver biochemical tests (including alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase)

●Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP)

●Peripheral blood flow cytometry to assess for clonality and lymphocyte phenotype

Asymptomatic patients with stable lymphocytosis on repeat testing and otherwise normal peripheral blood smear may be followed subsequently with serial CBCs (every three months). In general, reactive lymphocytosis can be expected to persist while the underlying inflammatory/infectious process is active. However, if lymphocytosis (ALC ≥5000/microL) persists for ≥3 months, obtain hematology consultation to evaluate for a clonal process. CLL may present as an isolated, incidentally discovered lymphocytosis. (See "Approach to the adult with lymphocytosis or lymphocytopenia", section on 'Assessment of clonality'.)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

Lymphocytosis is best assessed by the absolute number of lymphocytes in peripheral blood (rather than the percentage of lymphocytes in the WBC differential count). A normal absolute lymphocyte count for adults is usually between 1000 and 4000 lymphocytes/microL. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
