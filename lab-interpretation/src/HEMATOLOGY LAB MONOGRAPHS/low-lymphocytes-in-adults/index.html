ALGORITHM — 

Lymphocytopenia (typically an absolute lymphocyte count [ALC] below 1000 cells/microL [<1.0 x 109 cells/L]) may be encountered in the course of evaluating other clinical findings or as an incidental abnormality on a complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count. Lymphocytopenia may be the sole abnormality in the CBC and blood smear, or it may be accompanied by other white blood cell (WBC) abnormalities (eg, neutropenia) or abnormalities in other cell lines (eg, red blood cells and platelets) (algorithm 1).

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

The urgency of the evaluation is guided by acuity of illness, whether a diagnosis has previously been established (table 1), and whether there are other abnormalities in the CBC and peripheral blood smear.

Identify and address findings that require urgent attention. As examples:

●Hemodynamic instability, respiratory compromise, or complications of infection

●Diminished bone marrow function, such as severe cytopenia, pancytopenia, or an unexplained absolute neutrophil count (ANC) <500 cells/microL (<0.5 x 109 cells/L)

●Primary bone marrow disorder (eg, acute leukemia, aplastic anemia)

●Autoimmune disorder associated with autoimmune destruction of multiple cell lines

If there is an associated low neutrophil count, the degree of neutropenia influences the urgency of the evaluation and need for hematologic and/or infectious disease consultation. (See "Low neutrophils in adults", section on 'Immediate action'.)

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

In patients not requiring immediate action, the tempo of the initial evaluation and need for specialty consultation depend on the clinical context (see "Approach to the adult with lymphocytosis or lymphocytopenia", section on 'Lymphocytopenia'):

●Assess general health of the individual, including evidence of recent or active infection, immunosuppressive or cytotoxic medications (eg, glucocorticoids, antilymphocyte monoclonal antibodies, chemotherapy), exposure to toxins (eg, alcohol), and history of radiotherapy. Lymphocytopenia may be an incidental finding, especially in older adults.

●Review prior CBC with differential and platelet count to determine the duration, trajectory, and severity of lymphocytopenia, and whether abnormalities of other cell types or cell lines are present.

●Repeat CBC with differential and platelet count to confirm lymphocytopenia (in a seemingly well individual with mild lymphocytopenia, in approximately one month, before initiating a more extensive evaluation).

Transient lymphocytopenia — Lymphocytopenia that resolves within three months is most likely due to transient bone marrow suppression (eg, from a bacterial or viral infection, recent surgery, or other stress). No further evaluation is required unless lymphocytopenia recurs or worrisome clinical findings emerge.

Persistent lymphocytopenia — Lymphocytopenia may be related to a variety of conditions, which may be already known at the time of clinical presentation, including:

●Systemic autoimmune disorders, particularly during active disease (eg, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis)

●Chronic disease (eg, chronic kidney disease, sarcoidosis, chronic infection [eg, tuberculosis, HIV], malnutrition)

●Hematologic disorders (eg, myelodysplastic syndromes, lymphoproliferative disorders)

●Primary or secondary immunodeficiencies

If the patient has a condition associated with lymphocytopenia, manage the underlying condition (table 1).

In patients without a known diagnosis, persistent lymphocytopenia warrants further evaluation. Re-examine the patient for signs of an underlying disorder and to exclude active infection (eg, HIV).

Obtain:

●Serial CBC with differential and platelet count (repeat in six months, if clinically well)

●Peripheral blood smear examination

●Chemistries (glucose, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, electrolytes)

●Liver biochemical tests (including alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase)

●Serum protein electrophoresis and immunoglobulin levels

●HIV fourth-generation screening in selected individuals (eg, those with chronic, severe, or unexplained lymphopenia) (see "Screening and diagnostic testing for HIV infection in adults", section on 'HIV antibody tests')

●Hematology consultation if the patient is symptomatic (weight loss, fever, drenching night sweats), or if there are concerning signs (eg, unexplained lymphadenopathy and/or splenomegaly) or other hematologic abnormalities

●Immunology consultation if suspected immunodeficiency

REFERENCE RANGE — 

Lymphocytopenia is best assessed by the absolute number of lymphocytes in peripheral blood (rather than the percentage of lymphocytes in the WBC differential count). A normal ALC for adults is usually between 1000 and 4000 lymphocytes/microL. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the reference range reported by the laboratory.
