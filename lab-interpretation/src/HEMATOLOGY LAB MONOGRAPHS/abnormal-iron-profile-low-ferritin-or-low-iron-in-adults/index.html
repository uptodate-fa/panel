INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Ferritin may be ordered as part of an "iron studies panel" or as an isolated test to evaluate for iron deficiency. In contrast to an isolated low ferritin, low serum iron by itself does not distinguish iron deficiency from anemia of chronic disease.

Review complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count. Characteristic findings of iron deficiency anemia include ( table 1 ) (see "Diagnosis of iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia in adults", section on 'Findings on CBC' ):

● Low hemoglobin (Hgb; ≤12 g/dL [120 g/L] in nonpregnant females, Hgb ≤14 g/dL [140 g/L] in males) low hematocrit (HCT), and low red blood cell (RBC) count

● Microcytosis (low mean corpuscular volume [eg, <80 fL]) and hypochromia (decreased Hgb content within the RBC [eg, low mean corpuscular hemoglobin])

The platelet count may be increased.

Criteria for iron deficiency  —  Ferritin is an acute phase reactant that can increase independently of iron status in disorders associated with inflammation, infection, liver disease, heart failure, or malignancy. Thus, a normal (or even elevated) ferritin level may be "falsely normal" and cannot be used to eliminate the possibility of iron deficiency in individuals with comorbidities. If ferritin is thought to be unreliable due to an inflammatory state, and there is suspicion for iron deficiency, review (or obtain if unavailable) a fasting iron profile: iron, transferrin (total iron binding capacity [TIBC]), transferrin saturation (serum iron ÷  TIBC  x  100), and serum ferritin. Oral iron supplements can transiently increase the serum iron concentration. Therefore, testing should be obtained at least four hours after taking an oral iron supplement:

● In individuals with anemia (and without a disorder associated with inflammation), a ferritin <30 ng/mL (<30 mcg/L) is sufficient to diagnose iron deficiency.

● In individuals with anemia (and with comorbidities [eg, inflammatory state]), iron deficiency (absolute or functional) is likely if the transferrin saturation (TSAT) is ≤19 percent .

If the ferritin is above 30 ng/mL and the TSAT is above 20 percent but the suspicion for iron deficiency remains, other options for laboratory testing and/or a trial of iron replacement may be appropriate. (See "Diagnosis of iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia in adults", section on 'Iron studies (list of available tests)' .)

Referral to a hematologist is not indicated in the majority of patients with straightforward iron deficiency. However, referral is appropriate for those in whom iron studies are inconclusive, the diagnosis is unclear, or the administration of intravenous iron is under consideration.

Identify etiology of iron deficiency  —  The diagnosis of iron deficiency should prompt further evaluation to identify the cause of iron deficiency and a strategy to treat the deficiency, if clinically indicated, as well as management of the underlying cause of the deficiency. In the United States, the etiology is more likely to be due to blood loss (eg, menstruation or pregnancy in women, gastrointestinal in men or women). In resource-limited settings, parasitic infestations are a significant contributor. (See "Treatment of iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia in adults" and "Determining the cause of iron deficiency in adolescents and adults", section on 'Evaluation for the cause' .)

Referral to a gastroenterologist is appropriate in individuals for whom an occult source of gastrointestinal blood loss or malabsorption is suspected.

Evaluate for blood loss  —  The history and physical examination often identifies a source of bleeding. When the cause of blood loss is not apparent, men and postmenopausal women should be evaluated for occult bleeding. Occult bleeding typically involves the gastrointestinal tract (eg, gastritis, malignancy, telangiectasis) but may also include urinary losses if gross hematuria (eg, malignancy or hemolysis). Negative testing for occult blood does not eliminate the possibility of a gastrointestinal source, since bleeding may be intermittent. Anticoagulation or thrombocytopenia do not diminish the importance of searching for the site(s) of bleeding. (See "Evaluation of occult gastrointestinal bleeding" .)

In premenopausal women, iron deficiency may relate to menses ( table 2 ), pregnancy, and/or delivery. An empiric trial of oral iron supplementation may be considered if heavy menstrual blood loss seems likely. In postmenopausal women with iron deficiency, uterine bleeding requires an evaluation for uterine cancer.

Evaluate for reduced iron absorption  —  Iron deficiency occurs following gastric bypass surgery, in patients with diseases associated with generalized malabsorption or achlorhydria, and with the chronic use of drugs to treat gastritis or gastric reflux (as these drugs interfere with drug absorption). Further evaluation is required in patients with unexplained iron deficiency and in those who have not responded to iron repletion, including testing for Helicobacter pylori , autoimmune gastritis (screened for with antiparietal cell antibodies), or celiac disease. (See "Determining the cause of iron deficiency in adolescents and adults", section on 'Abnormal absorptive surfaces' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal ferritin concentration ranges from 30 to 200 ng/mL (30 to 200 mcg/L). Normal serum iron ranges from 11 to 27 micromol/L (60 to 150 mcg/dL). These ranges can vary depending on the patient population and clinical laboratory. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
