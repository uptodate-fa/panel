INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) is a useful screening test to identify an excess of proteins found in the gammaglobulin region (hypergammaglobulinemia) and to differentiate monoclonal from polyclonal gammopathies. SPEP must be interpreted in conjunction with serum immunofixation to confirm the clonality (monoclonal/biclonal, polyclonal) and to determine the immunoglobulin heavy and light chain class ( table 1 ).

Review:

● Serum immunofixation

● Complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count

● Serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, calcium, total protein

● Liver biochemical tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase

● Urinalysis with examination of sediment

Monoclonal gammopathy  —  Monoclonal proteins (M-protein) are usually present as a single narrow peak, like a church spire, in the gamma, beta, or alpha-2 region of the densitometer tracing or as a dense, discrete band on agarose gel ( figure 1 ). A monoclonal protein can be present in a number of different disorders (monoclonal gammopathies), including B cell and plasma cell proliferations ( table 2 ), and it may occur in up to 8 percent of healthy older adults.

If a monoclonal gammopathy has been identified, obtain:

● Serum free light chain (FLC) assay to detect Bence Jones protein that is not bound to a heavy chain, often in concentrations too low to be detected by routine serum immunofixation techniques.

● Twenty-four-hour urine protein electrophoresis (UPEP) with immunofixation to detect monoclonal free kappa or lambda chains (Bence Jones protein).

Disease definitions for the monoclonal gammopathies (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance [MGUS] and related disorders) differentiate premalignancy from malignant plasma cell disorders based on specific criteria, including ( table 3 ):

● Size of the M-protein.

● Evidence of end-organ damage that can be attributed to the plasma cell proliferative disorder:

• Normochromic normocytic anemia.

• Renal insufficiency.

• Hypercalcemia.

• One or more osteolytic lesions on bone imaging in patients with immunoglobulin G (IgG), immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin D (IgD), or immunoglobulin E (IgE) isotypes.

• Constitutional symptoms, hyperviscosity, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly (immunoglobulin M [IgM] isotype).

● Bone marrow findings or tissue biopsy for definitive diagnosis.

(See "Multiple myeloma: Clinical features, laboratory manifestations, and diagnosis" and "Epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis of Waldenström macroglobulinemia" and "Clinical presentation, laboratory manifestations, and diagnosis of immunoglobulin light chain (AL) amyloidosis" and "Diagnosis of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance" .)

M-protein <3 g/dL  —  When an M-protein is <3 g/dL, MGUS is likely. However, the size of the M-protein by itself does not exclude multiple myeloma. Further evaluation depends on risk of progression to malignancy.

● Assess risk – Patients with MGUS have a low risk of progression to malignancy when all of the following are present:

• Serum IgG M-protein <1.5 g/dL with normal FLC ratio (0.26 to 1.65) or serum IgM M-protein <1.5 g/dL

• No unexplained clinical concerns

• No evidence of end-organ damage

● Low-risk MGUS – In patients with low-risk MGUS, bone marrow biopsy and imaging for lytic bone lesions is not required ( table 3 ). In six months, obtain:

• CBC with differential and platelet count

• SPEP, serum FLC

• Serum creatinine, calcium

● All other patients – In all other patients, obtain:

• Hematologic consultation

• Bone marrow examination to distinguish MGUS (<10 percent bone marrow clonal cells) from more advanced plasma cell dyscrasias (≥10 percent bone marrow clonal cells)

• Bone imaging (whole-body, low-dose computed tomography [CT] without contrast) in all patients except those with IgM MGUS with no clinical concern for bone lesions or myeloma

M-protein ≥3 g/dL  —  By definition, an M-protein ≥3 g/dL is not MGUS. If the M-protein is non-IgM, multiple myeloma or smoldering multiple myeloma are more likely. If the M-protein is IgM, Waldenström macroglobulinemia, smoldering Waldenström macroglobulinemia, or other lymphoid disorders are more likely.

Obtain:

● Hematology consultation

● Bone marrow examination

● Serum albumin, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), beta-2-microglobulin, C-reactive protein (CRP)

● Bone imaging (whole-body, low-dose CT without contrast) in patients with non-IgM M-protein

Check serum viscosity in patients with signs and symptoms suggesting the hyperviscosity syndrome and in all patients, regardless of symptoms, with a monoclonal IgM protein spike >4 g/dL or an IgA or IgG protein spike >6 g/dL. (See "Multiple myeloma: Clinical features, laboratory manifestations, and diagnosis", section on 'Diagnosis' and "Multiple myeloma: Clinical features, laboratory manifestations, and diagnosis", section on 'Imaging' and "Epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and diagnosis of Waldenström macroglobulinemia", section on 'Hyperviscosity syndrome' .)

Biclonal gammopathy  —  In approximately 5 percent of sera with an M-protein, two M-proteins are present (biclonal gammopathy). Patients with biclonal gammopathy (two different monoclonal proteins) of undetermined significance have the same clinical spectrum as those with MGUS and should be evaluated in the same manner. (See 'Initial evaluation' above and "Diagnosis of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance", section on 'Biclonal gammopathy' .)

Polyclonal gammopathy  —  A polyclonal increase in immunoglobulins is usually present as a broad-based peak or band, usually of gamma mobility ( figure 2 ). Polyclonal gammopathies are most often due to infectious, inflammatory, or reactive processes.

The cause of a polyclonal increase in immunoglobulins is usually apparent based on history and initial laboratory tests (eg, liver disease, connective tissue disease, chronic infection, hematologic disorders and nonhematologic malignancy).

If unexplained, obtain additional tests:

● Hepatitis C antibody

● HIV screening

● Antinuclear, antimitochondrial, and antismooth muscle antibodies (if suspected autoimmune hepatitis)

● Blood, urine, and fluid cultures (if acutely ill)

(See "Laboratory methods for analyzing monoclonal proteins", section on 'Polyclonal gammopathy' and "Extrahepatic manifestations of hepatitis C virus infection", section on 'Autoimmune disorders' .)

Hypogammaglobulinemia  —  Hypogammaglobulinemia (<0.6 g/dL) is characterized by a decrease in size of the gamma mobility component on SPEP. (See "Laboratory methods for analyzing monoclonal proteins", section on 'Hypogammaglobulinemia' .)

Review medications for immunosuppressants (eg, glucocorticoids, rituximab, antiepileptics). Other acquired causes include multiple myeloma, AL (primary) amyloidosis, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, lymphoma, or the nephrotic syndrome.

Obtain:

● Quantitation of serum IgG, IgA, and IgM levels

● Twenty-four-hour UPEP and immunofixation

Further evaluation for secondary causes is based on a comprehensive history and physical examination.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

SPEP uses electrophoresis to separate serum proteins into five regions (albumin, alpha-1, alpha-2, beta, and gamma) ( figure 3 ). The immunoglobulin classes (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, and IgE) are usually located in the gamma region but may also be found in the beta-gamma and beta regions and, occasionally, extend into the alpha-2 globulin region. The laboratory should be contacted concerning interpretation of these tests.
