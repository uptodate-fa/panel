INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

CD4 T cell laboratory testing through flow cytometry reports the following results:

● CD4 absolute count (calculated by multiplying the CD4 percentage, the lymphocyte percentage, and the total white blood cell [WBC] count)

● CD4 percentage (the ratio of CD4 cell count/total lymphocyte count)

● CD4:CD8 ratio (reported by some laboratories but not used in clinical decision making)

As an alternative to flow cytometry, the "CD4 Select" method utilizes an automatic hematoanalyzer to assess both CD4 absolute count and percentage. It correlates well with standard flow cytometry. (See "Techniques and interpretation of measurement of the CD4 cell count in people with HIV", section on 'CD4 T cell testing in resource-limited settings' .)

In general, CD4 counts are not used to guide initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) since ART should be offered to all HIV-infected patients, including asymptomatic individuals, regardless of their CD4 count. Initiation of ART is more urgent the lower the CD4 count. (See "Selecting antiretroviral regimens for treatment-naïve persons with HIV-1: General approach" .)

The absolute CD4 count and CD4 percentage are used along with clinical assessment to define AIDS (CD4 cell count <200 cells/microL or the presence of any AIDS-defining condition) and advanced HIV infection (CD4 count <50 cells/microL). Serial CD4 counts provide information about the need for initiation and continued prophylaxis to prevent opportunistic infections, especially in patients with CD4 cell counts <200 cells/microL. (See "Overview of prevention of opportunistic infections in patients with HIV", section on 'When to administer antimicrobial therapy' .)

All patients with low CD4 counts should be managed in conjunction with a clinician who has expertise in HIV. Consultation with an infectious disease specialist, in particular, should be considered in the following settings (see "Primary care of adults with HIV" ):

● Initial HIV diagnosis or initiation of an ART regimen

● Declining CD4 counts or CD4 count <200 cells/microL

● Signs and/or symptoms of opportunistic infection

● Coinfection (eg, viral hepatitis, tuberculosis)

Review prior CD4 counts  —  Review prior CD4 counts to determine if there is a significant clinical change. If a prior CD4 count is unavailable, repeat the CD4 count using the same laboratory and method. A 30 percent change in the absolute count or an increase or decrease in CD4 percentage by 3 percentage points is considered to represent a significant change (eg, two standard deviations) between two tests performed within the previous three to six months (in the same assay and laboratory).

In general, the absolute CD4 count and CD4 percentage are concordant. When the absolute CD4 count is discordant with the CD4 percentage, the clinician needs to assess for factors that may alter total WBC count (eg, infection, medications). Significant changes in the total WBC count will have an effect on the CD4 cell count but not on the CD4 percentage. CD4 count should be repeated after resolution of ongoing infection and/or change in medication.

Confirmed decline in CD4 count  —  Perform a clinical evaluation in patients with a confirmed decline in CD4 count to determine potential reasons for change.

If there are barriers to taking ART, a decline in CD4 count increases the urgency with which providers should repeatedly encourage patients to start therapy. In patients who may be noncompliant with ART, address barriers. (See "When to initiate antiretroviral therapy in persons with HIV" .)

If availability of ART is limited and CD4 count is declining, treatment is prioritized to those with more advanced HIV disease (clinical stage 3 or 4 disease ( table 1 )) or a CD4 cell count of <200 cells/microL. (See "Use and impact of antiretroviral therapy for HIV infection in resource-limited settings" .)

For patients with declining CD4 counts, assess the need for measures to reduce the risk of opportunistic infection. Most opportunistic infections occur below a defined CD4 count threshold. In asymptomatic patients, the timing of when to initiate screening and/or prophylactic antibiotics to prevent symptomatic disease depends on the CD4 count as well as other factors, including risk factors, prior infection, residence in an endemic area, and duration of ART therapy. (See "Overview of prevention of opportunistic infections in patients with HIV", section on 'When to administer antimicrobial therapy' .)

Confirmed increase in or stable CD4 count  —  An adequate immune response to ART is defined as an increase in CD4 count in the range of 50 to 150 cells/microL per year. ART should be continued regardless of change in CD4 count. Measures to reduce the risk of opportunistic infections should be reassessed to determine the need for continued antibiotic prophylaxis. If the CD4 cell count is >500 cells/microL, CD4 cell count monitoring is optional. (See "Patient monitoring during HIV antiretroviral therapy", section on 'CD4 count' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal CD4 cell count for most laboratories falls in a range of 800 to 1050 cells/microL. Reference values vary depending on the age, sex, and race, as well as the laboratory instruments used to perform the tests. Sequential samples that are processed using different methods or by different laboratories should be interpreted with caution. Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based on the reference range reported with that result.
