Causes of high ferritin*
Condition Ferritin levels
Intense inflammatory response; ferritin release from necrotic cells:
  • Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)
  • Sepsis, systemic infections
  • Fulminant liver failure
  • Hematologic malignancies
  • Still's disease
 Typically very high (eg, >3000 ng/mL)
Iron overload states:
  • Hereditary hemochromatosis
  • Transfusional iron overload
  • Ineffective erythropoiesis, even in the absence of transfusion
    • Thalassemia major
    • Thalassemia intermedia
 Thresholds for iron overload are:
  • Males: >200 to 300 ng/mL
  • Females: >150 to 200 ng/mL
Levels increase gradually over time and are frequently 1000 to 4000 ng/mL at the time of diagnosis
Chronic liver disease:
  • Chronic hepatitis
  • Alcohol-associated liver disease
  • Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)
 Typically mildly increased (eg, 300 to 1000 ng/mL) unless concomitant iron overload
Other acute and chronic conditions:
  • Anemia of chronic disease (ACD)
    • End-stage kidney disease
    • Infection
    • Inflammatory bowel disease
    • Malignancy
    • Rheumatologic disorders
  • Endocrine disorders
    • Hyperthyroidism
    • Metabolic syndrome (abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, insulin resistance)
 Typically mildly increased (eg, 300 to 1000 ng/mL)
Reference range: Normal serum ferritin levels range from 40 to 200 ng/mL. Ranges for normal values vary, depending on gender, patient populations, and test assays used. Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the laboratory-specific normal value reported with that result.
* Elevated serum ferritin levels: ≥300 ng/mL in males or ≥200 ng/mL in females. The degree of elevation correlates with the severity and progression of the underlying disease, and abnormal ferritin levels must be interpreted within the context of the clinical findings.
