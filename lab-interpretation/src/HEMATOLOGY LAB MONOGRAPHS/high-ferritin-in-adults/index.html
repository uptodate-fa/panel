ALGORITHM  — 

Ferritin is the main iron-storage protein in the body and also an acute phase reactant. Serum ferritin is usually measured to evaluate anemia and/or iron overload. Patients may have anemia coexisting with iron overload (eg, thalassemia, history of multiple transfusions):

● Anemia – To distinguish between iron deficiency anemia (low ferritin levels) and anemia of chronic disease (high ferritin levels) ( algorithm 1 ) (see 'Evaluation of anemia' below)

● Iron overload – To screen for iron overload disorders in high-risk patients (eg, hereditary hemochromatosis [HH] or secondary iron overload) ( algorithm 2 ) (see 'Evaluation of suspected iron overload' below)

FERRITIN ≥3000 ng/mL, immediate action  — 

The finding of extremely high ferritin levels (eg, ≥3000 ng/mL [3000 mcg/L, 6741 picomoles/L]) should prompt consideration of uncommon, life-threatening disorders associated with an intense inflammatory response:

● Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), an acute illness with fever, cytopenias, neurologic findings, and abnormal coagulation studies (see "Clinical features and diagnosis of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis" )

● Sepsis and/or opportunistic infection in an immunocompromised host (eg, human immunodeficiency virus [HIV] infection, especially low CD4 count) (see "Acute and early HIV infection: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis" )

Ferritin levels >10,000 ng/mL (22,470 picomoles/L) may also be associated with fulminant liver failure, adult-onset Still's disease, or hematologic malignancies. (See "Clinical features and diagnosis of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis", section on 'Differential diagnosis' and "Approach to the patient with suspected iron overload", section on 'Other causes of high ferritin' and "Adult-onset Still's disease: Clinical manifestations and diagnosis", section on 'Serum ferritin' .)

Review tests usually available at the time of initial evaluation:

● Complete blood count (CBC), differential, and platelet count

● Prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)

● Liver biochemical tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST)

● Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, and urinalysis with microscopic examination

Evaluate for underlying infection:

● HIV serology

● Blood, urine cultures, other cultures as indicated

Additional testing is directed at suspected underlying cause. Life-saving interventions should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing.

FERRITIN <3000 ng/mL  — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to determine the cause of the elevated ferritin ( table 1 ).

Since ferritin is an acute phase reactant, it should be repeated in patients with or recovering from acute illness.

A persistently elevated ferritin should be interpreted in conjunction with the CBC and other iron studies (serum iron; total iron binding capacity [TIBC]; and transferrin saturation [TSAT], the ratio of serum iron to the TIBC, expressed as a percentage).

Evaluation of anemia  —  Anemia with increased ferritin may be due to anemia of chronic disease or thalassemia (with or without iron overload).

Anemia is usually classified according to red blood cell (RBC) size (the mean corpuscular volume [MCV]) and the corrected reticulocyte count ( algorithm 1 ).

MCV <80 fL  —  Ferritin is usually measured in conjunction with other iron studies, to distinguish between the two most common causes of microcytic anemia:

● Iron deficiency – Low ferritin (see "Diagnosis of iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia in adults" )

● Thalassemia – Increased ferritin and TSAT, typically proportional to the degree of excess iron stores (see "Diagnosis of thalassemia (adults and children)", section on 'Iron overload' )

Of note, thalassemia major or intermedia may be associated with substantial iron overload. In such patients, TSAT is ≥45 percent. If thalassemia is suspected based on the results of the personal and family history, findings on the CBC and blood smear, and iron studies, additional evaluation is needed. This is reviewed separately. (See "Diagnosis of thalassemia (adults and children)", section on 'Diagnostic evaluation' .)

Anemia of chronic disease (increased ferritin) is occasionally associated with a mild microcytosis, but it is most often associated with a normocytic anemia. (See 'MCV 80 to 100 fL' below.)

MCV 80 to 100 fL  —  Anemia of chronic disease is most often associated with a normocytic anemia (MCV 80 to 100 fL). The degree of ferritin elevation typically does not exceed 1000 ng/mL (mcg/L) and is associated with low serum iron, normal to low TIBC, normal to low TSAT, and inappropriately low reticulocyte count. (See "Anemia of chronic disease/anemia of inflammation", section on 'Diagnostic evaluation' .)

Common causes of anemia of chronic disease include acute and chronic infection, chronic kidney disease, inflammatory disorders, or malignancy. Myelodysplastic syndromes are associated with cytopenias and/or abnormal cell morphologies.

Obtain:

● Absolute or corrected reticulocyte count

● BUN, creatinine

● Liver biochemical tests, including ALT, AST

● C-reactive protein (CRP)

● Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)

MCV >100 fL  —  A high ferritin with a macrocytic anemia (MCV >100 fL) may be due to chronic liver disease, alcohol-associated liver disease, and myelodysplastic syndromes. A high ferritin (due to ineffective erythropoiesis) is also seen in megaloblastic anemias such as anemia due to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency; however, the underlying condition is usually treated before significant iron accumulation can occur.

Review the peripheral blood smear, especially if cytopenias are present, if the laboratory reports marked anisocytosis, or if the red cell distribution width (RDW) is high.

Obtain:

● Absolute or corrected reticulocyte count

● Liver biochemical tests, including ALT, AST

The subsequent evaluation depends on the clinical suspicion based on history, physical examination, and preliminary laboratory test results.

Evaluation of suspected iron overload  —  Potential causes of iron overload include:

● Beta thalassemia intermedia (even without transfusion) and other anemia associated with ineffective erythropoiesis

● Multiple RBC transfusions (eg, >20 units)

● HH

● Chronic liver disease (eg, alcohol-associated liver disease, chronic hepatitis, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease)

Typical laboratory findings in iron overload include:

● Increased ferritin level – ≥200 to 300 ng/mL (mcg/L) in a male or ≥150 to 200 ng/mL (mcg/L) in a female

● Increased TSAT (≥45 percent)

The TSAT is used to confirm or exclude iron overload in HH but may not be reliable in transfusional iron overload. A normal CBC with increased ferritin and TSAT suggests hemochromatosis (eg, HH). However, patients with hemochromatosis may have coexisting anemia (eg, anemia of chronic disease) due to other underlying medical conditions. (See "Approach to the patient with suspected iron overload", section on 'Causes of iron overload' .)

Repeat the ferritin with iron studies in one to two months ( algorithm 2 ).

For patients with persistently elevated ferritin and/or TSAT, obtain if not recently measured:

● CBC with RBC indices

● Liver biochemical studies (ALT, AST)

Additional testing depends on the TSAT.

Transferrin saturation <45 percent  —  Except for rare genetic conditions, TSAT <45 percent is good evidence that the patient does not have iron overload. Family history and patient evaluation guide further testing.

● All patients with a family history of HH should have HFE mutation testing.

If the etiology of the high ferritin is unexplained, evaluate for other causes ( table 1 ). Common causes of a high ferritin despite normal total body iron stores (TSAT <45 percent) include (see "Approach to the patient with suspected iron overload", section on 'Other causes of high ferritin' ):

● Alcohol-associated liver disease

● Metabolic syndrome/obesity/diabetes, with or without nonalcoholic fatty liver disease

● Systemic inflammatory conditions or infectious diseases (eg, HIV infection)

● Malignancy

Transferrin saturation ≥45 percent  —  Findings of a very high ferritin and TSAT ≥45 percent is sufficient to diagnose iron overload in patients with a known HFE mutation or history of multiple transfusions. However, high ferritin and/or TSAT ≥45 percent may occur in the absence of iron overload in some patients (eg, alcohol-associated liver disease).

Obtain, if unknown:

● HFE mutation testing for HH (see "Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of hereditary hemochromatosis" )

In patients with TSAT ≥45 percent and ferritin above the reference range, the severity of iron overload can be determined by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the liver and other organs (the intended purpose of the study should be indicated in the referral) and, in some cases, tissue biopsy and/or response to a course of therapeutic phlebotomy. In the absence of systemic inflammatory or infectious diseases, higher ferritin levels indicate greater likelihood of more severe hepatic iron overload, but cardiac iron does not correlate well with ferritin levels.

Perform:

● Liver MRI to confirm iron overload and estimate its severity (or liver biopsy in selected patients with unexplained liver biochemical abnormalities to quantify hepatic stores, assess fibrosis, and diagnose underlying liver disease)

● Cardiac MRI in selected individuals with significant transfusional iron overload, severe iron overload, heart failure, or cardiac conduction abnormalities from HH

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The normal reference range for serum ferritin is approximately 40 to 200 ng/mL (40 to 200 mcg/L, 89.9 to 449.4 picomoles/L) but can vary depending on the clinical laboratory and patient population (eg, in females, serum ferritin thresholds for iron overload are ≥150 to 200 ng/mL [337 to 449.4 picomoles/L]; in males, serum ferritin thresholds for iron overload are ≥200 to 300 ng/mL [449.4 to 674.1 picomoles/L]). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the reference range reported with that result.
