IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

Patients who are acutely ill or who have an extremely high eosinophil count (eg, ≥50,000 eosinophils/microL [≥50 x 10 9 /L]) require hospitalization and urgent evaluation for the cause ( table 1 ). Obtain specialty consultation for guidance about diagnostic evaluation and/or therapy. In particular, urgent eosinophil-lowering therapy should be considered for eosinophil-mediated myocarditis, dysrhythmias, hypoxia, or neurologic compromise. For acutely ill patients, therapy should not be delayed during laboratory testing. (See "Approach to the patient with unexplained eosinophilia", section on 'Acutely ill or extremely high AEC' and "Hypereosinophilic syndromes: Treatment" .)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Eosinophilia refers to an absolute eosinophil count (AEC) ≥500 eosinophils/microL (≥0.5 x 10 9 /L) in the peripheral blood. There are many possible causes of eosinophilia ( table 1 ), but, importantly, the level of eosinophilia is not sufficient to establish the diagnosis nor to predict the likelihood of organ damage. The diagnosis is best distinguished by the patient's history, clinical presentation, and specific laboratory testing. (See "Approach to the patient with unexplained eosinophilia" .)

Review:

● History and clinical presentation, including assessment for potential organ involvement (eg, rash, wheezing, diarrhea, cardiovascular symptoms).

● Medications ( table 2 ).

● Travel history.

● Dietary history (ingestion of raw or undercooked foods as potential source of parasitic infection).

● Prior complete blood count (CBC) with differential and platelet count, if available.

Signs of organ involvement present and/or AEC ≥1500/microL  —  Levels of eosinophilia do not strictly correlate with the potential for eosinophil-mediated tissue damage, although organ damage is more likely with AEC ≥1500/microL (≥1.5 x 10 9 /L). Patients with new or changing signs or symptoms of organ dysfunction (eg, rash, cardiac/respiratory abnormalities, gastrointestinal symptoms, or lymphadenopathy) and/or AEC ≥1500/microL require further evaluation.

● All patients should have a repeat CBC with differential and platelet count; review of the peripheral blood smear; and electrolytes, liver, and kidney function tests.

Further testing should be individualized based on the clinical presentation and may include:

● Cardiac troponin for AEC ≥1500/microL, cardiac symptoms, or suggestive findings on physical examination.

● Vitamin B12 and tryptase for abnormal blood smear, anemia or thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly, or other findings suggestive of a malignant disorder.

● Imaging (eg, chest radiograph, computed tomography [CT]) for respiratory symptoms.

● Infectious disease evaluation (or empiric treatment) for a history of travel or residence that suggests possible exposure to parasites and infections.

Evidence of organ dysfunction (eg, renal insufficiency, hepatic involvement, subclinical myocarditis [elevated troponin], abnormalities on chest radiography) should prompt urgent evaluation and may require prompt initiation of therapy and specialty consultation. (See "Hypereosinophilic syndromes: Clinical manifestations, pathophysiology, and diagnosis" .)

Asthma-associated eosinophilia is usually mild to moderate (eg, <1500/microL), so higher levels should prompt consideration of other eosinophilic pulmonary conditions (eg, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis [ABPA], eosinophilic granulomatosis and polyangiitis [EGPA]). (See "Overview of pulmonary eosinophilia" .)

When eosinophilia is due to drug hypersensitivity ( table 2 ), cessation of the inciting drug is necessary if there is evidence of organ dysfunction. Substitute acceptable alternatives for essential medications. Obtain CBC and differential every one to two weeks with periodic re-evaluation of the patient. Eosinophilia may require months to resolve following drug discontinuation.

Signs of organ involvement absent and AEC <1500/microL

● No travel history – Asymptomatic individuals with eosinophils ≥500/microL (≥0.5 x 10 9 /L) and no clinical findings suggestive of organ damage or concerning travel/residency history may be observed with a repeat CBC with differential and platelet count within one to two months. (See "Approach to the patient with unexplained eosinophilia", section on 'Asymptomatic or incidental eosinophilia' .)

● Travel/geographic history – Obtain testing for parasites, informed by the history of travel and/or residency. Parasitic helminths ( Strongyloides , trichinellosis, hookworm) are the most commonly identified infectious cause of eosinophilia. Parasitic infections may be asymptomatic. (See "Infectious causes of peripheral eosinophilia", section on 'Initial evaluation' .)

● Medication suspected – If medication is suspected as the cause of eosinophilia for patients with AEC ≥500 to <1500/microL with no evidence of organ involvement from the initial evaluation, discontinue nonessential medications and repeat the CBC with re-evaluation in one to two months. (See "Approach to the patient with unexplained eosinophilia", section on 'Suspect medication' .)

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

Typically, eosinophils represent <5 percent of the differential white blood cell count, but the AEC is needed to determine if eosinophilia is present. A normal AEC is <500 eosinophils/microL (<0.5 x 10 9 /L). This may be reported directly in some laboratories but otherwise will require calculation from the total white blood cell count and percentage of eosinophils ( calculator 1 ).
