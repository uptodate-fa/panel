INITIAL EVALUATION — 

An abnormal white blood cell (WBC) count may be encountered in the course of evaluating other clinical findings or as an incidental abnormality on a complete blood count (CBC) and differential. The urgency of evaluation is guided by the patient's clinical condition, severity of abnormality, and whether other cell lines (red blood cells [RBCs], platelets) are involved. Some patients may require evaluation and management in an acute care setting (see "Approach to the adult with unexplained neutropenia", section on 'Urgency of evaluation' and "Approach to the patient with neutrophilia", section on 'Urgency of evaluation' and "Evaluation of the peripheral blood smear", section on 'Schistocytes' and "Evaluation of the peripheral blood smear", section on 'Blasts or tumor cells'):

●Review current and prior CBC with differential and platelet count.

●Determine the predominant abnormality, the duration and trajectory, and whether other cell lines (RBCs, platelets) are involved.

The WBC differential reports percentages of different WBC subtypes (ie, percentages of neutrophils, lymphocytes, eosinophils, monocytes, and basophils). For clinical application, if not reported by the laboratory, percentages should be converted to absolute numbers by multiplying the percentage of WBC subtype by the total WBC count:

●Absolute neutrophil count (ANC) is the number of neutrophils plus bands but does not include metamyelocytes and less mature forms (calculator 1).

●Absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) is the number of lymphocytes in peripheral blood (ALC [cells/microL] = WBC [cells/microL] x percent lymphocytes ÷ 100).

●Absolute eosinophil count (AEC) is the number of eosinophils in peripheral blood (calculator 2).

●Absolute monocyte count (AMC) is the number of monocytes in peripheral blood.

The evaluation depends upon whether the total WBC count is low (leukopenia) or high (leukocytosis).

Leukopenia — Leukopenia refers to a reduced total WBC count (<4400 cells/microL in most clinical laboratories). The degree of leukopenia and the affected WBC type(s) influence the urgency of evaluation and the need for hematologic and/or infectious disease consultation. Leukopenia is almost always related to a decrease in neutrophils (see "Approach to the adult with unexplained neutropenia"). Leukopenia may be due to a primary bone marrow disorder, bone marrow suppression or replacement, hypersplenism, nutritional deficiency (eg, cobalamin, folate, copper), or due to an autoimmune disorder:

●Neutropenia – Neutropenia is usually defined as an ANC <1500 cells/microL (<1.5 x 109 cells/L). However, the World Health Organization defines neutropenia as an ANC ≤1800 cells/microL (≤1.8 x 109 cells/L). An unexplained ANC <500 cells/microL (<0.5 x 109 cells/L) is a medical emergency. (See "Low neutrophils in adults".)

●Lymphocytopenia – Lymphocytopenia is usually defined as an ALC <1000 cells/microL for adults. (See "Low lymphocytes in adults".)

Leukocytosis — Leukocytosis refers to an elevated WBC count (>11,000 cells/microL in most clinical laboratories). Neutrophilia is the most common type of leukocytosis (see "Approach to the patient with neutrophilia"). Leukocytosis may also be due to increased monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, and/or lymphocytes. Acute leukocytosis (hours to days) is more likely due to infection, stress reactions, surgery, or acute leukemias. Chronic leukocytosis (lasting weeks to months) is more likely due to chronic inflammation, smoking, and some malignancies:

●Neutrophilia – Neutrophilia refers to an increase of peripheral blood neutrophils at least two standard deviations above the mean, typically an ANC >7700 neutrophils/microL (7.7 x 109/L). (See "High neutrophils in adults".)

●Lymphocytosis – Lymphocytosis refers to an ALC >4000 lymphocytes/microL (4.0 x 109/L). (See "High lymphocytes in adults".)

●Eosinophilia – Eosinophilia refers to an AEC ≥500 eosinophils/microL (0.5 x 109/L) in the peripheral blood (calculator 2). (See "High eosinophils in adults".)

●Monocytosis – Monocytosis refers to an AMC >800 monocytes/microL (0.8 x 109/L). It may accompany neutrophilia in various conditions, including infections (eg, brucellosis, varicella-zoster) and malignancies (eg, chronic myelomonocytic leukemia, chronic myeloid leukemia). (See "High neutrophils in adults".)

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The normal range (ie, two standard deviations above and below the mean) for the WBC count in adults is 4400 to 11,000 cells/microL (4.4 to 11 x 109 cells/L) in most clinical laboratories. The WBC differential should contain a spectrum of mature leukocytes, including neutrophils (usually 40 to 70 percent of circulating WBCs), monocytes, and lymphocytes (usually 8 to 33 percent of circulating WBCs). Eosinophils normally comprise <5 percent of WBCs.

In addition to cell counts, many of the automated cell counters routinely calculate and display an absolute value for each type of WBC. Other laboratories require calculation by the clinician.

A normal ANC is usually between 1500 neutrophils/microL (1.5 x 109 cells) and 7700 neutrophils/microL (7.7 x 109/L), a normal ALC is usually between 1000 lymphocytes/microL (1.0 x 109/L) and 4000 lymphocytes/microL (4.0 x 109/L), a normal AEC is usually <500 eosinophils/microL (0.5 x 109/L), and a normal AMC is usually between 200 monocytes/microL (0.2 x 109 cells) and 800 monocytes/microL (0.8 x 109/L). Normal values may vary in different laboratories.
