ALGORITHM — 

Neutropenia is usually defined as an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) below 1500 cells/microL (<1.5 x 109 cells/L) (calculator 1). Neutropenia may be encountered in the course of evaluating other clinical findings or as an incidental abnormality on a complete blood count (CBC) and differential. Neutropenia may be the sole abnormality in the CBC and blood smear, or it may be accompanied by other white blood cell (WBC) abnormalities (eg, lymphocytes, monocytes) or abnormalities in other cell lines (eg, red blood cells and platelets) (algorithm 1).

IMMEDIATE ACTION — 

The urgency of the evaluation is guided by whether the degree and trajectory of neutropenia increases the patient's risk of overwhelming infection (table 1), the acuity of illness, whether a diagnosis has previously been established, and whether there are other abnormalities in the CBC and peripheral blood smear.

All patients, regardless of the level of ANC, with findings of sepsis, hemodynamic instability, respiratory compromise, or other clinical emergencies require immediate hospitalization and urgent evaluation (table 2). Life-saving interventions and specialty consultation should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing in patients with profound neutropenia.

Identify and address worrisome clinical findings that require urgent attention, including:

●Fever, acute infection, or suspected sepsis with any degree of neutropenia (see "Overview of neutropenic fever syndromes" and "Diagnostic approach to the adult cancer patient with neutropenic fever")

●Unexplained ANC <500 cells/microL (<0.5 x 109 cells/L) (see "Approach to the adult with unexplained neutropenia", section on 'Urgency of evaluation')

●Suspected idiosyncratic drug-induced agranulocytosis (table 3) (see "Drug-induced neutropenia and agranulocytosis", section on 'Clinical presentation')

INITIAL EVALUATION — 

In patients who do not require immediate hospitalization, the tempo of the initial evaluation and the need for specialty consultation depend on the clinical context, the degree and trajectory of neutropenia, the likely etiology (table 4), and the risk of an active infection (table 1). (See "Approach to the adult with unexplained neutropenia", section on 'Initial evaluation' and "Infectious causes of neutropenia".)

Repeat complete blood count — To confirm true neutropenia, repeat CBC with differential and platelet count and review peripheral blood smear report. (See "Approach to the adult with unexplained neutropenia", section on 'Peripheral blood smear'.)

Transient neutropenia — Patients with normal neutrophil counts on subsequent testing likely had transient bone marrow suppression (eg, from a viral infection) that may not recur, or the initial finding was a laboratory artifact. No further evaluation is likely to be required unless the neutropenia recurs or there are other worrisome clinical findings.

Persistent neutropenia — In patients with persistent unexplained neutropenia, review:

●Prior CBCs, if available, to assess neutropenia duration, severity, and trajectory (improving, worsening, or stable) and identify other cytopenias (eg, anemia, thrombocytopenia).

●Infection history – Isolated or predominant neutropenia may occur during or following bacterial, parasitic, rickettsial, and viral infections, including hepatitis and HIV infection. (See "Infectious causes of neutropenia".)

●Medications and history for possible drug-induced neutropenia (table 3). (See "Drug-induced neutropenia and agranulocytosis", section on 'Treatment'.)

Examine the patient for signs of an underlying disorder and for signs of current infection. Sites most likely to be infected include the skin, catheter sites, teeth, oropharynx and gingival surfaces, sinuses, lungs, abdomen, genitals, and perianal area.

Obtain:

●Repeat CBC with differential and platelet count – For asymptomatic patients with ANC ≥1000 cells/microL and no worrisome findings on blood smear, repeat within two to six weeks, followed by outpatient evaluation if neutropenia persists. Repeat within one to two weeks in asymptomatic patients if ANC is in the range of 500 to 999/microL. An unexplained ANC <500 cells/microL or worrisome findings on blood smear is a medical emergency. (See 'Immediate action' above.)

If not recently measured, obtain:

●Liver biochemical tests (serum alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase)

●Chemistries (glucose, blood urea nitrogen [BUN], creatinine, serum electrolytes)

Specialty consultation may be appropriate in selected patients. (See 'Indications for specialty consultation' below.)

Persistent neutropenia with other cytopenias — Persistent neutropenia accompanied by anemia and/or thrombocytopenia suggests any of the following:

●Nutritional deficiency (eg, vitamin B12, folate, protein/calorie deficiency).

●Hypersplenism.

●Primary bone marrow disorder (eg, myelodysplastic syndrome, acute leukemia, aplastic anemia) – If suspected (eg, blood smear reveals granulocytes with Pelger-Huet anomaly or reduced cytoplasmic granularity, immature forms), further evaluation and hematologic consultation are necessary. (See "Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and classification of myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)", section on 'Complete blood count' and "Acute myeloid leukemia: Clinical manifestations, pathologic features, and diagnosis", section on 'Peripheral blood'.)

●Chronic inflammatory state (eg, collagen/vascular disorders or other chronic inflammatory conditions).

●Autoimmune disorder associated with autoimmune destruction of multiple cell lines.

Obtain:

●Vitamin B12 and folate levels.

●HIV screening.

●Antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-DNA antibody, complement levels, and urinalysis (if suspicion for systemic autoimmune disease [eg, presence of arthritis, serositis]).

●C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (if suspicion for chronic inflammatory disorder).

Subsequent testing is directed at identifying a cause and excluding active infection.

Persistent isolated neutropenia — Common causes of neutropenia in asymptomatic outpatients include (table 4):

●Drug-induced neutropenia, typically occurring within three months of starting a new drug (table 3):

•Consider switching to a suitable alternative, or discontinuing if downward trend in ANC

•Monitor ANCs

●Duffy-null associated neutrophil count (DANC, formerly benign ethnic neutropenia), typically with ANCs 800 to 1200 cells/microL (0.8 to 1.2 x 109 cells/L) but can be lower:

•This is a normal neutrophil count for patients who are Duffy-negative, and this should not be referred to as "neutropenia." These individuals do not have an increased risk of infection and require no further evaluation. Duffy-negative blood typing (red blood cells Fya-, Fyb-) can be confirmed in the Blood Bank.

●During or following bacterial, parasitic, rickettsial, and viral infections:

•Obtain in patients with risk factors and/or abnormal liver biochemical tests: hepatitis serologies, HIV screening

●Autoimmune neutropenia, typically mild to moderate in severity:

•Obtain in patients with suspected autoimmune disease: ANA, anti-DNA antibody, complement levels, and urinalysis

Although myelodysplastic syndromes and hematologic malignancies typically cause pancytopenia, a minority of cases present as isolated neutropenia. (See "Approach to the adult with unexplained neutropenia", section on 'Causes of neutropenia'.)

Indications for specialty consultation — Hematologic consultation is appropriate in patients with findings suggestive of diminished bone marrow function or hematologic malignancy, as follows:

●Moderate (ANC <1000 cells/microL [<1.0 x 109 cells/L]) to severe neutropenia (ANC <500 cells/microL [<0.5 x 109 cells/L]) without a clear etiology.

●Worsening of neutropenia or persistence of ANC <1000 cells/microL (<1.0 x 109 cells/L) that is not DANC or due to a nutritional deficiency, a rheumatologic disorder, or hypersplenism (eg, due to liver cirrhosis, viral illness).

●Worrisome findings on peripheral blood smear that suggest acute leukemia or other hematologic malignancies (eg, blasts or other premature forms, teardrop cells).

●Development of unexplained cytopenia.

●Unexplained splenomegaly and/or lymphadenopathy that may be due to a malignancy.

For those with fever in whom the source of infection remains unclear, infectious disease consultation and culture of the bone marrow aspirate, including long-term culture for mycobacterial organisms, may be useful.

REFERENCE RANGE — 

The ANC is used to quantitate neutrophils in peripheral blood (calculator 1).

ANC (cells/microL) = WBC (cells/microL) x percent neutrophils ÷ 100

Neutropenia is usually defined as an ANC <1500 cells/microL (<1.5 x 109 cells/L); however, the World Health Organization defines neutropenia as an ANC ≤1800 cells/microL (≤1.8 x 109 cells/L). Interpretation of a specific abnormal test result should be based upon the age-adjusted reference range reported by the laboratory.
