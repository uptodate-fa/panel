ALGORITHM  — 

Neutrophilia (high neutrophil count) may be encountered in the course of evaluating acute or chronic illness or as an incidental abnormality on a complete blood count (CBC) and differential. Neutrophilia may be the sole abnormality in the CBC and blood smear, or it may be accompanied by other white blood cell (WBC) abnormalities (eg, lymphocytosis) or abnormalities of other cell lines (eg, red blood cells and platelets) ( algorithm 1 ).

IMMEDIATE ACTION  — 

The urgency of the evaluation is guided by the patient's clinical condition, the degree and rate of rise of neutrophils (if known), and worrisome findings (eg, blasts) on peripheral smear.

Identify and address worrisome clinical features that require urgent attention and hospitalization. As examples:

● Hemodynamic instability, high fever, hypothermia, respiratory compromise, or complications of infection.

● WBC abnormalities suggesting severe infection or sepsis (band forms, metamyelocytes, myelocytes, toxic granulations, or Döhle bodies ( picture 1 )).

● Hematologic abnormalities suggesting hematologic malignancy (total WBC >100,000/microL [100 x 10 9 /L], blasts, immature WBC forms, nucleated red blood cells and/or schistocytes ( picture 2 )). (See "Acute myeloid leukemia in adults: Overview", section on 'Complications/emergencies' and "Approach to the patient with neutrophilia", section on 'Leukemoid reaction/hyperleukocytosis' and "Evaluation of the peripheral blood smear", section on 'Blasts or tumor cells' .)

Life-saving interventions, including fluid resuscitation and empiric antibiotics in patients with sepsis, should not be delayed while awaiting the results of diagnostic testing. Additional studies and specialty consultation will depend on the clinical setting and likely diagnosis. (See "Sepsis syndromes in adults: Epidemiology, definitions, clinical presentation, diagnosis, and prognosis", section on 'Definitions' and "Evaluation and management of suspected sepsis and septic shock in adults", section on 'Immediate evaluation and management' .)

INITIAL EVALUATION  — 

Patients not requiring immediate action require a timely evaluation to determine the clinical significance of an elevated WBC count and the tempo of the subsequent evaluation (see "Approach to the patient with neutrophilia", section on 'Clinical evaluation' ):

● Review the CBC with differential and platelet count for:

• Abnormalities of other cell lines (eg, anemia, thrombocytopenia)

• Presence of immature cells

● Review medical history for evidence of conditions that can account for neutrophilia ( table 1 ). As examples:

• Reactive disorders – infection; chronic inflammation due to rheumatic disease, inflammatory bowel disease, or chronic hepatitis; postsurgery; postsplenectomy, or sickle cell disease

• Malignant disorders – acute or chronic leukemia, myeloproliferative disorders, solid tumors

● Review medications ( table 1 ) (see "Approach to the patient with neutrophilia", section on 'Medications' )

● Obtain prior CBCs and findings on peripheral blood smear, if available, to assess duration and trajectory of neutrophilia:

• Acute neutrophilia (hours to days) is more likely due to infection, stress reactions, surgery, or acute leukemia.

• Chronic neutrophilia (lasting weeks to months) is more likely due to chronic inflammation, obesity, smoking, or some malignancies.

Seemingly well  —  In a seemingly well individual, repeat the CBC with differential and platelet count within two weeks before initiating a more extensive evaluation (see "Approach to the patient with neutrophilia" ):

● Transient neutrophilia – Transient neutrophilia without symptoms of infection is most likely caused by a reactive process (eg, exercise, physical or emotional stress, pregnancy, obesity). It requires no further evaluation unless neutrophilia recurs or worrisome clinical findings emerge.

● Persistent neutrophilia – In patients with persistent neutrophilia, the diagnosis is often apparent at the time of clinical assessment. Unexplained persistent neutrophilia warrants further evaluation ( table 1 ). Persistent neutrophilia may be spurious, primary (eg, hereditary neutrophilia, myeloproliferative disorders), or secondary (eg, infection, stress, or drugs). The evaluation and management depends in part on the degree of neutrophilia and the clinical setting. As an example, a patient with an unexplained WBC count ≥25,000 should be followed frequently and evaluated for deep chronic infection or other chronic inflammation, whereas lower levels of chronic neutrophilia might be followed with serial blood counts, unless worrisome clinical findings emerge.

Suspected underlying process  —  Suspect an underlying process in patients who have worrisome clinical findings (eg, worsening or moderately high WBC count >20,000/microL [20 x 10 9 /L], abnormalities of other cell lines, and/or systemic symptoms [fever, night sweats, or unintentional weight loss] or signs [organomegaly or lymphadenopathy]).

The tempo of the evaluation depends on the degree and trajectory (improving, worsening, or stable) of the leukocytosis, findings on peripheral smear, likely etiology, and risk and/or presence of an active infection. WBC >50,000/microL constitutes a leukemoid reaction to a serious underlying condition (eg, infection, medication, asplenia, nonhematologic malignancy).

In patients with a suspected underlying process:

• Review tests often available at the time of presentation that may identify associated medical conditions or complications ( table 1 ):

- Routine chemistries (electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen [BUN], creatinine, glucose)

- Liver biochemical tests (eg, alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase

- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP)

• Obtain urinalysis with sediment

• Obtain serial CBC with differential and platelet count

● Exclude infection – Infections are the most common cause of neutrophilia in both the inpatient and outpatient settings. Neutrophilia with a left shift (increased in the percentage of band forms, generally accompanied by metamyelocytes and myelocytes) is commonly associated with acute bacterial infections, whereas neutrophilia with "atypical" lymphocytes is commonly associated with viral infections ( picture 3 ). Neutrophilia with monocytosis may occur in association with infections, including brucellosis, varicella-zoster, bacterial endocarditis, tuberculosis, malaria, typhoid fever, syphilis, and trypanosomiasis. Unexplained neutrophilia, even without diarrhea, may be a harbinger of Clostridioides difficile infection and usually occurs after 5 to 10 days of antibiotic treatment. (See "Approach to the patient with neutrophilia", section on 'Other leukocyte abnormalities' .)

• Cultures of blood, urine, and joint or body fluids and imaging targeted at the suspected source, as appropriate

Additional studies and specialty consultation will depend on the findings.

● Assess for other causes – In patients in whom infection has been excluded, assess for other common reactive causes, including acute and chronic inflammation, physical and emotional stress, medications, and/or asplenia ( table 1 ). Although less common than reactive causes, neutrophilia may be seen in any of the myeloproliferative neoplasms (eg, chronic myeloid leukemia [CML], polycythemia vera, and essential thrombocythemia) or in solid tumors.

Underlying malignancy should be suspected in patients with any of the following unexplained clinical features:

• Persistently high neutrophil counts (eg, WBC counts >50,000/microL [50 x 10 9 /L]), worrisome WBC differential (eg, marked left shift, blasts, eosinophilia, basophilia, dysplasia), or abnormalities of other cell lines (nucleated red blood cells, abnormal red blood cell and/or platelet counts).

• Organomegaly, lymphadenopathy.

• Systemic symptoms (eg, unintentional weight loss, night sweats, fever, and fatigue).

Obtain hematologic consultation to guide further testing (eg, flow cytometry, cytogenetic testing, or molecular testing of the bone marrow or peripheral blood) if hematologic malignancy is suspected.

REFERENCE RANGE  — 

The total WBC count in healthy adults normally varies from 4000 to 11,000 cells/microL. Neutrophils can be expressed as a percentage in the WBC differential count (usually 40 to 70 percent of circulating WBCs are mature neutrophils) or as an absolute number in the peripheral blood (upper limit of normal in most clinical laboratories is approximately 7700 neutrophils/microL [WBCs/microL x 70 percent]). Interpretation of a specific abnormal result should be based upon the age-adjusted reference range reported by the laboratory.
